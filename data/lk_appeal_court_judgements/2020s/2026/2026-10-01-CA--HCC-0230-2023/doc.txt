Judgement

1. This appeal arises from the judgment dated 25.08.2023, delivered by the learned High

Court Judge of Colombo. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as “the

Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Colombo on the following counts;

a. On or about 15.12.2016, within the jurisdiction of the High Court of Colombo,

the Accused, being a public servant, solicited a sum of Rs. 25,000/- as a

gratification from the complainant as an inducement to issue Environmental

Protection Licenses and a Certification of Conformity and thereby committed

an offence punishable under Section 19(b) of the Bribery Act No. 11 of 1954 as

amended by Acts No. 02 1965, No. 38 of 1974, and No. 09 of 1980;

b. During the same time, place, and in the course of the same transaction as

mentioned in the first count, being a public servant, the Accused, by soliciting

the said sum of money, committed an offence punishable under Section 19(c)

of the Bribery Act No. 11 of 1954 as amended by Acts No. 02 of 1965, No. 38

of 1974, and No. 09 of 1980;

c. On or about 20.12.2016, within the jurisdiction of the High Court of Colombo,

the Accused, being a government servant, solicited a sum of Rs. 25,000/- as a

gratification from the complainant as an inducement to issue Environmental

Protection Licenses and a Certification of Conformity, thereby committed an

offence punishable under Section 19(b) of the Bribery Act No. 11 of 1954 as

amended by Acts No. 02 1965, No. 38 of 1974, and No. 09 of 1980;

d. During the same time, place, and in the course of the same transaction as

mentioned in the third count, being a public servant, the Accused, by soliciting

the said sum of money, committed an offence punishable under Section 19(c)

of the Bribery Act No. 11 of 1954 as amended by Acts No. 02 of 1965, No. 38

of 1974, and No. 09 of 1980.

2. At the trial, six witnesses testified for the prosecution. Subsequently, the Appellant

made a dock statement.

3. At the conclusion of the trial, the learned Trial Judge found the Appellant guilty of four

counts. The Appellant was accordingly convicted and imposed 5 years’ rigorous

imprisonment for each count to run concurrently and a fine of Rs. 5000/- for each count,

in default, six months’ simple imprisonment. The Appellant was further ordered to pay

Rs. 25,000/- as a fine under Section 26 of the Bribery Act.

4. Aggrieved by the said conviction and sentence, the Appellant has preferred the present

appeal on the following grounds;

a. The learned High Court Judge has failed to give due consideration to the

material infirmities in the prosecution evidence relating to the first and second

counts;

b. The learned High Court Judge has failed to accord due weight to the dock

statement of the Appellant;

c. The impugned judgment of the High Court Judge is contrary to the evidence led

at the trial;

d. The learned High Court Judge failed to conclude that the prosecution had not

proved the case beyond reasonable doubt.

5. I shall now consider the first, third, and fourth grounds of appeal, as the Appellant

challenges the strength of the prosecution’s evidence. In doing so, I will examine

whether the learned Trial Judge failed to give due consideration to the infirmities in the

prosecution’s evidence and failed to analyze the prosecution’s evidence in terms of the

test of credibility and the test of probability, as contended by the Appellant.

6. At the trial before the High Court, the prosecution primarily relied on the evidence of

PW1, Anura Priyankara Perera, the complainant. His evidence may be briefly

summarized as follows;

7. At the time of the alleged offence, the Appellant was serving as a Technical Officer

attached to Payagala sub-office of Beruwela Pradeshiya Sabha. Prior to the alleged

incident, PW1 and the Appellant were known to each other. According to the evidence

of PW1, he was a businessman who owned Shakthi Saw Mill, Water Nest Hotel, and

Shakthi Hardware at the relevant time. PW1 had submitted an application dated

24.09.2016 for extension of the Environmental Protection License (EPL) in respect of

Shakthi Saw Mill and an application dated 02.09.2016 for the issuance of a Certificate

of Conformity (COC) in respect of the swimming pool at Water Nest Hotel.

8. According to PW1, the said EPL and COC were required to be obtained from Beruwala

Pradeshiya Sabha. Following the submission of the relevant applications, the Appellant,

in his capacity as the Technical Officer, visited the premises where Shakthi Saw Mill

and Water Nest Hotel were situated on 09.12.2016. PW1 was not present at the premises

at the time of the Appellant’s visit and, upon being informed of the visit, contacted the

Appellant by telephone. PW1 requested the Appellant to proceed with the site

inspection and informed him that he would return to the office on the following day.

The Appellant, however, requested PW1 to meet him on 14.09.2016.

9. Accordingly, PW1 went to Payagala Sub-Office on 14.06.2016 and met the Appellant.

According to PW1, on that occasion, the Appellant informed him that a substantial sum

of money would be required in order to facilitate the approval of the aforesaid

applications. It is significant, however, that, according to PW1’s own evidence, the

Appellant did not, at that stage, demand or solicit any specific sum of money from him.

on 19.12.2016.

13. The evidence further reveals that, on 19.12.2016, officers of the Bribery Commission

proceeded to the Hotel Water Nest belonging to PW1 and met with him there. PW1 then

18. In particular, PW1 contradicted his own evidence with regard to the amount allegedly

Appellant had solicited a sum of Rs. 45,000/- during the said telephone conversation,

20. It should be noted that the evidence suggests the existence of a strained relationship

between PW1 and the Appellant. Having regard to the prior interactions between them,

including the circumstances surrounding the recommendation of a fine to PW1, such

circumstances cannot be disregarded in assessing the evidence as a whole. They

constitute relevant circumstances to be taken into consideration in evaluating the

credibility and reliability of PW1’s testimony.

21. These circumstances, when considered together with the material inconsistencies in

PW1’s evidence, raise questions as to the probability of the prosecution version and

cast doubt upon the accounts of events given by PW1.

22. Accordingly, when the evidence of PW1 is considered as a whole, the material

inconsistencies and surrounding circumstances create a reasonable doubt as to the

credibility and reliability of his testimony. In such circumstances, it would be unsafe to

sustain the conviction of the Appellant upon the testimony of PW1, particularly where

his evidence does not furnish a consistent and reliable account of the material events

alleged by the prosecution.

23. The next witness to testify on behalf of the prosecution was PW2, Amarasinghe, an

officer attached to the Bribery Commission, who acted as the decoy in the present case.

24. PW2 testified that, on 19.12.2016, he, together with other officers of the Bribery

Commission, proceeded to investigate the allegation made by PW1. Accordingly, they

went to the Water Nest Hotel, where they met PW1. Thereafter, PW3, the Chief

Investigating Officer, recorded a statement from PW1 concerning the alleged

solicitation of gratification by the Appellant.

25. Upon recording the said statement, PW3 inquired from PW1 whether he was willing to

assist the Commission in its investigation. Upon obtaining his consent, PW3 requested

PW1 to select an officer whom he considered suitable to accompany him when meeting

the Appellant. PW1 accordingly selected PW2 for that purpose.

26. PW3 thereafter instructed PW2 to accompany PW1 and to ascertain from the Appellant

the particulars of the alleged gratification that had been solicited.

27. Pursuant to the aforesaid instructions, PW2 and PW1 proceeded to the sub-office at

Payagala on 19.12.2016. However, they were unable to meet the Appellant, as he was

not present at the office. Consequently, the proposed raid was postponed until the

following day.

28. On 20.12.2016, acting upon the instruction of PW3, PW1 and PW2 again proceeded to

the said office and met the Appellant. After engaging in conversation for some time,

34. Thus, the evidence of PW5 and PW6 independently establishes that PW1 had been

35. In my opinion, the inconsistencies and infirmities apparent in the evidence of PW1 and

PW2 are not confined to minor or peripheral matters, but relate to material aspects of

the prosecution case, including the sequence of events and the circumstances

surrounding the alleged solicitation. These discrepancies, when considered together

with the surrounding circumstances, materially affect the credibility and reliability of

the prosecution evidence.

36. It is also desirable to emphasize that the officers, when conducting a raid of this nature,

are required to make notes on every important event and are also entitled to peruse their

notes in order to refresh their memory when testifying. Hence, where material

contradictions are present in their testimony, the Court cannot lightly disregard such

inconsistencies and proceed to convict the Appellant.

37. Accordingly, I am of the view that the learned High Court Judge failed to give due

consideration to the material infirmities in the prosecution evidence and that the

impugned judgment is not supported by the evidence led at the trial. The prosecution

has therefore failed to establish the charges against the Appellant beyond reasonable

doubt.

41. Unfortunately, the aforesaid infirmities, together with the material inconsistencies in

Court Judge. It is well settled that an appellate court is generally slow to interfere with

the findings of a Trial Judge. Nevertheless, where material discrepancies exist that go

to the root of the prosecution case and render it unsustainable, and where such

discrepancies appear to have been overlooked by the Trial Judge, appellate intervention

is warranted.

42. Furthermore, it is significant to note that the learned High Court Judge rejected the dock

statement of the Appellant on the ground that it was an unsworn statement and that the

prosecution had no opportunity to cross-examine the Appellant. In my view, the fact

that the statement was unsworn and was not subjected to cross-examination does not,

in itself, justify its outright rejection, as these are inherent characteristics of a dock

statement.

43. The principles governing the evidentiary value of a dock statement are well settled in

our jurisprudence. In Gunasiri and two others v. Republic of Sri Lanka 2009 (1) Sri

L.R. 39, it was held with authority as follows:

“If the dock statement is believed it must be acted upon. If it creates a reasonable

doubt the defence must succeed.”

44. To elaborate further, although a dock statement is unsworn and is not subject to cross-

examination, it must nevertheless be treated as evidence. Its probative value must be

assessed by considering whether, and to what extent, the matters stated therein are

consistent with, or contradicted by, the other evidence led at the trial, the surrounding

circumstances, and the inherent probabilities of the case. The trial Judge must consider

the dock statement together with the totality of the evidence and determine what weight,

if any, should properly be attached to it. The mere fact that the statement is unsworn

and was not subjected to cross-examination does not, by itself, justify its rejection

without such evaluation.

45. The above position is further articulated in Ehelepola v. Officer in Charge, Police

Station, Kandy and Another (1998) 1 Sri L.R. 295, by the Supreme Court as follows:

“I have examined the judgment of the learned Magistrate and find that the

Magistrate has indeed stated that the dock statement made by the appellant had no

evidentiary value. On this matter the learned Magistrate has clearly misdirected

himself. It is indeed well settled law that when an unsworn statement is made by an

accused from the dock, that such statement must be looked upon as evidence subject

however to the infirmity that the accused had deliberately refrained from giving

sworn testimony. Though there is no statutory provision for it, the right of an

accused to make an unsworn statement from the dock has been recognized by our

Courts for many years. (See The King v. Sittamparam 20 NLR 257 and is now part

of the established procedure in our Criminal Courts. If such statement is believed,

it must be acted upon, if it raises a reasonable doubt in their minds about the case

for the prosecution, the defence must succeed. However, it should not be used

against another accused. (Vide The Queen v. Kularatne 71 NLR 529. In the instant

case, the learned Magistrate had admittedly given no consideration whatsoever to

the unsworn statement made by the appellant from the dock and has specifically

stated that such statement had no evidentiary value. In The Queen v. Mapitigama

Buddharakhita Them and two others 63 NLR 433, the Court of Criminal Appeal has

observed that the right of an accused person to make an unsworn statement from

the dock is recognized in our law. That right would be of no value, unless such a

statement is treated as evidence on behalf of the accused, subject however to the

infirmity it attaches to statements that are unsworn and have not been tested by

cross-examination.”

46. Thus, it is incumbent upon the Trial Judge to analyze and evaluate the dock statement

and give reasons for declining to accept it. Failure to do so would result in the defence

evidence remaining unevaluated and unassessed, thereby denying the appellant a fair

trial. In the present case, regrettably, the learned Trial Judge failed to analyze and

evaluate the probative value of the Appellant’s dock statement. This failure, in my view,

caused grave prejudice to the Appellant, as the defence evidence was thereby left

unevaluated and unassessed.

47. In the aforesaid circumstances, I conclude that it is not safe to affirm the conviction

against the Appellant.

conviction and sentence imposed on the Appellant by the learned High Court Judge.
