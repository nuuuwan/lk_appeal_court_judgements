JUDGMENT

M.C.B.S. Morais J.

This is an appeal by way of case stated against the determination of the Tax Appeals

Commission (TAC) dated 7th of April 2022, in the case of TAC/IT/(PAYE)058/2017.

This appeal is filed under Section 11A(1) of the Tax Appeals Commission Act No. 23 of

2011 (as amended).

The tax in dispute is against the assessment made for the taxable period 2011/2012 and the

disputed income in issue is Rs. 634,686,195.00 with a penalty of Rs.317,343,097.00.

FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The Access Engineering PLC (hereinafter will be referred to as the Appellant) is a Private

limited liability company incorporated and domiciled in Sri Lanka. The principal business

activity of the Appellant is engaging in the construction activities.

The Appellant has submitted the return for the taxable period 2011/2012, which were

rejected by the Assistant Commissioner of Inland Revenue by the intimation letter dated 30th

of December 2014 and assessed the PAYE tax liability of the Appellant and issued an

assessment. Accordingly, the Assessor has issued the Notice of Assessment dated 27th of

March 2015 for the reasons set out in the intimation letter.

The Appellant being aggrieved by the decision of the Assessor, made an appeal to the

Commissioner General of Inland Revenue (hereinafter will be referred to as the Respondent).

The Respondent in his determination dated 20th of March 2017 determined the appeal by

upholding the assessment issued by the Assistant Commissioner. The Appellant being

dissatisfied with the said determination of the Respondent, made an appeal to the Tax

Appeals Commission (TAC). The TAC after considering the submissions made by both

parties made the determination dated 7th of April 2022 in favor of the Respondent.

Being dissatisfied with the said determination, the Appellant has requested the TAC to cause

a Case Stated to the Court of Appeal on the following Questions of Law.

I. “Did the Tax Appeals Commission err in law when it concluded that the Appellant

was liable to deduct PAYE tax from the remuneration of its employees on the

premise that shares gifted by the Appellant's directors, out of their own personal

shareholding in the company, constituted a profit from employment received from

the Appellant?

Page 4 of 18

II. Did the Commissioner General of Inland Revenue err in law when valuing the

shares received by its employees, gifted out of the personal shareholdings if its

directors, when determining the Appellant's PAYE Tax liability?

III. Is section 114 of the Inland Revenue Act, No. 10 of 2006 (as amended), applicable

to profits or benefits from employment not received or derived from the employer?

IV. Did the Respondent err in law when he concluded that the Assessor/Assistant

Commissioner/Deputy Commissioner was entitled to make an assessment under

section 125 of the Inland Revenue Act, No. 10 of 2006 (as amended), in

circumstances where no taxable remuneration was paid by the employer to its

employees?

V. Did the Respondent err in law when he conclude that the Appellant was obliged to

deduct PAYE tax in respect of 38 persons who were not employees of the

Appellant?

VI. Did the Respondent err in law when he decided to increase the assessment and

thereby widen the scope of the assessment?

VII. In view of the facts and circumstances of the case did the Tax Appeals

Commission err in law in arriving at the conclusion that it did?”

The substantive Questions of Law No. I, II, and V are in relation to the applicability of PAYE

(Pay-as-you-Earn) tax from Appellant’s employees. Therefore, I shall take all three questions

together and address them.

The Question of Law No. III is in relation to the applicability of Section 114 of the Inland

Revenue Act No 10. of 2006 as amended (IRA 2006) and the Question of Law No. IV is in

relation to the power of Assessor to make an assessment under section 125 of the IRA 2006.

Since these two questions are based upon the substantive issues of the case, I shall discuss

them together.

Questions of Law No. I, II and IV

It is the contention of the Appellant that the PAYE tax for the taxable periods 2011/2012 was

duly paid. However, in the Notice of Assessment dated 27th of March 2015 and received on

3rd of March 2015, the Assessor has made a deduction in respect of PAYE tax and imposed a

penalty for late payment. The Appellant therefore contends that there was no outstanding

PAYE liability and, accordingly, no further tax was payable.

Before considering the respective contentions of the parties, it is necessary to understand the

nature of the transaction in question.

Page 6 of 18

The Board of Directors of the Appellant company transferred certain shares owned by the

shareholders of the Appellant to certain individuals, whom the Appellant claims were not

employees of the company.

It is therefore the position of the Appellant that the transfer was not made by the Appellant

and, accordingly, does not give rise to any liability for PAYE tax. The Appellant further

contends that it is required by law to deduct PAYE tax only where remuneration is provided

by the Appellant.

On the other hand, the Respondent contends that the shares were transferred among the

family members of the main shareholders and to employees of the companies within the

Access Group. According to the Respondent, the three main shareholders, who held 100% of

the shares in the company, transferred 114,948,560 shares to 920 employees and a further

5,051,440 shares to employees of other companies within the Access Group. Accordingly, the

Assessor concluded that a total of 118,000,000 shares transferred to employees had not been

declared in the Annual Declaration of Employer PAYE for the year of assessment 2011/2012,

notwithstanding that the share transfer applications had been submitted to the Inland Revenue

Department.

The Respondent therefore contends that the transaction falls squarely within the scope of

sections 4(1)(a)(ii) and 4(1)(e) of the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006 and is accordingly

subject to PAYE tax.

The principal question before this Court is whether the transfer of shares by the Directors of

the Appellant to its employees, by way of gift, is subject to PAYE tax under the Inland

Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006.

Before considering this question, I find it appropriate to briefly examine the nature of PAYE

tax and the manner in which it is imposed.

Page 7 of 18

The Pay-As-You-Earn (PAYE) system was introduced in Sri Lanka in 1971 as a mechanism

for collecting income tax on employment income at the time remuneration was paid. Under

the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006, PAYE (Pay-As-You-Earn) was provided for under

section 114, which required an employer to deduct income tax from an employee's

employment income at the time the remuneration was paid. The provision was designed to

facilitate the collection of income tax at source, rather than requiring the employee to pay the

full liability at the end of the year. The amount to be deducted was determined in accordance

with tax tables specified by the Commissioner General of Inland Revenue, taking into

account the employee's remuneration and the applicable income-tax rates and reliefs. Section

114 was subsequently amended during the period in which the 2006 Act remained in force,

and the Act itself was amended by, among others, the Inland Revenue (Amendment) Acts

Nos. 10 of 2007, 9 of 2008 and 19 of 2009.

For reference, I shall reproduce the section 114 of the IRA 2006 below;

“114.

(1) Every employer shall deduct income tax in accordance with the

provisions of this Chapter from the remuneration of his employees for each

pay period at the time of payment of such remuneration.

(2) Income tax deducted under subsection (1) from the remuneration of an

employee and remitted to the Commissioner General as provided in this

Chapter, shall be deemed to have been paid by such employee to the

Commissioner-General on the date on which such deduction was made.

For the purpose of this section, any person who receives remuneration in cash or kind

from an employer is deemed to be an employee of such employer.”[emphasis added]

Accordingly to the above provision, an employer is legally required to deduct the applicable

income tax from an employee’s remuneration each time the remuneration is paid, in cash or

in other form. The tax deducted by the employer is then remitted to the Commissioner-

Page 8 of 18

General, and once deducted, it is legally treated as tax paid by the employee on the date of

deduction. The section also adopts a broad definition of “employee”, covering any person

who receives remuneration, whether in cash or in any kind from an employer. Thus, the

provision ensures that the employee’s income-tax liability is collected through the employer,

rather than being paid separately by the employee after receiving the remuneration.

Accordingly, it is the contention of the Respondent that the transfer of shares by the Directors

of the Appellant falls within the scope of sections 4(1)(a)(ii) and 4(1)(e) of the Inland

Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006. The Respondent further contends that the transfer cannot be

regarded as a gift, as claimed by the Appellant, but constitutes a benefit provided to the

employees of the Appellant in consideration of the services rendered by them to the

Company. Accordingly, the Respondent's position is that the shares transferred by the

Directors to the employees constitute a benefit or commission within the meaning of the

Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006 and, consequently, form part of the employees' taxable

remuneration and are subject to PAYE tax.

The section 4 of the IRA 2006 elaborates on Profits from employment and section 4(1)(e)

discuses about the transfer of shares extensively in the following manner;

“4. (1) Profits from any employment include—

(a) (ii) the value of any benefits to the employee or to his spouse, child or parent,

including the value of any holiday warrant or passage;”

(e) the value “at the time of its disposal, where such disposal takes place prior to April

1, 2007, of any share of a company received as a benefit from the employer or on behalf

of the employer at no cost or at a price which is less than the prevailing market value of

such share of that company, whether directly or through a share option scheme. The

value at the time of its disposal of such share shall be the surplus over the cost of

acquisition of such share—

Page 9 of 18

(i) in the case of a sale, the sale price or the market value of such share as at the

date of sale, whichever is higher;

(ii) in the case of disposal otherwise than by way of sale, the market value of such

share as at the date of disposal;

(iii) in the case of an employee ceasing to be in the employment of such employer

without selling or disposing of such share, the market value as at the last date of

his employment with such employer, which date shall be deemed to be the date of

the disposal of such share:

Provided however, in the event of the death of such employee during his period of

employment with such employer, the value of such share shall be zero.”

Section 4 of the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006 identifies the types of receipts which

constitute profits from employment for income-tax purposes. Section 4(1)(a)(ii) adopts a

broad approach by including the value of any benefit provided to an employee by reason of

his employment. In addition, section 4(1)(e) specifically addresses the treatment of shares

provided to an employee as a benefit by the employer or on behalf of the employer. Where an

employee receives shares at no cost or at a price below their prevailing market value, the

provision treats the benefit arising from those shares as employment income.

Accordingly, having regard to the above provision, the transfer of shares to an employee may

constitute a benefit arising from employment where the transfer is made in consideration of,

or in connection with, the employee's services to the company and falls within the scope of

section 4(1)(e) of the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006.

The next question that arises for determination is therefore whether the transfer of the shares

in the present case constituted a gift simpliciter, as contended by the Appellant, or whether it

was provided as an incentive, reward or benefit in recognition of the services rendered by the

employees to the Company.

Page 10 of 18

Although the Appellant has consistently maintained that the said transfer of shares did not

constitute remuneration or an employment-related benefit, but was instead a gift, the nature

of the transaction must be determined having regard to the circumstances surrounding the

transfer and the intention of the parties. In this regard, it is significant to consider the letter

dated 15th of June 2011, which was signed by all three shareholders of the Company, namely,

Mr. Sumal Perera, Mr. Ranjan Gomez and Mr. Christopher Joshua.

The contents of the said letter are material in determining the purpose for which the shares

were transferred. The letter expressly states that the shares were being transferred in

appreciation of the employees' commitment, hard work and contribution to the Company.

Page 11 of 18

The above squared portion of the letter clearly express the said transaction is linked to the

employees' commitment, efforts and contributions to the Company. This contemporaneous

documentary evidence consequently supports the inference that the transfer of shares was

made in recognition of the services rendered by the employees and therefore it can be

considered as an employment-related benefit. Accordingly, notwithstanding the Appellant's

position that the said transfer is a transaction of gift, the substance and purpose of the

transfer, indicates that the shares were provided as a benefit arising from the employees'

employment.

In this regard it is pertinent to understand the observations made by E.Goonerane in ‘Income

Tax in Sri Lanka’ (2ndedn 2009, p.103); where the distinction between a gift and a profit from

employment is discussed.

“A reward for past services does not acquire the character of a gift because it was

granted without a legal obligation. A voluntary benefit given to an employee on

retirement will be profits from employment if the consideration for the benefit is

past service. The difference between periodic payment made for present services and

a single payment for past services is that the first is recurrent and the other non-

recurrent. The occasion or time a payment is made is not conclusive of the nature of

the payment. There is in every gift a close relationship between the donor and donee.

The parties maybe master and servant. The employer-employee relationship maybe

evidence that a benefit given by the employer was remuneration for service and

equally it may be evidence that it was a gift. There is no onus on an employee to prove

that the sum of money or benefit given to him by his employer or some other person

was a gift or non-taxable receipt of some other description. If the claim is made that it

is a non-taxable receipt the onus is on the assessor to prove that the receipt was a

profit from employment. A donor selects the objects of his bounty and if he elects in

favour of an employee the relationship does not change the nature of the transaction.

If the transaction is one that could have been entered into between parties who are

not master and servant the existence of a contract of service is not by itself sufficient

to make the benefit a profit from employment. 66 When the benefit given by the

employer is restricted to persons in his service the benefit comes to the employee

because of the position held by him. The position held by the employee qualifies him

Page 12 of 18

to receive the benefit but does not determine the nature of the benefit as profits or

income. The parties to a transaction may be bound to one another as master and

servant but the transaction may be unconnected with the conditions of service. The

aid given by an employer to an employee out of a fund established by him to relieve

persons in distress is not profits from employment. If such aid is given by an employer

under a scheme which he restricts to his employees the other circumstances must be

examined to determine whether the aid is provided as remuneration or an act of

kindness. The test laid down in several cases to distinguish profits of employment

from a gift is to find out whether the benefit was given in consideration of service

past or present. The test is not decisive. There is no rule that a benefit given to an

employee can be profits of employment only if it is referable to some kind of service.

Profits or income from a source include accruals. When a benefit is attached to an

office it is profit from employment of the holder of the office. And it is not necessary

to find out whether it is referable to some service.” [emphasis added]

The author observes that a reward for past services does not acquire the character of a gift

merely because it was granted without a legal obligation, and that a voluntary benefit

provided to an employee may constitute profits from employment where the benefit is given

in consideration of past or present services.

Therefore, the main aspect that needs to be highlighted upon in the transaction in question is

that the said transfer is made to the employees as a consideration for the past services offered

to the company and not otherwise. This concept has also been considered in the cases of

Sutherland V. Commissioner of Income Tax, (Sri Lanka Tax Cases Vol IV pg.446) and in

Kanagasabapathy V.CGIR (Sri Lanka Tax Cases Vol IV pg.140).

When applying these principles to the present case, the material consideration is not merely

the fact that the recipients were employees of the Appellant, but the purpose and

circumstances in which the shares were transferred. As stated in the letter dated 15th of June

2011, signed by all three shareholders of the Company, which expressly records that the

shares were transferred in appreciation of the employees' commitment, hard work and

contribution to the Company. The contents of the document establish a direct connection

Page 13 of 18

between the transfer of the shares and the services and contribution of the employees to the

Company. The benefit was therefore not shown to have been conferred independently of the

employment relationship, but rather in recognition of the employees' past services and

contribution to the Company.

In these circumstances, I am of the view that the transfer of shares cannot properly be

characterised as a mere gift. The surrounding circumstances and the materials presented

before this court indicate that the shares were transferred as an employment-related benefit

arising from the employees' services.

Now, that I have analyzed the issue at hand in the above manner, it is pertinent to draw

parallels with the transaction at hand. When considering the transaction in issue, it can be

clearly seen that major portion of the transfer was given to the immediate employees of the

Appellant Company and the rest were transferred to the non-employees, who were a part of

the Group. However, when considering the Law in relation to PAYE tax it should be noted

that the Employer who is acting as an agent to collect the PAYE tax from its employees is

required to remit the same to the Department as required by law. Thereby, the employee in

question has to be in the salary scale of the employer for the said procedure to be adopted.

However, in the present matter, shares worth of Rs.5,051,440 have been transferred to the

non- employees of the Appellant Company who formed part of the companies in the Group

structure.

Therefore, I am of the view that the transfer of shares made to the 38 non-employees in the

present matter is considered as a gift since there has been no employer-employee relationship

between the transferor and the transferee. Consequently, this court is of the view that the

Appellant should duly pay PAYE tax on the 920 employees of the Appellant company, since

the transaction does not amount to a gift.

Accordingly, I am of the view that the Tax Appeals Commission did not err in determining

that the transfer of shares by the Directors of the Appellant to its employees did not constitute

a benefit arising from employment and was therefore liable to PAYE tax under section 114 of

the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006. However, the decision of the TAC needs to be

Page 14 of 18

amended in the manner where the Appellant is required to submit the PAYE tax on the 920

employees of the company and not for the 38 non-employees, who were not a part of the

company.

Although the receipt of shares may give rise to a personal income tax liability on the part of

those 38 persons, such liability, if any is their own and does not impose an obligation on the

Appellant to deduct and remit PAYE tax in respect of them.

For the foregoing reasons, I answer the Questions of Law No. I and II in the Negative, and

the Question of Law No. V in the Affirmative.

Questions of Law No. III and IV

III. Is section 114 of the Inland Revenue Act, No. 10 of 2006 (as amended), applicable

to profits or benefits from employment not received or derived from the employer?

The Appellant contends that the transfer of shares to its employees was not made by the

Appellant Company but by its Directors in their individual capacity and, therefore, the

transfer cannot constitute profits from the employment of the recipients or give rise to any

PAYE liability on the part of the Appellant.

This contention has already been considered in determining the Questions of Law Nos. I, II

and V, particularly in relation to the nature of the share transfer and its character as an

employment-related benefit. In view of the findings made in respect of those questions, I do

not consider it necessary to examine this contention at length

Although the shares were transferred directly by the Directors/shareholders, they did so in

their capacity as Directors of the Appellant Company. Accordingly, the fact that the shares

were transferred directly by them does not, by itself, alter the character of the benefit received

Page 15 of 18

by the employees, particularly where the transfer was made in recognition of their services

and contribution to the Company.

Accordingly, for the reasons set out above, I answer the Question of Law No. III in the

Negative.

IV. Did the Respondent err in law when he concluded that the Assessor/Assistant

Commissioner/Deputy Commissioner was entitled to make an assessment under

section 125 of the Inland Revenue Act, No. 10 of 2006 (as amended), in circumstances

where no taxable remuneration was paid by the employer to its employees?

It is the contention of the Appellant that the Assessor is not entitled to invoke section 125 of

the IRA 2006, since the shares were given to the employees as a gift and therefore it is not a

remuneration to be considered for the PAYE tax.

“125. (1) Where an employer who is required under the provisions of this Chapter to

deduct income tax from the remuneration paid to his employees–

(a) fails to deduct the whole or any part of the income tax for any pay

period;

(b) fails remit to the Commissioner-General the whole or any part of the

income tax deducted for any pay period; or

(c) fails to furnish any monthly or annual declaration under section 120,

An Assessor or Assistant Commissioner may at any time during the year of

assessment within which that pay period falls or within three years from the

end of that year of assessment, assess the amount if income tax or the

additional amount of income tax which such employer in the opinion of the

Assessor or Assistant Commissioner should have deducted and paid to the

Commissioner-General for such pay period, and shall, by notice in writing

Page 16 of 18

require such employer to pay such amount forthwith together with such

amount as may be due under section 127. ”[emphasis added]

Section 125 empowers an Assessor or Assistant Commissioner, to assess the amount of

income tax, or the additional amount of income tax, which in his opinion ought to have been

deducted and remitted to the Commissioner-General, when an employer has failed to deduct

the whole or any part of the income tax required to be deducted from the remuneration of its

employees. The provision therefore operates where there has been a failure by an employer

to comply with its obligation to deduct and remit PAYE in respect of taxable remuneration.

In the present matter, it is apparent that the Appellant has failed to declare the said transaction

when disposing their PAYE tax obligation. Therefore, the Assessor in such a circumstance is

empowered by law to assess the tax payable and send notice accordingly.

Furthermore, in determining Questions of Law Nos. I, II and V, I have already considered

whether the transfer of shares constituted a gift or an employment-related benefit and have

concluded that the shares constituted a benefit arising from the employees' employment and

were therefore liable to PAYE tax under the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006.

Consequently, the Appellant's contention that section 125 cannot be invoked because the

shares were gifts, cannot be sustained.

In these circumstances, once the share benefit is found to constitute taxable remuneration and

the employer has failed to deduct and remit the corresponding PAYE, the Assessor is

empowered to act under section 125 of the IRA 2006 and assess the amount of income tax

which in his opinion, ought to have been deducted and remitted in respect of the relevant pay

periods.

Accordingly, I answer Question of Law No. IV in the Negative.
