JUDGMENT

P. Kumararatnam, J.

The above-named Accused-Appellants (hereinafter referred to as the

Appellants) were indicted for committing the murder of Warnakulasuriya

Jemis Fernando on 08/02/1996 which is an offence punishable under

Section 296 of the Penal Code read with Section 32 of the Penal Code.

After a non-jury trial, the Learned High Court Judge has found the

Appellants guilty of the charge and sentenced him to death on 06/12/2017.

Being aggrieved by the aforesaid conviction and sentence, the Appellants

preferred this appeal to this court.

The Learned Counsel for the Appellant informed this court that the

Appellants have given consent to argue this matter in their absence. Also, at

the time of argument the Appellants were connected via the Zoom platform

from prison.

The Appellants had raised the following grounds of appeal:

1. The Learned High Court Judge has relied on the evidence of PW1 when

the evidence had failed the test of credibility and consistency.

2. The Learned High Court Judge had failed to evaluate the evidence of

Appellants and thereby failed to consider whether their evidence is

sufficient to reduce the culpability on the basis of sudden provocation.

Background of the Case

According to PW01 Shantha Fernando, the son of the second marriage of the

deceased, the Appellants are sons of the deceased from his first marriage. All

of them live close to each other and they usually draw water from a common

well. The well was situated in front of the deceased’s house. The 1st Appellant

had also used the same well water. A dispute had arisen with regards to

drawing water from the well between the 1st Appellant and the deceased. Due

to this, on the day prior to the incident, the 1st Appellant had fought with a

girl called Manju who was adopted by the deceased. Due to this incident, the

said Manju was hospitalised.

In the morning of the day of the incident, at around 7.00am, the 1st Accused

had shouted that he was unable to draw water from the well as the deceased

had removed the pulley wheel which facilitated to draw water from the well.

Due to this, a tense situation arose and the 1st Appellant had gone to the

house of the deceased and had shouted to come out. As the inmates of the

house had closed the door, the 1st Appellant had pelted stones at the

deceased’s house which broke the window. At that time, the 2nd Appellant

had also arrived at the scene and the 1st Appellant had broken open a door

and entered the deceased’s house. At that time the deceased was armed with

a spear and had tried to attack the 1st Appellant. The 1st Appellant had

grabbed a spear and attacked the deceased and pelted stones. In the

meantime, the 2nd Appellant had assaulted the deceased with a pole that he

had taken from a nearby house.

The police investigation had revealed that the glass window and a door of the

deceased’s house was broken and the police had also found several stones

inside the house.

According to medical evidence, the injury caused to the head had been fatal

and no stab injury was found on the stomach area of the deceased.

Both Appellants had given evidence from witness box.

According to the 1st Appellant, he admits that he did attack the adopted

daughter of the deceased on the previous day. On the following morning at

about 7.00pm, the wife of the 1st Accused had complained that the pulley

wheel of the well had been removed by the deceased, and as a result, the 1st

Appellant’s family could not draw water from the well. When he went to

inquire about this the deceased had called him inside the house and tried to

attack him with a spear. At that time the 1st Appellant had grabbed the spear

and attacked the deceased. At that the 2nd Appellant had come into the

deceased’s house by breaking a window. Understanding the difficult

situation faced by the 1st Appellant, the 2nd Appellant had then attacked the

deceased after picking up a pole from the vicinity.

The 2nd Appellant also narrated the same as to the circumstances under

which he had assaulted the deceased.

Both in their evidence admitted to assaulting the deceased due to sudden

provocation and in self-defence.

The learned Counsel for the 2nd Appellant had commenced the argument first

in this case.

Argument

The learned Counsel for the Appellant strenuously argued that the initial

statement given by PW1 contained several omissions and contradictions

which certainly would go to the root of the case.

Although PW1 had mentioned in his evidence that the 1st Appellant had

shouted and asked the deceased to come out, he had not mentioned the

same in his statement to the police. PW1 had admitted that when the

Appellants asked from the deceased why he removed the pulley wheel from

the well, the deceased had come armed with a knife. Further, PW1 had

admitted that he had given false evidence at the non-summary inquiry. He

had further admitted that he had not mentioned to the police and in the non-

summary inquiry that the 1st Appellant had threatened him not tell the truth.

Further, he had not mentioned that he was threatened to lie on the ground,

in the police statement nor in the non-summary inquiry. Therefore, the

learned Counsel for the Appellant contended that the evidence given by PW1

is tainted with ambiguities which had escaped the attention of the Learned

High Court Judge.

Upon considering the evidence adduced, it is clear that an unpleasant

incident occurred as a result of the deceased’s act of preventing the

Appellant’s family from obtaining water from the well. Therefore, the learned

Counsel for the 2nd Appellant submits that this is an appropriate case to be

considered under Section 297 of Penal Code on the basis of a sudden

provocation.

At this stage, the learned Counsel for the 1st Appellant submitted to court

that he too agrees to proceed with the argument put forward by the learned

Counsel for the 2nd Appellant.

According to exception 1 of Section 294 of the Penal Code

“Culpable Homicide is not murder if the offender whilst deprived of the

power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death

of the person who gave the provocation, or causes the death of any other

person by mistake or accident”

The explanation under this exception read as that:

“Whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough to prevent the

offence from amounting to murder is a question of fact”.

The learned Counsel for the Appellants seek relief for lesser culpability on

the plea of grave and sudden provocation, which was already suggested to

the prosecution before the commencement of the trial. Further, the defence

had suggested to the prosecution witnesses that a sudden fight had

occurred, which was elicited from the evidence of the prosecution witnesses.

According to Section 105 of the Evidence Ordinance, it is the responsibility

of the Appellant to prove the existence of a general or special exception

during the trial on a balance of probabilities. In the absence, the Learned

trial Judge should direct his mind to the circumstances and the fact that the

Appellant is entitled to have the benefit of the lesser verdict.

In The King v Bellana Vithanage Eddin 41 NLR 345 the court held that:

"In a charge of murder, it is the duty of the judge to put to the jury the

alternative of finding the accused guilty of culpable homicide not

amounting to murder when there is any basis for such a finding in the

evidence on record, although such defence was not raised nor relied upon

by the accused”.

In J. M. Aruna v Attorney General CA/57/2011 the Court held that:

“…by an act of the individual which my, sometimes, not provoke a normal

man. In such a situation if he causes the death of the said individual

especially when he was suffering from the loss of his power of self-control,

he is entitled to claim the benefit of the plea of grave and sudden

provocation. But for him to get the benefit of grave and sudden provocation,

the other individual namely the deceased person must do some kind of act

towards him”.

In the case of Gamini v Attorney General [2011] 1 SLR the court held that:

“Although the accused appellant in his defence did not take the defence of

grave and sudden provocation, the trial judge must consider such a plea

in favour of the accused appellant in it if emanates from the evidence of

the prosecution.”

In the case of Rathnayake Mudiyanselage Karunaratne v. The Attorney

General –CA 181/2009 decided 21 November 2011, it was held that:

“If the plea of grave and sudden provocation is available from the evidence

of the prosecution itself, Court has a duty to consider such a plea even if

the accused did not raise it.”

Further in the case of Palamura Hewage Harishchandra Kumarasinghe

v. Attorney General (CA HCC 0070/2023) it was held:

“Our Courts have considered that to qualify under Exception 4 to Section

294 of the Penal Code, the Accused has to establish that the incident

was committed without premeditation in a sudden fight in the heat of

an argument. C. Ananda Grero, Culpable Homicide, Proof and Defences,

1988 at page 69: "Gour in the Penal Law of India states: "Pre-meditation

may be established by direct or positive Evidence or by circumstantial

evidence. Evidence of pre-meditation can be furnished by former

grudges or previous threats and expressions of ill-feelings; by acts of

preparation to kill, such as procuring a deadly weapon or selecting a

dangerous weapon in preference to one less dangerous, and by the

manner in which the killing was committed. For example, repeated

shots, blows or other acts of violence are sufficient evidence of

meditation.”

In the case of S.C. Appeal No. 168/2018, (decided on 25.7.2025) it was

further examined:

“The most relevant part in relation to the instant appeal of the said

Exception is the section that reads “… in a sudden fight in the heat of

passion upon a sudden quarrel”. The said exception speaks of sudden

fight which results upon a sudden quarrel. These phrases were used by

the Legislature to denote a position that reflects a transformation of a

quarrel to a fight within a very short duration of time. It is important to

note in this context that the word “sudden” appears twice in the text of

8 | P a g e

the said Exception and that too, after the phrase “without

premeditation”. The emphasis laid in the text of the section on the

spontaneity of the act which resulted in the death of a person is

therefore clearly recognisable.

The start of the quarrel and its escalation into a fight must happen

within a short duration of time. The emphasis placed by the Section on

the progressive but sudden escalation of the intensity of the degree of

passion with which the opposing parties acts in a quarrel culminating

with the act or acts that results in the fatality could easily be discerned

from the phrase “… in a sudden fight in the heat of passion upon a

sudden quarrel”.

Analysing the evidence presented in this case, it clearly demonstrates that

the strained relationship with the deceased had resulted in this unfortunate

incident. The Learned High Court Judge had not discussed the concept of

sudden provocation in his judgment. Therefore, the Learned High Court

Judge had not awarded the benefit to the Appellants under the said

mitigatory plea. Further, the evidence given by PW1 is not convincing and it

contains lots of contradictions and omissions.

Considering all the circumstances stressed before this court, I conclude that

this is an appropriate case to consider the Appellant’s entitlement to the

benefit of a plea of grave and sudden provocation under Exception-1 to

Section 294 of the Penal Code.

Hence, I set aside the death sentence and convict the Appellants for culpable

homicide not amounting to murder under Section 297 of the Penal Code. I

sentence the Appellant to 13 years rigorous imprisonment with a fine of

Rs.20,000/- with a default sentence of 01-year simple imprisonment.

Considering all the circumstances, I order the sentence be effective from the

date of conviction namely 06/12/2017.
