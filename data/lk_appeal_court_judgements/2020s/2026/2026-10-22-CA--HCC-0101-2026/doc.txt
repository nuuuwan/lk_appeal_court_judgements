1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentence dated 21.11.2025 delivered by the

learned High Court Judge of Anuradhapura. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter

referred to as “the Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Anuradhapura on

a charge of committing grave sexual abuse against one Kappetiyawe Gamage Saman

Kumara Gamage, an offence punishable under Section 365B(2)(b) of the Penal Code,

as amended by Acts No. 22 of 1995, No. 29 of 1988, and No. 16 of 2006.

2. At the trial before the High Court, the prosecution called four witnesses, while the

Appellant opted to make a dock statement. Upon the conclusion of the trial, the learned

Trial Judge found the Appellant guilty of the charge and convicted him accordingly.

3. Consequently, the Appellant was sentenced to seven years’ rigorous imprisonment and

ordered to pay a fine of Rs. 10,000/-, with a default sentence of three months’ simple

imprisonment. In addition, the learned High Court Judge directed the Appellant to pay

Rs. 250,000/- as compensation to the victim, with a default sentence of eight months’

simple imprisonment.

4. Being aggrieved by the said conviction and sentence, the Appellant has preferred the

instant appeal. The Appellant relied on the following grounds of appeal;

19. However, in evaluating the evidence of the victim, due consideration must be given to

the fact that he was a child at the time of the alleged incident. Accordingly, any

discrepancies or inconsistencies in his testimony must be assessed in the context of his

age and the circumstances in which the alleged incident occurred, having regard to the

nature of the evidence expected from a child witness.

20. In the present matter, it is noteworthy that the victim was fifteen years old at the time

of the alleged incident, and testified before the Trial Court approximately five years

after the incident. Accordingly, in view of his tender age at the time of the incident and

the passage of time before he gave evidence, it is only natural that certain minor

inconsistencies would appear in his testimony. Such inconsistencies do not, in my view,

undermine his overall credibility.

21. The evidence of a child witness must be assessed as a whole, taking into account the

entirety of the evidence adduced at the trial. At the conclusion of the case, the court

must determine whether the child’s evidence, having been tested through cross-

examination, is trustworthy. This assessment depends upon whether the child was able

to narrate the events coherently, communicate effectively, understand the questions

posed, and provide rational and intelligible answers.

22. If the child’s evidence has not undergone any material or substantial change and the

essence of the allegations remains intact, the court may safely accept such evidence as

satisfactory in all material respects after evaluating it alongside the evidence of the

accused. The expression “satisfactory in all material respects” does not require that

every aspect of the testimony be free from discrepancies or inconsistencies. Rather, the

court must consider the evidence in its entirety, bearing in mind that minor

discrepancies are not uncommon, particularly in the testimony of a child witness, and

determine whether the evidence, viewed as a whole, is sufficiently reliable to establish

the guilt of the accused.

23. In Ranjeet Kumar Ram v. State of Bihar [2015] SCC Online SC 500, the court held

that:

“Evidence of the child witness and its credibility would depend upon the

circumstances of each case. Only precaution which the court has to bear in mind

while assessing the evidence of a child witness is that the witness must be a reliable

one.”

24. At this stage, it is essential to understand how the testimony of a child witness should

be looked into and appreciated.

25. In Dattu Sakhare Vs. State of Maharashtra (1997) 5 SCC 341, held that;

“There is no requirement or condition that the evidence of a child witness must be

corroborated before it can be considered, and rather the insistence of any

corroboration is only a rule of prudence that would depend upon the peculiar facts

and circumstances of each case.”

26. In Suryanarayana Vs. State of Karnataka (2001) 9 SCC 129, held that;

“The evidence of the child witness cannot be rejected per se, but the court, as a rule

of prudence, is required to consider such evidence with close scrutiny and only on

being convinced about the quality of the statements and its reliability, base

conviction by accepting the statement of the child witness.”

27. In Rameshwar Vs. State of Rajasthan (1952) AIR 54; the landmark judgment held that

child testimony, much scrutinized, does not require compulsory corroboration in the

absence of grounds for suspecting tutoring or fabrication.

28. In The State of Madhya Pradesh Vs. Balveer Singh (2005) INSC 261, the Supreme

Court of India revisited fundamental principles regarding the testimony of child

witnesses and the application of Section 106 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872

(“Evidence Act”) in cases involving suspicious deaths within the confines of a home.

This decision carries paramount significance as it clarifies the standard for assessing

the credibility of child witnesses, addresses the implications of delayed recording of

such testimony, and underscores the shifting burden of proof under certain factual

scenarios.

“The Trial Court must also record the demeanour of the child witness during the

course of its deposition and cross-examination and whether the evidence of such

child witness is his voluntary expression and not borne out of the influence of

others.”

“There is no requirement or condition that the evidence of a child witness must be

corroborated before it can be considered. A child witness who exhibits the

demeanour of any other competent witness and whose evidence inspires

confidence can be relied upon without any need for corroboration and can form

the sole basis for conviction.”

29. Therefore, in my view, the aforementioned inconsistencies in the testimony of the

victim are not of such a material nature as to affect the core of the prosecution’s case or

render his evidence inherently unreliable. Such discrepancies, when considered in the

context of the evidence as a whole, do not warrant the rejection of his testimony. It is

further significant that the evidence of the victim remained consistent in its material

particulars throughout the trial before the High Court. Accordingly, I am of the view

that the said inconsistencies do not materially undermine the credibility of the victim

or the evidentiary value of the prosecution’s case.

30. Moreover, it should be noted that the learned Trial Judge observed the demeanour and

deportment of the victim, and that the victim was crying while giving the evidence. It

is desirable to emphasize that the learned Trial Judge has the advantage of observing

the demeanour of the witness. Accordingly, the appellate courts are slow to interfere

with the findings of a Trial Judge unless it is demonstrated that the Trial Judge has

manifestly misdirected herself on a material issue, thereby causing prejudice to the

Appellant.

31. In Alwis vs. Piyasena Fernando (1993) 1 Sri. L R 119, it was held that:

“It is well established that findings of primary facts by a trial Judge who hears

and sees witnesses are not to be lightly disturbed on appeal.”

32. Similar sentiments were articulated in Dharmasiri vs. Republic of Sri Lanka [2010] 2

Sri. L.R.241, where it was held that:

“Credibility of a witness is mainly a matter for the trial Judge; the Court of Appeal

will not lightly disturb the findings of a trial Judge with regard to the credibility of

a witness unless such findings of the trial Judge are manifestly wrong.”

33. In Ariyadasa vs. Attorney General [2012] 1 Sri LR 84, the Court of Appeal held that;

“The Court of Appeal will not lightly disturb a finding of a Judge with regard to the

acceptance or rejection of a testimony of a witness, unless it is manifestly wrong,

when the Trial Judge has taken such a decision after observing the demeanour and

deportment of a witness…….”

34. Accordingly, this Court recognizes that the learned Trial Judge had the advantage of

observing the demeanour and deportment of the victim and that such an advantage is

ordinarily entitled to due consideration by the appellate court; any assessment of his

credibility and probability must nevertheless be made upon a proper and cumulative

consideration of the entirety of the evidence, including the evidence elicited in cross-

examination.

35. The next material witness called by the prosecution was PW6, the JMO, who examined

the victim and prepared the Medico-Legal Report. According to the evidence of PW6,

the victim was examined on 30.01.2019 at about 11.45 a.m. The brief history provided

by the victim to the JMO was that, on 28.09.2019 at about 4.30 – 5.00 p.m., he had

gone to a paddy field to pluck green leaves. The Appellant had thereafter taken him to

a nearby jungle, pressed him to the ground, and subjected him to anal penetration. The

victim further stated that he experienced pain and cried following the alleged incident

and returned home approximately ten minutes later. He also stated that the Appellant

threatened to rape her mother if he disclosed the incident to his mother.

36. Upon examining the victim, PW6 made the following observations: a fresh laceration

measuring 1 cm was observed at the 12 o’clock position of the anus; the anal sphincter

opened easily upon traction; stool was observed around the anus. The JMO further

indicated that this was the first occasion on which the alleged anal penetration had

occurred.

37. The JMO opined that, having regard to the observations made during the medical

examination, the laceration identified in the victim’s anal region could have resulted

either from constipation or from the insertion of a foreign object into the anus from

outside. However, the JMO further testified that there was neither a history of

constipation nor any clinical features suggestive of constipation in the victim. In these

circumstances, the medical findings are consistent with the history provided by the

victim to the JMO, namely, that the Appellant had subjected him to anal penetration.

Nevertheless, the medical evidence must be considered together with the other evidence

on record, as the findings are not, in themselves, conclusive as to the precise identity of

the person responsible.

38. According to the evidence of the JMO, the laceration observed in the victim’s anus

could have been sustained within three days preceding the medical examination. As the

victim was examined on 30.01.2019, this medical finding is consistent with the alleged

incident said to have occurred on 28.01.2019.

39. The JMO further opined that it would be practically impossible for the alleged act of

penetration to have occurred while the victim was standing.

40. The testimony of the victim, which the Learned Trial Judge had the advantage of

observing firsthand, was accepted as credible. Her testimony is materially corroborated

by the medical evidence, which confirmed recent penetration, which is compatible with

the history narrated by him. Accordingly, the medical evidence, when considered

together with the victim’s testimony and the other evidence on record, provided a

further basis for evaluating the credibility of the prosecution’s case.

41. In these circumstances, I find no basis to accept the Appellant’s contentions. On the

contrary, the judgment demonstrates a careful consideration of both the prosecution and

defence evidence. Accordingly, I find that the grounds of appeal advanced by the

Appellant are devoid of merit and must fail.

42. For the foregoing reasons, I find no basis to interfere with the findings of the learned

Trial Judge. Accordingly, I affirm the conviction and sentence imposed by the learned

High Court Judge of Anuradhapura on 21.11.2025. In these circumstances, the appeal

is dismissed.

43. Considering all the circumstances, I order that the sentence be operative from the date

of conviction, namely, 21.11.2025.
