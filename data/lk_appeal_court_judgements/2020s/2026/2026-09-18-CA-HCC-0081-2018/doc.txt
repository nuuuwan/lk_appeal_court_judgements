Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentence dated 18.01.2018, delivered by the

learned High Court Judge of Colombo. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to

as “the Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Colombo for committing the

offence of criminal breach of trust, an offence punishable under Section 389 of the

Penal Code.

2. At the conclusion of the trial, the learned High Court Judge found the Appellant guilty

of the charge and convicted him accordingly. Consequently, the Appellant was

sentenced to three years’ rigorous imprisonment and ordered to pay a fine of Rs.

500,000.00, with a default sentence of nine months’ imprisonment. The learned Trial

Judge further ordered the Appellant to pay Rs. 9 million as compensation to PW1, with

a default sentence of eighteen months’ imprisonment.

3. It is against the said conviction and sentence that the Appellant has preferred the instant

appeal.

4. The Appellant has urged the following grounds of appeal;

a. The Accused-Appellant denied the opportunity of a fair trial in the High Court;

b. The Learned High Court Judge has failed to consider the legal principle of

estoppel which is applicable to the main prosecution witness, in his judgment;

c. The Learned High Court Judge has misdirected himself regarding the principles

embodied in Chapter vii of the Evidence Ordinance, especially Section 93 of

the said Ordinance; and

d. The Learned High Court Judge has failed to apply the test of probability to the

stand taken by the main prosecution witness that the Accused-Appellant, being

an Attorney-at-Law of a relatively long standing, will devote his time to projects

without remuneration for a period of two years only on reimbursement of

expenses.

5. The amount involved in the charge of criminal breach of trust was AUD 108,500.00.

The prosecution case is that the said amount was credited to NRFC Account No.

9393462 for the purpose of investing in Hakwatuna Oya Resorts Pvt Ltd. It is alleged

that the Appellant thereafter dishonestly misappropriated or converted the said funds to

his own use, in violation of the terms of the express or implied legal arrangement

between the Appellant and PW1.

6. At the trial, 11 witnesses testified for the prosecution. The Appellant made a dock

statement and did not call any witness to testify.

7. The Appellant has maintained that the monies in question were received by him as

professional fees for the services rendered by him to the said company and, accordingly,

contends that he was entitled to utilize those monies at his discretion.

8. The principal contention advanced by the Appellant was that the Accused-Appellant,

being an Attorney-at-Law of a relatively long standing, would devote his time to

projects without remuneration for a period of two years only on reimbursement of

expenses. So, his argument was that such monies were used as his professional fee for

the services he provided for the said company.

9. Section 388 of the Penal Code provides for the offence of criminal breach of trust.

10. Section 388 of the Penal Code reads as follows:

Whoever, being in any manner entrusted with property, or with any dominion over

property, dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use that property, or

dishonestly uses or disposes of that property in violation of any direction of law

prescribing the mode in which such trust is to be discharged, or of any legal contract,

express or implied, which he has made touching the discharge of such trust, or willfully

suffers any other person so to do, commits 'criminal breach of trust.'

11. In this regard, it would be appropriate to consider the ingredients necessary to prove

criminal breach of trust as follows;

a. Entrustment with property or dominion over property, and either

b. (i). Dishonest misappropriation or conversion to his own use, or

(ii). Dishonest use or disposal, or

(iii). Willfully suffering any other to do b (i) or b (ii)

12. Those being the ingredients necessary to prove the offence of criminal breach of trust,

the entrustment of property is undisputed as per the prosecution witnesses.

13. PW1, Nihal Pallege Nanayakkara, was the first witness to testify for the prosecution.

He is a consultant physician who, after working at several hospitals in Sri Lanka,

migrated to Australia with his wife. According to his evidence, he subsequently formed

an intention to invest in a hotel resort project in Sri Lanka. While working in Sri Lanka,

PW1 became acquainted with one Sumathipala Kariyawasam, a bank employee, and

subsequently came to know the Accused-Appellant through him.

14. Following several discussions concerning the proposed investment, PW1 decided to

name the proposed hotel “Hakwatuna Oya Resorts.” He thereafter caused the business

to be incorporated as a company under the name of ‘Hakwatuna Oya Resorts Pvt Ltd,’

with himself and his wife being registered as its directors. PW1 further appointed

Sumathipala Kariyawasam and the Accused-Appellant as alternative directors of the

said company. In addition, PW1 established another company under the name “Nilaweli

Beach Resorts and Spa,” in which he and his wife were appointed as directors, while

other persons were appointed as alternative directors.

15. In furtherance of the proposed hotel project, PW1, on several occasions, remitted funds

from his wife’s account maintained at the Australian ANZ Bank to his NRFC Account

maintained at the People’s Bank in Sri Lanka. At the request of the Accused-Appellant

and Kariyawasam, PW1 subsequently executed a power of attorney authorizing them

to operate and deal with the funds maintained in the said NRFC account for the purpose

of making investments in and meeting expenses relating to the proposed hotel project.

16. By virtue of the authority conferred upon them under the said power of attorney, the

Accused-Appellant and Kariyawasam thereby obtained control over the funds

deposited into PW1’s NRFC account. PW1’s evidence was that he had expressly

instructed them that the monies placed at their disposal were to be utilized solely for

the purposes connected with the proposed hotel investment.

such services. On the contrary, the evidence was that the expenses and incidental costs

monies paid pursuant to those invoices represented genuine professional fees for

services rendered to Hakwatuna Oya Resorts Pvt Ltd.

61. Furthermore, the evidence regarding the actual movement and subsequent utilization of

the monies is inconsistent with the assertion that the entire amount of AUD 108,500

was paid to the Accused-Appellant as professional remuneration. The evidence

demonstrates that the monies were entrusted to him in connection with the investment

in the hotel project and the acquisition of his agreed 7% shares in the company.

However, portions of those funds were subsequently transferred between his personal

accounts, utilized towards the settlement of a bank overdraft, and placed in fixed

deposits.

62. Accordingly, the aforesaid contention of the Accused-Appellant is devoid of merit and

cannot be sustained on the evidence. The totality of the evidence establishes the nature

and purpose of the monies entrusted to the Accused-Appellant and does not support the

assertion that the said monies were paid to him as professional fees. Therefore, the said

ground of appeal must necessarily fail.

63. In this regard, the following authorities further elaborate the essential ingredients

required to establish the offence of criminal breach of trust;

64. In Attorney General Vs. Dewapriya Walgamage and Another (1990) 2 Sri. L.R. 212,

held that;

“If the initial taking could be only an irregularity of procedure or would not

constitute theft or cheating, the accused could be found guilty of criminal

misappropriation or criminal breach of trust. The correct test is to ascertain

whether the accused is guilty of another offence such as cheating or theft at the time

of the initial taking.”

20 | P a g e

“I have already held that the evidence in the case does not in any way warrant an

inference that the accused was guilty of the offence of cheating at the time of the

initial taking of the property. In these circumstances I see no merit in the

submissions made by Counsel for the Accused-Appellant. I accordingly uphold the

conviction of the Accused-Appellant.”

65. In Basnayake Vs. Officer-in-Charge of Special Crimes Detection Unit,

Anuradhapura (1988) 2 Sri. L.R. 50, held that;

“The inference of dishonest misappropriation or conversion is an essential

ingredient of the offence under Section 391 of the Penal Code.”

66. In Kalyanaratne Vs. Gunadasa 40 NLR 44, held that;

“It is submitted on behalf of the appellant that his liability is civil only since he

received the Rs. 5, if he did receive it, by virtue of the promissory note which was

made out to him, and Gunadasa was liable in law to discharge his obligation to the

appellant, and the appellant only, who thus received the money in his own legal

right. But I think the terms of section 388 of the Penal Code are sufficiently wide to

cover a case of this kind, and illustration (a) to this section certainly indicates one

instance where the legal owner of property can commit criminal breach of trust in

respect of it. If that were not so, many cases of misappropriation by servants and

agents of the property for which they are bound to account to their employers would

go unpunished. This case does in fact bear a sufficiency of resemblance to the King

Vs. Perera, where Jayewardena A.J. upheld the conviction for criminal breach of

trust of a salesman who misappropriated cheques made out in his own name by

purchasers of his employer’s goods.”

67. The principles set out in the aforesaid authorities are directly applicable to the present

case. The evidence establishes that the monies were entrusted to the Accused-Appellant

pursuant to an agreement for a specific purpose, namely, to be utilized in connection

with the investment in Hakwatuna Oya Resorts (Pvt) Ltd and the acquisition of his

agreed 7% shares in the project. The Accused-Appellant was therefore not placed in

possession of the monies with unrestricted right to deal with them as his own. His

CA/HCC/0081/2018

21 | P a g e

obligation was to utilize the funds in accordance with the arrangement between the

parties.

68. The evidence, however, demonstrates a departure from that agreed purpose. Instead of

applying the monies towards the intended investment, the Accused-Appellant

transferred substantial sums between his personal bank accounts, utilized part of the

funds to settle a personal bank overdraft and subsequently placed monies in fixed

deposits. These transactions cannot reasonably be regarded as acts done in furtherance

of the investment for which the funds had been entrusted and thereby breached the

arrangement pursuant to which the monies had been entrusted to him.

69. In these circumstances, the subsequent use of the entrusted funds for his own financial

affairs cannot be justified merely by referring to the services he had rendered to the

company as contended by the Appellant. As recognized in the aforesaid authorities,

even where a person asserts a personal right or interest in property, criminal breach of

trust may arise where the property was entrusted to him for a particular purpose and he

dishonestly deals with it otherwise.

70. The circumstances therefore provide a sufficient basis for the inference of dishonest

misappropriation or conversion required for the offence of criminal breach of trust. The

authorities cited above, when applied to the facts of the present case, support the

conclusion that the Accused-Appellant dishonestly converted monies entrusted to him

for his own use.
