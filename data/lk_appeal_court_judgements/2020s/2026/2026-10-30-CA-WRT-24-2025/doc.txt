Position of the Respondents

The Respondents resist the application. Their principal contention is that the Petitioners’

acquittal in criminal proceedings does not automatically entitle them to payment of the salary

and other emoluments withheld during interdiction.

It is submitted that criminal proceedings and departmental disciplinary proceedings are

distinct and that an acquittal by a criminal court does not necessarily amount to exoneration

for disciplinary purposes.

The Respondents further maintain that the Petitioners did not perform duties during the

period of compulsory leave and interdiction and were therefore not entitled to remuneration

for that period.

It is also contended that annual salary increments are not automatic entitlements but are

subject to the applicable conditions of service.

The Respondents raise a preliminary objection that the Petitioners have not identified, with

sufficient certainty, the administrative decisions they seek to quash by writs of certiorari.

They further submit that the Petitioners have failed to establish a clear legal right and a

corresponding public duty sufficient to justify the issuance of writs of mandamus.

Issues for Determination

Having considered the pleadings, documents and submissions, the principal issues to be

determined are;

• whether the decisions sought to be quashed have been sufficiently identified;

• whether the Petitioners’ acquittal and reinstatement confer an entitlement to the

withheld salary;

• whether the failure to conclude disciplinary proceedings affects the legality of the

continued non-payment; and what significance should be attached to the Attorney-

General’s advice, marked R1.

• Whether the decision of the CAA to initiate a preliminary disciplinary inquiry

concerning the allegations dating back to September 2016, after a prolonged and

unexplained delay of more than eight years, notwithstanding the Petitioners’

10

subsequent acquittal and reinstatement in service, constitutes a lawful and reasonable

exercise of disciplinary power.

It is also necessary to determine whether the Petitioners are entitled to annual salary

increments during the period of compulsory leave and placement on the appropriate salary

scales according to their seniority, and ultimately, whether the circumstances justify the

issuance of the writs sought.

Preliminary Objection

Learned State Counsel submits that the application is not maintainable because the Petitioners

have failed to identify the administrative decisions sought to be quashed, namely, the prayers

(e), (f), (g), (h) and (i) refer generally without identifying the dates of those decisions or the

persons responsible for making them.

Ordinarily, a writ of certiorari is directed against an identifiable administrative decision. The

Court must be able to identify the challenged decision and the grounds on which its validity is

questioned.

However, the absence of a formal decision addressed directly to an applicant does not

necessarily establish that no administrative decision was made.

In the present application, P15(b) expressly records that the CAA had decided not to pay

remuneration for the relevant period because the officers concerned had not performed duties.

The letter marked P15(b), addressed by the Chairman of the CAA to the Labour Officer,

Colombo South, expressly discloses the decision of the CAA not to pay remuneration to the

officers concerned for the relevant period on the ground that they had not performed duties

during their interdiction.

The Petitioners have also specifically complained of the continued refusal to pay their

salaries and grant annual increments.

Furthermore, the Respondents have produced the Attorney-General’s advice marked R1,

which directly concerns the question of payment of salary arrears.

In these circumstances, I cannot accept the contention that the application must fail merely

because the Petitioners have not produced a formal letter addressed to him communicating

the refusal.

11

The material before this Court sufficiently discloses an administrative position concerning

non-payment which is capable of judicial examination.

Nevertheless, the Court must maintain the distinction between the refusal of salary arrears

and the alleged refusal of increments, as the evidentiary foundation for the two claims is not

identical.

Accordingly, I reject the preliminary objection insofar as it seeks to dismiss the entire

application without considering the merits.

The Relevant Provisions of the Establishments Code

The letters marked P4(a), P4(b), P5(a) and P5(b) expressly refer to Chapter XLVIII of the

Establishments Code.

The Respondents have also relied upon the provisions of that Chapter in justifying the

decisions concerning the Petitioners.

It is therefore necessary to examine the relevant provisions, while recognising that the CAA

is a statutory authority and that the precise applicability of the Establishments Code must be

determined in the light of the governing conditions of service.

Paragraph 31:10 provides that the non-payment of emoluments or payment of one-half of the

emoluments of an officer under interdiction is to be decided by the Disciplinary Authority.

Paragraph 31:11 identifies circumstances in which an interdicted officer should not receive

emoluments during interdiction.

Paragraph 31:11:1 specifically refers to circumstances where legal proceedings have been

initiated for an offence of bribery or corruption.

The allegations against the Petitioners concerned bribery. Accordingly, if these provisions

apply, the initial withholding of emoluments during interdiction cannot be regarded as

unlawful merely because the Petitioners were subsequently acquitted.

However, the authority to withhold emoluments during interdiction must be distinguished

from the ultimate determination of whether those emoluments should remain unpaid after the

conclusion of the relevant proceedings.

Paragraph 31:14 provides that where a formal disciplinary inquiry results in the imposition of

a punishment less than dismissal, the Disciplinary Authority should decide whether the full

12

emoluments withheld, or a percentage thereof, should be paid. Such determination forms part

of the disciplinary order.

Paragraph 31:15 defines the expression “emoluments” by reference to the emoluments of the

officer’s substantive post. It excludes allowances in the nature of duty allowances and

reimbursements of expenditure incurred in the performance of official duties, including

travel, transport, and combined allowances.

Paragraph 31:17 recognises that reinstatement of an officer who has not been found guilty,

following interdiction without sufficient cause, may result in payment of emoluments for a

period during which no work was performed. The provision therefore requires the appropriate

authority to satisfy itself before interdicting an officer.

These provisions demonstrate that the initial withholding of emoluments and their ultimate

payment are distinct matters.

Paragraph 31:11:1 cannot, by itself, be interpreted as authorising the permanent forfeiture of

salary following acquittal and reinstatement, irrespective of the subsequent conduct of the

Disciplinary Authority.

Equally, Paragraph 31:17 does not establish that every criminal acquittal automatically

entitles an officer to full back wages.

The ultimate entitlement must be determined upon the applicable legal provisions and the

particular circumstances of the case.

Effect of Criminal Acquittal and Reinstatement

The High Court acquitted the Petitioners on 18th May 2023 under Section 200(1) of the Code

of Criminal Procedure Act, without calling the defence. That acquittal ended the criminal

proceedings. Nevertheless, an acquittal in criminal proceedings does not necessarily prevent a

disciplinary authority from conducting an independent inquiry into the same conduct.

The purposes and standards of proof applicable to criminal and disciplinary proceedings are

different. Therefore, I do not accept the proposition that the Petitioners’ criminal acquittal,

considered in isolation, automatically entitled them to recover the entirety of the salary

withheld during interdiction.

However, the present case does not concern criminal acquittal alone. The CAA subsequently

reinstated the Petitioners with effect from 01st August 2023. No material establishes that the

13

reinstatement was made subject to a concluded finding of disciplinary action or that the CAA

imposed any disciplinary order on the Petitioners. More importantly, this Court has not been

presented with any concluded disciplinary inquiry or final disciplinary order.

The legal effect of these circumstances must therefore be examined together, not separately.

The Decision in Nandadasa v M.S. Jayasinghe

In Nandadasa v. M. S. Jayasinghe, Secretary, Ministry of Justice and Constitutional

Affairs and Others (2001) 1 SLR 14, the Supreme Court considered the legality of a

disciplinary authority’s decision to withhold permanently the salary of a public officer who

had been interdicted following allegations of bribery.

The petitioner in that case was prosecuted for bribery and subsequently acquitted. Following

his acquittal, he was reinstated subject to a disciplinary inquiry. The inquiring officer

exonerated him of all charges. However, the disciplinary authority rejected those findings,

found him guilty and directed that the salary withheld during interdiction should not be paid.

The only other punishment imposed was a warning.

Justice Mark Fernando, examining the relevant provisions of Chapter XLVIII of the

Establishments Code, drew a distinction between withholding salary during interdiction and

subsequently deciding that the withheld salary should not be paid.

Justice Fernando observed at page 24 as:

“The initial withholding of salary during interdiction is not a punishment. It cannot

be a punishment, because it is an order made before there is any finding of guilt.”

The Supreme Court then examined whether the subsequent non-payment could lawfully be

imposed independently of a disciplinary punishment.

Under the provisions then applicable, the Court held that the order concerning withheld salary

was a consequence of punishment and that, since the disciplinary authority had imposed only

a warning, which was expressly excluded from the category of punishments, the order

directing non-payment lacked the necessary legal foundation. The Supreme Court also found

serious violations of natural justice in the manner in which the disciplinary authority rejected

the inquiring officer’s findings and in the subsequent consideration of the petitioner’s appeal.

The Supreme Court quashed the impugned orders and held the petitioner entitled to the salary

withheld during interdiction, together with interest.

14

The importance of the reasoning of Nandadasa lies in the distinction between provisional

withholding of emoluments during interdiction and their ultimate deprivation.

The former does not necessarily establish the legality of the latter. However, a factual

distinction exists between the Nandadasa judgement and the present application.

In Nandadasa, a disciplinary inquiry had been conducted and a final disciplinary order made,

although that order was subsequently found to be unlawful.

In the present application, no concluded disciplinary inquiry or final disciplinary order has

been established.

Nevertheless, the underlying principle remains relevant. The ultimate refusal to pay withheld

emoluments must be supported by the applicable legal provisions and cannot be justified

solely by reference to the original interdiction.

The Supreme Court also emphasised the importance of disclosing findings and reasons to an

officer affected by an adverse disciplinary decision.

The Decision in Don Karunasena Athukorala

In Don Karunasena Athukorala v. H. M. Gunasekera and Others (2016) 1 Sri LR 286, the

Supreme Court considered the withholding of emoluments during interdiction and the

financial consequences of disciplinary proceedings against a public officer.

The petitioner, a school principal, had been interdicted following allegations of bribery and

was subsequently acquitted in criminal proceedings.

However, an independent disciplinary inquiry was conducted, and he was found guilty of

nine charges of misconduct.

The disciplinary authority thereafter determined that half wages should be paid during the

period of interdiction.

Justice Prasanna Jayawardena, examining the relevant provisions of Chapter XLVIII of the

Establishments Code, upheld the decision concerning payment of half wages.

The Supreme Court also considered the legality of deductions from the petitioner’s pension

and gratuity and set aside the pension deduction because the prescribed procedural

requirements had not been complied with. The decision confirms that criminal acquittal does

15

not automatically invalidate an earlier interdiction or establish an unconditional entitlement to

full emoluments.

It also demonstrates that the financial consequences of disciplinary proceedings must be

determined in accordance with the applicable legal provisions.

The distinction between that case and the present application is significant.

In Athukorala, disciplinary proceedings had been concluded, and findings of misconduct had

been made.

In the present application, the CAA communicated its intention to initiate disciplinary

proceedings in October 2016 but did not establish that any disciplinary inquiry had been

initiated or concluded to date.

Accordingly, Athukorala supports the Respondents’ contention that criminal acquittal does

not automatically entitle an employee to back wages.

However, it does not resolve the separate question whether the CAA may continue to deny

payment indefinitely without concluding disciplinary proceedings or making a lawful

determination concerning the withheld salary.

The Decision in Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis

In Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis v. D. K. A. Sanath Kumara and Others,

SC/FR/100/2022, decided on 06th October 2023, the Supreme Court considered the

prolonged interdiction of a police officer without the conduct of a preliminary investigation

as required by the applicable provisions of Chapter XLVIII of the Establishments Code.

The petitioner in that case had been interdicted without pay following his arrest in connection

with alleged criminal offences.

Although the relevant authority possessed the power to interdict him, no preliminary

investigation had been conducted for more than two years.

Justice Thurairaja, examined Paragraphs 31:3, 31:4, 31:5:3, 31:7 and 27:10 of the

Establishments Code and emphasised the duty of the disciplinary authority to comply with

the prescribed procedural requirements.

Of particular relevance is the Supreme Court’s consideration of Paragraph 27:10, as amended

by Public Administration Circular No. 06/2004 dated 15th December 2004.

16

The provision required a preliminary investigation to be conducted within two months in the

circumstances contemplated therein.

The Supreme Court also considered Paragraph 27:10:1, which permits the disciplinary

authority, in the circumstances specified by that provision, to authorise payment of salary not

exceeding one-half where the criminal proceedings remain unresolved beyond one year.

The Supreme Court strongly criticised the failure to commence a preliminary investigation

even after the lapse of two years.

The petitioner was subsequently reinstated during the pendency of the fundamental rights

application, and arrears of salary were paid.

The Supreme Court held that the petitioner’s fundamental rights under Articles 12(1) and

14(1)(g) of the Constitution were violated.

Significantly, the Court directed the retrospective grant of salary increments, benefits and

promotions, in addition to awarding compensation.

The decision demonstrates that the exercise of disciplinary powers is subject to the

procedural safeguards prescribed by law and that prolonged administrative inaction may have

consequences affecting an officer’s financial entitlements.

It must nevertheless be recognised that Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis arose in the

exercise of the Supreme Court’s fundamental rights jurisdiction and concerned facts

materially different from those in the present application.

Furthermore, the applicability of the precise procedural requirements considered in that case

to employees of the CAA depends upon the governing service rules.

Nevertheless, the broader principle is relevant. A disciplinary authority must exercise its

powers within the framework prescribed by law and cannot leave an employee’s disciplinary

position unresolved indefinitely.

Decision to Conduct a Preliminary Inquiry

The Petitioners have also sought, by prayer (f) of the Petition, a writ of certiorari to quash the

decision of the CAA to conduct a preliminary inquiry concerning the incident which gave rise

to the criminal proceedings instituted against them.

17

The Respondents have raised a preliminary objection that the Petitioners failed to identify or

produce a specific decision that certiorari can quash. They further contend that a decision to

conduct a preliminary investigation does not constitute a final determination affecting the

Petitioners’ rights and that challenging it is premature.

I cannot accept the proposition that a decision to initiate disciplinary proceedings is

necessarily immune from judicial review merely because the proceedings have not

concluded. Where such a decision is made without lawful authority, in breach of mandatory

legal requirements, for an improper purpose, or in circumstances amounting to an abuse of

administrative power, the supervisory jurisdiction of this Court may be invoked.

Nevertheless, the Petitioners must establish an identifiable decision amenable to certiorari

and demonstrate sufficient grounds to warrant judicial intervention.

Paragraph 22 of the Petition states that the Petitioners were informed by letters dated 03rd

October 2016, marked P5(a) and P5(b), that the CAA had decided to initiate disciplinary

action against them. Although the CAA communicated its decision to initiate disciplinary

action against the Petitioners in October 2016, no material has been placed before this Court

establishing that any preliminary investigation or formal disciplinary inquiry was thereafter

commenced.

The Petitioners were subsequently acquitted of the criminal charges by the High Court on 18th

May 2023. Thereafter, the CAA reinstated them in service with effect from 01st August 2023,

without imposing any express condition relating to disciplinary proceedings. The letters of

reinstatement, marked P9(a) to P9(c), do not refer to any pending or proposed disciplinary

inquiry.

In paragraph 24 of the Petition, the Petitioners state that, at a meeting held on 26th December

2024 with the 2nd and 3rd Respondents, they were informed that the Board of Directors of the

CAA had decided to conduct a preliminary inquiry concerning the same incident.

However, the Respondents denied the averments in paragraph 24 of the Petition in paragraph

14 of their Statement of Objections, insofar as they are inconsistent with the matters

subsequently pleaded. Although the Respondents maintain that the CAA retains the authority

to institute disciplinary proceedings notwithstanding the Petitioners’ acquittal, they have not

expressly admitted that the Board made the particular decision alleged to have been

communicated on 26th December 2024.

18

It is relevant that, by prayer (d) of the Petition, the Petitioners have expressly sought a

direction requiring the Respondents to produce the minutes of the Board of Directors

recording the alleged decision to conduct a preliminary inquiry. Such records would

ordinarily be within the possession or control of the CAA. Nevertheless, no Board resolution

or other documentary material recording the alleged decision has been produced before this

Court.

The failure to produce the relevant records must be considered in the light of the pleadings

and the evidence. However, the mere absence of the Board minutes does not necessarily

establish the alleged decision. The Petitioners must still establish a sufficient factual

foundation for the issuance of certiorari.

Apart from the question of whether the alleged decision has been sufficiently established, the

prolonged delay in taking disciplinary action requires careful consideration.

In Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis, Thurairaja, PC, J., considered the relevant authorities’

prolonged failure to conduct a preliminary investigation in accordance with Chapter XLVIII

of the Establishments Code. The Supreme Court emphasised the obligation of disciplinary

authorities to comply with the prescribed procedures and to act without unreasonable delay.

Although that decision arose in the exercise of the Supreme Court’s fundamental rights

jurisdiction, the principles concerning prolonged administrative inaction and compliance with

disciplinary procedures are relevant when examining the legality of the exercise of

disciplinary powers.

I accept that an acquittal in criminal proceedings does not, by itself, extinguish the

disciplinary jurisdiction of an employer. However, the question arising for determination is

whether the CAA can lawfully exercise that jurisdiction after an inordinate and unexplained

delay, particularly where the Authority had knowledge of the alleged misconduct from the

outset and had already communicated its intention to initiate disciplinary action.

The Supreme Court of India has considered the principles governing prolonged delay in

disciplinary proceedings in several decisions which, although not binding on this Court,

provide persuasive guidance.

In State of Madhya Pradesh v. Bani Singh and Another, 1990 Supp SCC 738, the Supreme

Court considered disciplinary proceedings initiated more than twelve years after the alleged

misconduct. In paragraph 4 of its judgement, the Court observed that the department had been

19

aware of the alleged irregularities for several years and had offered no satisfactory

explanation for the delay. The Court upheld the decision quashing the disciplinary

proceedings, holding that permitting the inquiry to proceed after such an unexplained lapse of

time would be unfair.

The significance of the above judgement lies in the recognition that a disciplinary authority

cannot indefinitely postpone proceedings concerning allegations already within its knowledge

and then insist on proceeding as though the passage of time were legally irrelevant.

In State of Andhra Pradesh v. N. Radhakishan, (1998) 4 SCC 154, the Supreme Court

examined the circumstances in which prolonged delay may vitiate disciplinary proceedings.

In paragraph 19 of its judgement, the Court recognised that no inflexible rule could be

prescribed for every case and that the relevant circumstances must be weighed, including the

nature of the charges, their complexity, the reasons for the delay, the prejudice occasioned to

the employee and the public interest in maintaining disciplinary standards.

The Court further recognised that where the delay is unexplained, prejudice to the employee

may be inferred. It emphasised that disciplinary proceedings must be pursued with reasonable

expedition and that delay may defeat justice. Applying those principles, the Supreme Court

upheld the quashing of the relevant charge memorandum.

The decision in P. V. Mahadevan v. Managing Director, Tamil Nadu Housing Board, (2005)

6 SCC 636, is particularly relevant to the present application. In that case, disciplinary

proceedings were initiated approximately ten years after the alleged irregularity. The

Supreme Court considered the earlier decisions in Bani Singh and N. Radhakishan and

examined whether the employer had offered a satisfactory explanation for the delay.

In the concluding portion of its judgement, the Supreme Court recognised that allowing

disciplinary proceedings to continue after such a prolonged period would cause serious

prejudice to the employee. The Court considered the mental distress associated with

unresolved allegations concerning corruption and integrity and concluded that it was

necessary to bring the proceedings to an end. Accordingly, the Court quashed the charge

memorandum.

The reasoning in these decisions shows that disciplinary jurisdiction does not confer an

unrestricted right to exercise it, regardless of the passage of time. Where the disciplinary

authority remains inactive for an inordinate period without satisfactory explanation,

20

subsequent initiation of proceedings may constitute an unreasonable and unfair exercise of

administrative power.

In the present application, the CAA was aware of the allegations against the Petitioners in

September 2016. Indeed, it communicated its decision to initiate disciplinary action in

October 2016. Nevertheless, no preliminary investigation or formal disciplinary inquiry

commenced during the intervening period.

The criminal proceedings were concluded in May 2023, and the Petitioners were reinstated in

August 2023. Even after their acquittal and reinstatement, the Respondents have not shown

that they initiated any disciplinary proceedings before communicating the alleged decision in

December 2024.

By December 2024, more than eight years had elapsed since the original allegations and the

communication of the decision to initiate disciplinary action. The continued absence of a

concluded disciplinary process must also be viewed against the further passage of time

thereafter.

The Respondents have not furnished a satisfactory explanation for this prolonged inaction.

Although the allegations are serious, their seriousness cannot, by itself, justify an indefinite

failure to exercise disciplinary powers. Nor can the mere pendency of criminal proceedings

automatically explain the failure to initiate disciplinary action, without evidence

demonstrating why the Authority was unable or legally prevented from proceeding.

The opinion of the Hon. Attorney-General, marked R1, does not alter this conclusion. The

Respondents’ contention in paragraph 22 of their Statement of Objections, that the opinion

does not prohibit disciplinary proceedings, may be accepted as a statement concerning the

scope of that opinion. However, the absence of such a prohibition does not establish that

disciplinary proceedings may lawfully be initiated after an inordinate and unexplained delay.

The Petitioners have already undergone a prolonged period of compulsory leave and

interdiction, have faced criminal prosecution, and have subsequently been acquitted and

reinstated. The continued uncertainty concerning disciplinary proceedings, together with the

withholding of their financial entitlements, is relevant when assessing the prejudice arising

from the Respondents’ inaction.

I am mindful that the allegations concern bribery and that the public interest requires

allegations of misconduct by employees of public authorities to be dealt with seriously.

21

Nevertheless, that public interest also requires disciplinary authorities to act diligently, fairly

and within a reasonable period. The interests of sound administration are not served by

allowing disciplinary proceedings to remain indefinitely unresolved.

Having considered the cumulative circumstances, I am of the view that the prolonged and

unexplained delay in the present case is unreasonable and that the belated decision to conduct

a preliminary inquiry concerning the allegations dating back to 2016 cannot be justified

merely by reference to the CAA’s continuing disciplinary powers.

There remains the question of identifying the particular decision sought to be quashed. The

Petitioners allege that the Respondents communicated the decision to them on 26th December

2024. Although the Respondents have not produced the relevant Board resolution, the

Petitioners have expressly sought its production by prayer (d) of the Petition. The failure to

produce records ordinarily in the Respondents’ possession is a relevant circumstance in

evaluating the evidence concerning the alleged decision.

On considering the pleadings and the evidence regarding the communication of the decision

and the Respondents’ position, I find that the impugned decision to conduct the preliminary

inquiry is sufficiently established.

For the reasons set out above, I hold that the decision to conduct the proposed preliminary

inquiry, after such prolonged and unexplained delay, constitutes an unreasonable and unfair

exercise of administrative power and is liable to be quashed.

Accordingly, a writ of certiorari is issued quashing the decision of the CAA to conduct the

preliminary inquiry concerning the allegations which gave rise to the criminal proceedings

against the Petitioners, as sought in prayer (f) of the Petition.

The Failure to Communicate Reasons

There is another significant aspect of the CAA’s conduct.

Although the CAA had decided not to pay the withheld remuneration, no reasoned decision

explaining the legal basis for such non-payment appears to have been communicated to the

Petitioners.

The letter marked P15(b), addressed to the Labour Officer in connection with another

officer’s complaint, states that the CAA had decided not to pay remuneration because the

officers concerned had not performed duties during the relevant period. However, that letter

22

was not addressed to the Petitioners. Nor does it disclose any determination of their

entitlement under the applicable provisions of the Establishments Code. It is particularly

significant that the CAA had reinstated the Petitioners following their acquittal without

having concluded disciplinary proceedings against them. In these circumstances, the CAA

was required to address their claim in accordance with the applicable legal provisions and

communicate a decision supported by lawful reasons.

The failure to communicate such a decision assumes greater significance when considered

alongside the prolonged failure to conduct disciplinary proceedings.

The Petitioners were left without a determination of their disciplinary responsibility and

without an adequate explanation of the legal basis upon which their financial claims were

being denied.

An administrative authority cannot merely withhold financial benefits and leave the affected

persons to ascertain the reasons for that decision from correspondence addressed to another

institution concerning another employee.

The importance of communicating findings and reasons was emphasised by Fernando J. in

Nandadasa, although the particular statutory context of that case differed from the present

application.

In Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis, the Supreme Court likewise emphasised that

administrative decisions must be supported by material capable of justifying them when

challenged before a court.

In the present case, the failure to communicate a reasoned decision is a material consideration

in determining whether the CAA acted lawfully and reasonably.

Legal Effect of the Attorney-General’s Advice Marked R1

A further matter of considerable significance is the advice furnished by the Hon. Attorney-

General dated 06th December 2024, which has been produced by the Respondents marked

R1.

According to R1, the Attorney-General advised that, since the Petitioners had been acquitted

by the High Court, the Disciplinary Authority had reinstated them in service, and no appeal

had been preferred against the acquittal, arrears of salary should be paid for the period

commencing on 01st September 2016 and ending on 01st August 2023.

23

Thus, the precise question concerning the payment of salary withheld during the period of

compulsory leave and interdiction was considered by the Attorney-General.

The advice was not confined to a general observation concerning the Petitioners’ acquittal. It

addressed the financial consequences of the acquittal and reinstatement and identified the

period for which salary arrears should be paid.

It is necessary to emphasise that advice furnished by the Attorney-General does not, by itself,

constitute a binding administrative determination requiring payment.

The responsibility for making the relevant administrative decision remains with the authority

upon which the law confers that power.

Nevertheless, advice furnished by the Attorney-General on a question of law is plainly a

material consideration in determining whether the subsequent conduct of the Authority was

lawful and reasonable.

The CAA was not entitled to disregard that advice arbitrarily.

If the CAA considered that the advice should not be followed, it was incumbent upon the

competent decision-making body to identify a lawful basis for adopting a different position.

In the present application, the Respondents have not demonstrated that a reasoned

determination rejecting the Petitioners’ claim, notwithstanding the advice contained in R1,

was communicated to them.

The explanation appearing in P15(b), namely that the officers concerned had not performed

duties during the relevant period, does not satisfactorily answer the question.

The Petitioners’ absence from service resulted from compulsory leave and interdiction

imposed upon them by the CAA itself.

Moreover, the Establishments Code expressly contemplates circumstances in which

emoluments withheld during interdiction may subsequently become payable.

The mere fact that an officer did not perform duties during interdiction cannot therefore be

treated as an absolute bar to the subsequent payment of withheld salary.

The significance of R1 is further reinforced by the absence of a concluded disciplinary

inquiry or an adverse disciplinary order.

24

The CAA had decided to initiate disciplinary proceedings in October 2016 but failed to bring

those proceedings to a conclusion to date.

The Attorney-General’s advice must therefore be considered together with the Petitioners’

acquittal, reinstatement, the absence of an adverse disciplinary determination and the CAA’s

prolonged failure to conclude disciplinary proceedings.

In these circumstances, I am of the view that the continued refusal to pay the Petitioners’

salary arrears, without identifying a lawful basis for departing from the advice contained in

R1, cannot be justified merely upon the ground that they did not perform duties during the

period of interdiction.

Unreasonableness and procedural unfairness

Having considered the cumulative effect of the foregoing circumstances, I am satisfied that

the continued refusal of the CAA to pay the Petitioners’ salary arrears, without identifying

and communicating a lawful basis for that refusal, is unreasonable and procedurally unfair.

The absence of a concluded disciplinary inquiry, the subsequent reinstatement of the

Petitioners, and the failure to address the advice furnished by the Hon. Attorney-General are

material considerations in reaching this conclusion. Accordingly, the continued refusal to pay

the withheld salary, founded merely upon the ground that the Petitioners did not perform

duties during the period of interdiction, cannot be sustained.

Meaning of Emoluments Under Paragraph 31:15

It is necessary to clarify the scope of the financial entitlement claimed by the Petitioners.

Paragraph 31:15 of Chapter XLVIII defines the expression “emoluments” as the emoluments

attached to an officer’s substantive post. The provision expressly excludes allowances in the

nature of duty allowances and reimbursements of expenditure incurred in the performance of

official duties, including travel, transport and combined allowances.

Accordingly, an entitlement to recover withheld emoluments does not necessarily extend to

allowances payable only upon the actual performance of official duties or the incurring of

expenditure in connection with those duties.

Any amount payable to the Petitioners must therefore be calculated based on the salary and

other emoluments lawfully attached to their substantive post, subject to the exclusions

prescribed by the applicable provisions.

25

The Petitioners cannot claim reimbursement of expenses which were never incurred merely

because they are entitled to recover salary withheld during interdiction.

Annual Salary Increments

The Petitioners also seek writs of certiorari and mandamus in respect of annual salary

increments for the period of their compulsory leave commencing on 01st September 2016.

This claim requires separate consideration.

An annual salary increment is not necessarily an unconditional entitlement arising merely

from the passage of time. Ordinarily, the grant of an increment depends upon the applicable

conditions of service, satisfactory performance and eligibility under the relevant salary

scheme. An employee claiming increments must therefore establish that the conditions

governing their grant have been satisfied.

However, Paragraphs 24:6 and 24:7 of Chapter XLVIII of the Establishments Code make

specific provision concerning salary increments in the context of disciplinary proceedings.

Paragraph 24:6 provides that where deferment, reduction or stoppage of a salary increment is

ordered as part of a disciplinary order, the order should be implemented with reference to the

salary step last received by the officer. The provision further contemplates the grant of

subsequent increments even where the officer was not in service. Paragraph 24:7 provides

that where an order not to pay arrears of salary during interdiction forms part of a disciplinary

order, the officer will not be entitled to arrears of salary increments for which he would

otherwise have been eligible. However, the provision expressly requires that the officer be

placed on the appropriate step of the salary scale, taking into account the increments for

which he would have been eligible, notwithstanding the fact that he was not in service. These

provisions demonstrate that the non-payment of salary arrears during interdiction does not

necessarily justify the permanent denial of salary progression. It is important to distinguish

between the actual payment of arrears of increments and the recognition of eligible

increments for determining the appropriate salary step.

In the present application, no concluded disciplinary inquiry or final disciplinary order

imposing stoppage, deferment or reduction of increments has been established.

The Petitioners were reinstated with effect from 01st August 2023 and thereafter continued in

service to date.

26

The CAA cannot therefore rely merely upon their absence from service during interdiction as

a sufficient basis for refusing to recognise increments for which they were otherwise eligible.

However, it does not follow that the Petitioners are automatically entitled to every increment

claimed. The Petitioners must establish their eligibility under the applicable service rules. The

position relating to the period following reinstatement is particularly significant. The

Petitioners actually performed duties from 01st August 2023 to date.

If their salary upon reinstatement was fixed at an incorrect step because otherwise eligible

increments had not been recognised, that error may have affected the salary payable during

the subsequent period of actual service.

The decision in Rannulu Sugath Mohana Mendis is relevant in this context.

The Supreme Court directed the retrospective grant of salary increments, benefits and

promotions upon finding that the relevant authorities had violated the petitioner’s

fundamental rights.

Nevertheless, that decision does not dispense with the requirement to establish eligibility

under the governing service rules in every case.

Accordingly, I am of the view that the Petitioners are entitled to have their eligibility for

annual increments determined in accordance with the applicable conditions of service and,

where such eligibility is established, to have those increments recognised for the purpose of

determining their appropriate salary step.

Any consequential arrears arising from an incorrect salary placement following reinstatement

must also be addressed in accordance with law.

PAYE Tax

The Petitioners also seek a direction that PAYE tax should not be deducted from any lump-

sum payment of salary arrears. I am unable to accept this claim as an unconditional

entitlement. Any salary arrears or other remuneration payable to the Petitioners must be dealt

with in accordance with the applicable provisions of the Inland Revenue Act and the statutory

requirements governing deductions of income tax. The fact that remuneration is paid in a

lump sum following a period of withholding does not, by itself, establish an exemption from

taxation.

27

Accordingly, any payment made pursuant to this judgement shall be subject to such

deductions as are required by law.

Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, I am of the view that the Petitioners have established sufficient

grounds for the exercise of the discretionary writ jurisdiction of this Court.

Accordingly, the application is allowed in part, and the following relief is granted.

A writ of certiorari is issued quashing the decision of the CAA to conduct a preliminary

disciplinary inquiry against the Petitioners concerning the allegations which gave rise to the

criminal proceedings instituted against them, as sought in prayer (f) of the Petition. Having

regard to the prolonged and unexplained delay since the alleged misconduct in 2016, the

subsequent acquittal and reinstatement of the Petitioners, and the principles discussed in this

judgement, I am of the view that the belated decision to conduct such an inquiry constitutes

an unreasonable and unfair exercise of administrative power.

A writ of certiorari is also issued quashing the decision of the CAA refusing payment of the

Petitioners’ salary arrears for the period commencing on 01st September 2016 and ending

upon their reinstatement with effect from 01st August 2023, insofar as that refusal is founded

upon the ground that the Petitioners did not perform duties during the relevant period.

A writ of mandamus is issued directing the CAA to calculate and pay the salary arrears

lawfully due to the Petitioners for the said period, having regard to the applicable conditions

of service and the advice of the Hon. Attorney-General dated 06th December 2024, marked

R1.

In calculating the amount payable, the Respondents shall distinguish the salary and

emoluments attached to the Petitioners’ substantive posts from duty-related allowances and

reimbursements excluded under Paragraph 31:15 of Chapter XLVIII of the Establishments

Code, insofar as that provision governs their employment.

In respect of annual salary increments, the CAA is directed to determine the increments for

which the Petitioners were eligible under the applicable service rules during the period

commencing on 01st September 2016 to the date of this judgement.

28

In making that determination, the CAA shall distinguish between the payment of arrears of

increments during interdiction and the recognition of eligible increments for the purpose of

determining the appropriate salary step upon reinstatement.

Any increments for which the Petitioners were lawfully eligible shall be recognised in fixing

their salaries, and any consequential arrears arising from an incorrect salary placement

following reinstatement shall be calculated and paid in accordance with law.

The prayer seeking a direction prohibiting PAYE tax deductions is refused. Any payments

made pursuant to this judgement shall remain subject to the applicable tax laws.

The Respondents shall take the necessary steps to give effect to the foregoing directions

within three months from the date of this judgement.

Considering all the circumstances of this application, I make no Order as to costs.
