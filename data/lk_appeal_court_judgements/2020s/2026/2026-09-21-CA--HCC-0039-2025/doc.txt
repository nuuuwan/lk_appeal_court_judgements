Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment dated 04.01.2024, delivered by the Learned High

Court Judge of Colombo. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as “the

Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Colombo in case No. HC 592/2018

on the following counts:

a. On or about 28.07.2016, within the jurisdiction of the High Court of Colombo,

the Accused was found in possession of 8.88 grams of Heroin without the

authority or a license as permitted by the Director in terms of Chapter 5 of the

Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance as amended by Act No. 41 of

2022, an offence punishable under Section 54A (d) of the said Ordinance;

b. At the same time, during the same transaction, the Accused, by trafficking 8.88

grams of Heroin, committed an offence punishable under Section 54A (b) of the

Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance as amended by Act No. 41 of

2022.

2. At the trial, two witnesses testified for the prosecution. Since both parties admitted the

Government Analyst’s Report under Section 420 of the Code of Criminal Procedure

Act, it was marked as P10 in evidence through the High Court Interpreter. The

Appellant subsequently made a dock statement.

3. At the conclusion of the trial, the learned Trial Judge found the Appellant guilty of both

counts. The Appellant was accordingly convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment.

Aggrieved by the said conviction and sentence, the Appellant has preferred the present

appeal on the following grounds;

a. The learned High Court Judge had failed to take into consideration the serious

infirmities of the prosecution case; and

b. The learned High Court Judge had failed to evaluate the dock statement and

adduce reasons for his conclusion to reject the dock statement.

4. The first ground of appeal advanced by the Appellant was that the learned Trial Judge

failed to take into consideration the serious infirmities of the prosecution case. I shall

now consider whether such a contention is borne out by the judgment, in light of the

evidence adduced by the prosecution.

5. PW1, Wimalasena Lionel, was the first witness to testify on behalf of the prosecution.

At the material time, he was serving as an Inspector of Police attached to the Police

Narcotics Bureau. According to his evidence, at approximately 14.45 hours, he received

information from a private informant that a person known as ‘Niyas,’ residing at

Kajeema Watta, Ferguson Road, was engaged in the trafficking of Heroin and would be

proceeding towards the vicinity of the Weligoda Mosque carrying Heroin.

6. PW1 testified that, upon receipt of this information, he informed the Officer-in-Charge,

following which the officers of the Police Narcotic Bureau left the police station at

travelled in police jeep No. WPKP 5795, driven by PS Udayakumara 18314.

7. PW1 further testified that, prior to leaving the police station, he made the relevant out

entry in his notes at approximately 15.15 hours. Thereafter, they arrived at Stadium

Park at about 15.55 hours and met the informant at about 16.20 hours. According to

18. These unexplained omissions and inconsistencies in the evidence of PW1 and PW4

raise serious doubts regarding the integrity of the investigation. The cumulative effect

of the aforementioned contradictions in the evidence of PW1 and PW4 substantially

undermines the credibility of the prosecution case. Their inconsistent statements

regarding the place at which they met the informant, and the time of the Appellant’s

arrest, create serious doubt as to the reliability of the prosecution version.

19. These are not minor discrepancies but go to the root of the prosecution’s case,

particularly in relation to the time and place of the arrest. In a case of this nature, where

the prosecution’s case rests predominantly on the evidence of police officers, it is

imperative that such evidence be clear, consistent, and free from material

contradictions. The failure to meet this standard renders it unsafe to place reliance on

the testimonies of PW1 and PW4, thereby entitling the Appellant to the benefit of a

reasonable doubt.

20. Owing to the contradictory nature of the prosecution evidence, a reasonable doubt arises

regarding the time and place of the arrest, and the manner in which the raid was

allegedly conducted. Moreover, these inconsistencies directly undermine the credibility

of PW1 and PW4, as the discrepancies in their evidence have not been sufficiently

explained and are difficult to justify. The following authorities illustrate the

consequences of the prosecution’s failure to establish the charges beyond a reasonable

doubt.

21. In Kalinga Premathilake vs. The Director General of the Commission to Investigate

Allegations of Bribery or Corruption, SC Appeal No. 99/2007, decided on 30-07-2009,

it was held,

“What needs consideration now is when the evidence led for the prosecution in this

case is closely scrutinized, whether it would be satisfied that the prosecution had

discharged the burden of proving the case beyond a reasonable doubt. If not, the

appellant is liable to be acquitted of the charges. The prosecution must stand or fall

on its own legs, and it cannot derive any strength from the weaknesses in the

defence, and when the guilt of the accused is not established beyond a reasonable

doubt, he is liable to be acquitted as a matter of right and not as a matter of grace

or favour.”

22. In Narendra Kumar vs. State (NCT of Delhi), AIR 2012 SC 2281, the Indian Supreme

Court held:

“Prosecution case has to stand on its own legs and cannot take support from the

weaknesses of the case of defence. However great the suspicion against the accused

and however strong the moral belief and conviction of the Court, unless the offence

of the accused is established beyond a reasonable doubt on the basis of legal

evidence and material on the record, he cannot be convicted for an offence. There

is an initial presumption of innocence of the accused, and the prosecution has to

bring home the offence against the accused by reliable evidence. The accused is

entitled to the benefit of every reasonable doubt.

23. It is also desirable to emphasize that the officers, when conducting a raid of this nature,

are required to make notes on every important event and are also entitled to peruse their

notes in order to refresh their memory when testifying. Hence, where material

contradictions are present in their testimony, the Court cannot lightly disregard such

inconsistencies and proceed to convict the Appellant.

24. Unfortunately, the aforesaid infirmities, together with the material inconsistencies in

the prosecution evidence, appear to have escaped the attention of the learned Trial

Judge.
