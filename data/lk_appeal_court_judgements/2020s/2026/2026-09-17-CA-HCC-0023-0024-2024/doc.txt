7. One of the grounds advanced on behalf of the 1st Appellant is that the impugned

judgment does not comply with the requirements of Section 283 of the Code of

Criminal Procedure Act. In this regard, it was contended that, in the absence of a

conviction on Count 2, the 1st Appellant could not properly have been convicted on

8. The impugned judgment largely consists of a narration and reproduction of the

evidence, without proper analysis of the evidentiary material or a reasoned assessment

of the findings arrived at. Such failure to evaluate the evidence and provide reasons in

support of the conclusions reached is not in conformity with the requirements of Section

283 of the Code of Criminal Procedure Act.

9. According to the prosecutrix, the 1st Appellant had sexual intercourse with her for the

first time at a place near her school. On the second occasion, the 1st Appellant took her

to a house in Weerawila, where he had sexual intercourse with her.

10. It is significant to note that the learned High Court Judge acquitted the 1st Appellant of

Counts 1 and 2. Count 2 charged the Appellant with kidnapping the prosecutrix from

the lawful guardianship of her guardian, an offence alleged to have been committed on

or about 10.01.2012. Count 3 was a charge of rape arising from the alleged kidnapping

referred to in Count 2, namely, that the Appellant committed rape upon the prosecutrix

after kidnapping her from lawful guardianship in the course of the same transaction.

11. The learned Trial Judge acquitted the 1st Appellant of Counts 1 and 2 for want of

sufficient evidence.

12. Nevertheless, the learned Trial Judge expressly held that there was sufficient evidence

to establish that the prosecutrix had been raped at Weerawila. In the circumstances, a

question arises as to how the 1st Appellant could properly have been convicted on Count

3 when the underlying allegation of kidnapping from lawful guardianship, which

formed an essential part of Count 3, had not been established. If there was insufficient

evidence to sustain Count 2, on what basis could the learned Trial Judge conclude that

the rape charged in Count 3 had been committed after the prosecutrix had been

kidnapped from lawful guardianship?

13. These findings appear to be inconsistent. If there was insufficient evidence to establish

the charge of kidnapping, it is difficult to understand how the learned Trial Judge could

have concluded that the prosecutrix was raped at Weerawila, the very place to which,

according to Count 2, she was alleged to have been kidnapped.

14. To elaborate further, the charge set out in Count No. 3 is that the prosecutrix was raped

at Weerawila following the alleged kidnapping referred to in Count No. 2. If the learned

Trial Judge found that there was insufficient evidence to establish that the prosecutrix

had been kidnapped by the Appellant as alleged in Count No. 2, then, in my view, the

finding on Count No. 3 also cannot be sustained, as that count specifically alleges that

the rape was committed at Weerawila following the said kidnapping.

15. The inconsistent nature of the testimony of the prosecutrix is further evident from her

to acquit the Appellant on the first and second counts, while convicting him on the third

count, notwithstanding that all three counts arose from the same transaction. Such

inconsistency in the reasoning process clearly demonstrates the lack of a proper analysis

of the evidence and the irrational conclusions reached by the learned High Court Judge.

19. Furthermore, the charge against the 2nd Appellant was that she aided and abetted the 1st

Appellant in committing the offence set out in the 4th count, namely, the rape of the

prosecutrix on 16.01.2011. However, a careful examination of the testimony of the

prosecutrix reveals that she had not seen the 2nd Appellant prior to the day on which the

school authorities contacted her parents at her residence and informed them that she

the first occasion on which she had spoken to the 2nd Appellant and that she had not

22. What is discernible from the evidence of the prosecutrix is that the 2nd Appellant had

no involvement in facilitating or permitting the 1st Appellant to take the prosecutrix to

the residence of the 2nd Appellant. Had the 2nd Appellant aided and abetted the 1st

expected that the prosecutrix would have seen or encountered the 2nd Appellant prior to

the day on which the school authorities contacted her parents at her residence and

informed them that she had not attended school. However, the prosecutrix categorically

stated that this was the first occasion on which she had spoken to the 2nd Appellant. She

further stated that she had not seen the 2nd Appellant at the residence to which the 1st

Appellant had taken her and where he had sexual intercourse with her.

23. At the conclusion of the prosecution case, the 2nd Appellant made a dock statement. In

her statement, she stated that she had met the prosecutrix on a particular day when the

Thereafter, the 2nd Appellant telephoned a teacher named Piyawathi, whom she knew

24. It is significant to note that the account given by the 2nd Appellant in her dock statement

is substantially corroborated by the evidence of the prosecutrix. The prosecutrix, in

response to questions during cross-examination, also stated that she had not met or seen

the 2nd Appellant prior to the date on which the school authorities discovered that she

was absent from school and accordingly contacted her parents.

25. The evidence of the prosecutrix, when considered together with the dock statement of

the 2nd Appellant, clearly establishes that the 2nd Appellant did not aid or abet the 1st

Appellant in the commission of the alleged offence. Unfortunately, the learned Trial

Judge failed to properly evaluate the dock statement of the 2nd Appellant and, more

importantly, failed to appreciate its evidentiary value in its proper perspective.

26. When the prosecutrix’s account regarding the involvement of the 2nd Appellant, as

given in her evidence-in-chief, is contradicted by her own evidence during cross-

examination, it would be unsafe to sustain the conviction of the 2nd Appellant.

Regrettably, the learned Trial Judge failed to appreciate this material discrepancy in the

prosecutrix’s testimony when evaluating the evidence against the 2nd Appellant. Hence,

it is my considered view that the evidence adduced by the prosecution is insufficient to

sustain the conviction of the 2nd Appellant.

29. Referring to page 148 of the appeal brief, learned Counsel for the 1st Appellant

30. The credibility of PW1’s evidence was further challenged on behalf of the 1st Appellant

on the basis that, although she testified that she had been raped on three occasions, the

history she provided to the doctor referred to only two incidents.

31. According to the prosecutrix, on the first occasion, the 1st Appellant had sexual

intercourse with her for the first time at a place near her school. On the second occasion,

the 1st Appellant took her to a house in Weerawila, where he had sexual intercourse with

her. On the third occasion, which occurred on the day preceding the day on which the

32. However, the history given by the prosecutrix to the Judicial Medical Officer (JMO) is

inconsistent with her evidence-in-chief. According to the history she gave to the JMO,

the 1st Appellant had intercourse with her for the first time at a house in Weerawila on

10.01.2012. She further stated that, on the second occasion, the 1st Appellant had

intercourse with her at a house behind the school.

33. Thus, it is further observed that, in the history given to the JMO, the prosecutrix

mentioned only two instances of sexual intercourse by the 1st Appellant, as opposed to

her evidence-in-chief, where she stated that the 1st Appellant had intercourse with her

on three separate days.

34. It must be emphasized that a Court may disregard trivial contradictions and

inconsistencies, particularly where the prosecutrix has testified several years after the

10 | P a g e

alleged incident. However, where such infirmities relate to principal events and material

facts, they cannot be treated as mere discrepancies arising from the passage of time.

Such inconsistencies would undoubtedly undermine the credibility and reliability of her

testimony and, consequently, render it unsafe to base a conviction of the accused solely

upon such evidence.

35. In assessing the nature and significance of contradictions and omissions, the following

authorities provide valuable guidance.

36. In The Attorney General vs. Sandanam Pitchai Mary Theresa [2011] 2 SLR 292, the

court held that:

“Whilst internal contradictions or discrepancies would ordinarily affect the

trustworthiness of the witness statement, it is well established that the Court must

exercise its judgment on the nature of the inconsistency or contradiction and

whether they are true material to the facts in issue.”

37. In the case of Mohamed Niyas Naufer and others vs. Attorney General (SC. 01/2006

decided on 08/12/2006), the Court observed that;

“When faced with contradictions in a witness's testimonial, the Court must bear in

mind the nature and significance of the contradictions, viewed in light of the whole

of the evidence given by the witness."

38. In State of Rajasthan vs. Smt. Kalki & Anr., AIR 1981 SC 1390, while dealing with

this issue, the Supreme Court observed as under:

"In the depositions of witnesses, there are always normal discrepancies, however

honest and truthful they may be. These discrepancies are due to normal errors of

observation, normal errors of memory due to lapse of time, due to mental

disposition such as shock and horror at the time of the occurrence, and the like.

Material discrepancies are those which are not normal, and not expected of a

normal person."

39. Evidently, the Judicial Medical Officer who examined the prosecutrix obtained a

vaginal swab, which was thereafter sent to GeneTech for DNA analysis. Similarly, a

blood sample obtained from the 1st Appellant was also sent for DNA analysis.

Following the analysis, GeneTech submitted a report to the Court. However, the

prosecution did not seek to lead the said DNA report in evidence at the trial.

40. During the pendency of the appeal, an application was made on behalf of the 1st

Appellant under Section 329 of the Code of Criminal Procedure Act, seeking

permission to adduce the said report in evidence. This Court allowed the application.

Accordingly, the scientist who conducted the DNA profiling of the samples obtained

from the prosecutrix and the 1st Appellant testified before the High Court, and the said

report was marked in evidence as ‘X’.

41. According to the findings set out in Report ‘X’1 issued by GeneTech, the two vaginal

smears contained DNA of a female human individual, and no detectable amount of

DNA belonging to the 1st Appellant was found in either of the two vaginal smears. Most

significantly, the DNA profiles obtained from P3-VSM1 and P3-VSM2 did not match

the DNA profile of the 1st Appellant. This indicates that the DNA contained in the two

vaginal smears marked P3 did not originate from the 1st Appellant.

42. The significance of these findings has to be considered in the backdrop of the evidence

of the prosecutrix, the JMO, and PW11, Kadiragamanadan Mohan, who conducted the

DNA analysis of the specimens obtained from the prosecutrix and the 1st Appellant. In

her testimony, the prosecutrix stated that the 1st Appellant ejaculated during sexual

intercourse and that she observed a whitish substance on her genitals at that time.

According to the JMO, the prosecutrix was examined by him within 48 hours of the

alleged incident, and it was he who obtained the vaginal smears from her. The JMO

further opined that spermatozoa could remain alive in the vagina for up to 48 hours

following sexual intercourse.

43. Thus, having regard to the period that elapsed between the alleged incident and the

examination of the prosecutrix by the JMO, there was a significant possibility of

detecting spermatozoa in her vagina. PW11, in his testimony, stated that spermatozoa

could remain alive in the vagina for up to 72 hours following sexual intercourse.

44. Nevertheless, the DNA analysis did not yield any evidence corroborating the

prosecution’s allegation that it was the 1st Appellant who had sexual intercourse with

the prosecutrix. The most that can be inferred from the expert evidence is that the

prosecutrix had sexual intercourse with a person recently prior to her examination by

the JMO. However, the DNA evidence does not establish that the 1st Appellant was that

person.

45. It is true that, as evidenced by the expert testimony, the possibility of detecting

spermatozoa or DNA of a perpetrator in a given sample may depend upon various

factors. Nevertheless, the fact that no DNA of the 1st Appellant was detected in the

vaginal smears of the prosecutrix assumes significance when considered in the light of

the manner in which she described the alleged sexual intercourse with the 1st Appellant

and the relatively short period that elapsed before she was subjected to judicial medical

examination.

46. Further, although it was elicited that exposure of vaginal smears to high temperatures

may affect the detection of spermatozoa or the perpetrator’s DNA, the same samples

were found to contain DNA attributable to a female human individual, while no DNA

of the 1st Appellant was detected. If the absence of the 1st Appellant’s DNA is attributed

to improper storage or exposure to high temperature, it is pertinent to consider how the

female DNA remained detectable in the same samples. In these circumstances, the

absence of DNA belonging to the 1st Appellant in the vaginal smears materially

undermines the credibility of her testimony that it was the 1st Appellant who had sexual

intercourse with her.

47. In these circumstances, where the scientific evidence does not support the prosecution

version and, instead, raises a reasonable doubt as to the identity of the person who had

sexual intercourse with the prosecutrix, such doubt must necessarily inure to the benefit

of the 1st Appellant.

48. It is a fundamental principle of criminal law that the prosecution must prove its case

beyond reasonable doubt on the strength of its own evidence and not on the weakness

of the defence. Accordingly, even if the defence version is rejected in its entirety, where

the identity of the Appellant as the perpetrator remains doubtful, the Appellant is

entitled to the benefit of that doubt; no conviction can safely be sustained.

49. Having considered the aforementioned facts and circumstances of the present case, I

am unable to agree with the findings of the learned Trial Judge.

50. For the foregoing reasons, the convictions and sentences imposed on the Appellants by

the learned High Court Judge of Hambantota on 18.10.2023 are hereby set aside, and

the Appellants are acquitted of the charges. Accordingly, the appeal is allowed.
