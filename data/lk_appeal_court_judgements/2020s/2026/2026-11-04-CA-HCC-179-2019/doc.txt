JUDGMENT

P. Kumararatnam, J.

The above-named Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as the

Appellant) along with another person, who was named as the 2nd Accused

were indicted in the High Court of Kandy under Section 296 read with

Section 32 of the Penal Code for committing the murder of Hapugoda

Maithripala on or about 06th November 1999.

In the course of the same transaction, the Appellant was also indicted for

voluntarily causing hurt on Devate Gedera Alahakoon, which is an offence

punishable under Section 315 of the Penal Code.

Trial commenced before the High Court Judge of Kandy as the Appellant and

the other Accused had opted for a non-jury trial. After the conclusion of the

prosecution’s case, the Learned High Court Judge had called for the defence

and the Appellant had made a statement from the dock and the 2nd Accused

had given evidence from the witness box.

After considering the evidence presented by both parties, the Learned High

Court Judge had convicted the Appellant as charged and sentenced him to

death on the first count. For the second count, the Appellant was sentenced

to 03 years rigorous imprisonment. A fine of Rs.10,000/- subject to a default

sentence of 6 months rigorous imprisonment, along with a compensation of

Rs.50,000/- was also imposed on the Appellant.

The 2nd Accused was acquitted from the murder charge.

Being aggrieved by the aforesaid conviction and sentence, the Appellant

preferred this appeal to this court.

The Learned Counsel for the Appellant informed this court that the Appellant

has given consent to argue this matter in his absence. At the time of the

argument, he was connected via the zoom platform from prison.

The learned President’s Counsel confining to one ground of appeal contended

that the evidence of a sole eye witness cannot be believed and his testimonial

trustworthiness cannot be relied upon to allow the conviction to stand.

Background of the case

According to PW1, Alahakoon, the incident had happened on the road which

is leading to the residence of a person called Lokku Bappa at about 8.00pm

on the day of the incident. He had met the deceased on the way to attend the

funeral of Punchi Menika of the village.

At that time, they had encountered the Appellant and the 2nd Accused who

was acquitted by the High Court Judge. The Appellant had possessed a knife

generally used to tap toddy from Kithul trees and the 2nd Accused had

possessed a club. PW1 had identified them very clearly with the light

emanating from the residences of A. G. Banda and Lokku Bappa.

As the Appellant and the 2nd Accused had assaulted the father of PW1 on the

previous day, PW1 had inquired from them why they assaulted his father. At

that time, the Appellant had slapped PW1 and the 2nd Accused had assaulted

the deceased on his head with the club he had in his possession. As a result,

the deceased had fallen on the ground and the Appellant had cut the

deceased by using the knife which was in his possession. Due to fear, PW1

had run towards his grandmother’s house however, he had fallen down due

to the slipperiness of the floor as a result of rain. Using this opportunity, the

Appellant had inflicted cut injuries to PW1’s left arm and stomach, using the

knife which he was carrying.

PW6, ASP/Amarasinghe who had conducted the investigation, had visited

the scene and had recovered a knife first. Afterwards, he had gone in search

of the Appellant and recovered a knife from his house, which was not a

recovery under Section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance.

According to PW11 Dr. Upendra Sirisena, the JMO who held the post

mortem, opined that the death was due to cardio respiratory failure due to

shock and haemorrhage due to compound deformed fracture of left temporal

bone and damage to the brain over temporal area and also haemorrhage due

to damage to left temporal carotid artery due to a heavy blow. The doctor

suggested that the death may have occurred almost instantaneously.

The JMO has noted one cut injury on the stomach area of PW1. He opined

that this injury could have been caused by a sharp weapon similar to the

knife marked P2.

It was further noted that the cut injuries found on the body of the deceased

would have been inflicted when the assailant was facing the deceased, while

the blow on the head of the deceased could have been dealt to the deceased

while he was upright or on the ground.

After the closure of the prosecution’s case, the defence was called and the

Appellant opted to make a statement from the dock. The second Accused

gave evidence from witness box.

The Learned High Court Judge, in his judgement convicted the Appellant as

charged and sentenced him to death on count number one and imposed

three years rigorous imprisonment on count two with a fine of Rs.10,000/-

subject to a default sentence of 06 months rigorous imprisonment.

Additionally, the Appellant was ordered to pay Rs.50,000/- as compensation

to PW1 which was subject to a default sentence of 06 months rigorous

imprisonment.

The learned Counsel for the Appellant contends that the conviction is wholly

unsafe in view of the uncorroborated and unsatisfactory nature of the

evidence of the sole eye witness along with the other respective witnesses,

led by the prosecution.

The learned President’s Counsel for the Appellant argues that PW01’s

evidence is inconsistent with the medical evidence and clearly contradicts

the position of the prosecution. In this case, PW01 is an eye witness. It was

the position of the prosecution that he had clearly seen the incident without

any disruption on that day.

In the case of Sumanasena v. The Attorney General [1999] 3 Sri.L.R 137

held that;

“Evidence must not be counted but weighed and the evidence of a single

solitary witness if cogent and impressive could be acted upon by a Court

of Law”

In the case of Chacko Alias Aniyan Kunju & others v. State of Kerala-

[2004] INSC 87 (21st January 2004) held that:

“If the evidence is unblemished and beyond all possible criticism and the

Court is satisfied that the witness was speaking the truth then on his

evidence alone conviction can be maintained”.

CA/HCC/179/2019

6 | P a g e

In the case of Madduma Siripala and another v. The Attorney General

CA/125-126/10 decided on 27/10/2017, Justice Thurairaja held that:

“With reference to the above-mentioned section, there is no requisite

number of witnesses needed to be called to prove a fact. In fact, the

evidence of a single witness is sufficient to prove a fact provided the

evidence of the witness is uncontradicted, truthful, independent and

reliable to the court”.

With reference to the above cited judicial decisions, it is abundantly clear

that the trial court can act on the evidence of a single witness whose evidence

is truthful and impressive, to come to a correct finding.

The learned President’s Counsel for the Appellant, referring to the evidence

given by the witnesses called by the prosecution, further argues that the

evidence given by PW1 is highly tainted with ambiguity and uncertainty

which seriously compromises the culpability of the Appellant in this case.

PW1, in his evidence categorically stated that the fatal blow on the head of

the deceased was dealt by the second Accused, with a wooden club that he

was carrying at that time. For clarity, the relevant portions of the evidence

In the case of the Queen v Julis 65 NLR 585 the court held that:

“that, by falsely implicating the 1st accused, the two witnesses gave false

evidence on a material point. Applying the maxim falsus in uno, falsus in

omnibus (He who speaks falsely on one point will speak falsely upon all),

their evidence implicating the 4th and 5th accused should also be rejected.

When such evidence is given by witnesses, the question whether other

portions of their evidence can be accepted as true should not be resolved

in their favour unless there is some compelling reason for doing so”.

Though, PW1 said in the examination in chief that the Appellant had cut the

deceased using a knife generally used for tapping Kittul toddy, he could not

identify the two knives recovered during the investigation by the police.

In all criminal trials, the burden of proof is always on the shoulder of the

prosecution. Accordingly, the prosecution will always bear the weight of

proving the guilt of the Accused, beyond a reasonable doubt.

In the case of Mohamed Nimnaz V. Attorney General CA/95/94 held:

“A criminal case has to be proved beyond reasonable doubt. Although we

take serious view in regard to offences in relation to drugs, we are of the

view that the prosecutor should not be given a second chance to fill the

gaps of badly handled prosecutions….”

In Attorney-General v. Rawther 25 NLR 385, Ennis, J. states thus: [1987}

1 SLR 155

"The evidence must establish the guilt of the accused, not his innocence.

His innocence is presumed in law, from the start of the case, and his guilt

must be established beyond a reasonable doubt”.

CA/HCC/179/2019

9 | P a g e

In Miller v. Minister of Pensions (1947) 2 All E.R. 372 the court held that:

“the evidence must reach the same degree of cogency as is required in a

criminal case before an accused person is found guilty. That degree is well

settled. It need not reach certainty, but it must carry high degree of

probability. Proof beyond reasonable doubt does not mean proof beyond

the shadow of a doubt. The law would fail to protect the community if it

admitted fanciful possibilities to deflect the course of justice. If the evidence

is so strong against a man as to leave only a remote possibility in his favour

which can be dismissed with the sentence, “of course it is possible, but not

in the least probable,” the case is proved beyond reasonable doubt, but

nothing short of that will suffice”.

After the conclusion of the submissions made by the learned President’s

Counsel for the Appellant, the learned Senior Deputy Solicitor General

highlighting the dock statement of the Appellant, contended that this is a fit

and proper case to bring down the charge to culpable homicide not

amounting to murder as the Appellant had admitted that he also dealt a blow

with a door pole on the deceased as the deceased had tried to assault him at

that time.

I am unable to agree with the learned SDSG as the eye witness had very

clearly said that the second Accused was the person who dealt the fatal blow

on the deceased’s head. The JMO who held the post mortem of the deceased

opined that the death had been caused due to the fatal blow on the head.

Therefore, the above-mentioned inconsistencies have created a doubt

regarding the guilt and culpability of the Appellant in this case. Hence, I

conclude that the benefit of doubt should be awarded to the Appellant in this

case. The Appellant has been in remand since the date of judgment, i.e., from
