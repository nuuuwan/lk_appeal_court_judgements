Judgement

Introduction

The Petitioner was acting as the Principal of Bataduwa Sri Rathnajothi Nawodya Vidyalaya,

Bataduwa, Galle, at the time of his interdiction in October 2018. The said interdiction had taken

place pursuant to a letter sent by the Auditor General's Department to the 1st Respondent. The

Petitioner alleges that no explanation was called for, from him prior to the service of the letter

of interdiction (P11); in addition to that, no statement was recorded prior to the interdiction.

However, after interdiction, it took more than twenty-three months to start the preliminary

investigation against the Petitioner. Thereafter, the Petitioner was charge-sheeted after the

preliminary investigation, and after being charge-sheeted, it also took several months to

conclude the inquiry, where the Petitioner was found guilty at the end of the inquiry. However,

the matter proceeded ex parte, in the absence of the Petitioner and his representative, despite

the serious objections raised by the Petitioner against the 14th Respondent on the basis of bias.

In addition to that, the Petitioner was not paid his monthly salary since his interdiction.

In those circumstances, the Petitioner challenges the letter of interdiction P11, the preliminary

investigation report (P20), the charge sheet (P21), the amended charge sheet (P25), the

proceedings of the inquiry, the final report containing the said decision (P34(ee)), and the final

disciplinary order (P34(gg)) by this Application.

The 1st Respondent is the Director of the Southern Province Department of Education, the 2nd

Respondent is the Zonal Director, and the 3rd Respondent is the Deputy Director of Education

(Administration). The 4th Respondent is the Secretary of the Ministry of Education, while the

5th Respondent is the Chairman of the Southern Province Public Service Commission, where

the 6th to 11th Respondents are its members and the 12th Respondent is its secretary. The 13th

7

Respondent is the Governor of the Southern Province. The 14th and 15th Respondents are the

Inquiring Officer appointed by the Southern Province Public Service Commission to conduct

the disciplinary inquiry and the officer representing the Petitioner at the disciplinary inquiry,

respectively.

After issuance of formal notice on Support, the Respondents have filed their respective

Objections and denied the allegations made by the Petitioner in the Petition and sought the

dismissal of this Application.

Thereafter, this was argued before my brother Justice Morais and myself on 06.07.2026; hence,

this judgement.

The following arguments were advanced at the hearing by the counsel, and in addition to that,

the Counsel for the Petitioner has filed her Written Submissions, further elaborating the oral

submissions advanced at the hearing.

Arguments

Ms. Meepagala, the Counsel for and on behalf of the Petitioner attacks the letter of interdiction

marked as P11 on the basis that it is ultra vires as it is authored by a subordinate officer, being

the Director, who does not have any authority to take any disciplinary action against the

Petitioner. Thus, the letter of interdiction itself is ultra vires. In addition to that, the said

interdiction was effected without any payment of salary or half-salary. Therefore, it is argued

for and on behalf of the Petitioner that the letter of interdiction is, on the face of the record,

ultra vires and irrational.

Secondly, Ms. Meepagala attacks the conduct of the preliminary investigation, and since during

the course of the preliminary investigations, it was believed that the accountant was also

responsible for the misappropriation of money, and settled by him, this directs that the total

8

responsibility cannot be levelled against the Petitioner. In addition to that, it is attacked on

delay as well, since it took several years to conclude the preliminary investigations.

Furthermore, the Petitioner also attacks the manner in which the final inquiry was conducted,

particularly in his absence, and the conclusion arrived at by the Inquirer.

Therefore, on all three grounds, the Petitioner attacks the entire process of the disciplinary

inquiry and sought writs accordingly.

On the other hand, the learned State Counsel, Ms. Jayasekara, argues on behalf of the

Respondents that the Petitioner has already admitted that he is guilty by writing the document

marked as 1R1 to the 1st Respondent and agreeing to pay the relevant amount in instalments;

therefore, she argues that this itself shows the Petitioner as guilty.

Secondly, she argues that the Petitioner has not cooperated right throughout, to conduct the

disciplinary inquiry by constantly obtaining dates and postponements instead of diligently

attending them; thereby, caused the delay of the inquiry. Therefore, he cannot complain against

the delay.

Thirdly, she argues that in any case, the Petitioner's guilt is proven, and therefore, the Petitioner

is not entitled to obtain any discretionary remedy from this Court.

The Petitioner's position

The Petitioner asserts that he is a married person having three children; to establish the same

the Petitioner has marked and annexed his Marriage Certificate as P1(a) and the birth

certificates of his children as P1(b) to P1(d), respectively.

Further, he asserts that he was appointed as a primary teacher by the letter dated 01.08.1992,

which is marked as P2(a) annexed to the Petition. Thereafter, in 2012, he was promoted to the

Principal's Grade Class 3 by letter dated 14.09.2012, which is marked as P2(b) annexed to the

9

has also annexed the document marked as P2(c). Further, he asserts that by the time he was

working as a teacher at St. Aloysius' College, Galle, he was placed in the Principal Grade Class

3 – Principal Service Officer, and this is reflected in the log entry of the principals, made by

the Principal, marked and annexed as P2(d).

However, later, he was absorbed into the Provincial Public Service, as established in P3, and

by P4(a), he was appointed as the Deputy Principal of Sulaimaniya Central College; thereafter,

by P4(b), he was transferred to Sri Rathnajothi Nawodya School, Bataduwa, Galle as a Deputy

Principal.

While he was serving as a principal, he had completed the three-year Teacher Training Course

in Primary Education at the National Institute of Education, as well as obtained a Bachelor's

Degree in Education. Further, he has obtained several other qualifications, including a

leadership qualification in a principals training course as reflected in P5(f), and General

Information Technology (Part One) Examination as reflected in P5(e). In addition to that, he

is involved in extra-curricular activities, including scouts as reflected in P5(g).

His services were made permanent by P6(a). There are other certificates given to him for his

service which is marked as P6(b) to P6(d). His service record is also marked as P6(e) to P6(i).

In the meantime, he had obtained sabbatical leave for further studies which is reflected in P8(a).

However, there had been two letters, marked as P8(b) and P8(c), directing him to hand over

the responsibilities as principal of Sri Rathnajothi Nawodya Vidyalaya to the successor during

his sabbatical leave.

In the meantime, the Petitioner alleges that there had been an audit inquiry, and an audit query

had been forwarded to the Department of Education by the Auditor General's Department,

which is marked as P10, where it refers to certain embezzlement of money from the bank

10

account maintained by the School Development Society. Based on the said report, the

Petitioner's service had been interdicted by P11, and later confirmed by the Provincial

Education Secretary by P12. Further, the Petitioner had been asked to participate in the

investigation, as reflected in the documents marked as P13(a) and P13(b).

The Petitioner further annexed to the Petition, certain circulars and amendments to the said

circulars, where it refers to matters relating to disciplinary inquiries conducted expeditiously

Thereafter, the Petitioner claims that one Mr. Welarathna, had been appointed as a member of

Two of such letters have been marked as P16(a) and P16(b). However, it is his position that

no preliminary investigation had been conducted, or charge sheet had been framed against him,

nor had there been any preliminary inquiry carried out by the relevant parties up until 2021,

which is more than twenty-three months.

Thereafter, there had been a preliminary inquiry; the report of the said preliminary inquiry is

marked as P20, by which it was recommended to charge sheet the Petitioner. Accordingly, the

Petitioner had been charge-sheeted, and the Petitioner alleges that after several days of inquiry,

and sometimes even without the Petitioner's presence or representation, the Panel of Inquiry

had inquired into the allegations levelled against the Petitioner, and thereafter, reported against

him by the report marked as P20.

Thus, the Petitioner challenges the letter of interdiction (P11), the report of the preliminary

him on the basis of violation of the rules of natural justice, as well as not following the

Establishment Code and Rules and Regulations reflected in the circulars, particularly relating

11

to the timeframe within which an inquiry against an interdicted person has to be concluded.

Accordingly, the Petitioner challenges the said documents and findings by this Application.

Objections

The 1st Respondent has filed separate Statement of Objections while the 4th Respondent had

filed a separate Statement of Objections, while the 5th to 12th Respondents have filed a joint

Statement of Objections.

All the Respondents take up the position that inter alia the Petitioner is guilty of laches, not

cited the necessary parties before this Court, and since the Petitioner is seeking a discretionary

remedy, the Petitioner has not come with clean hands before Court, nor invoked available

alternative remedies; additionally, since there are disputed facts asserted and alleged by the

respective parties, the Petitioner cannot have and maintain this Application.

In addition to that, the 1st Respondent has taken up the position that the Petitioner had admitted

as guilty by the letter marked as 1R1 and sought permission to settle the due amount in

instalments; therefore, sought the dismissal of this Application.

The 4th Respondent has annexed the document 4R1, by which the Petitioner's sabbatical leave

had been cancelled and directed him to report to work due to the fact that there was an

embezzlement of money from the funds of the Development Society. In addition to that, the

proceedings and charge sheet are annexed to the Statement of Objections marked as 4R2 and

4R3, respectively. The document marked as 4R4 annexed to the Objections is the interim report

on the preliminary inquiry.

On the other hand, the 5th to 12th Respondents have also filed certain documents along with

their Statement of Objections, which are marked as 12R1 to 12R25. I will refer to them in the

course of my judgement, when necessary, as they mostly contain documents on certain

12

correspondence between the parties, the relevant circulars, and certain proceedings of the

inquiry.

In view of the matters pleaded in the Petition, objections pleaded in the respective Statement

of Objections, Counter Affidavits, as well as the arguments advanced orally and in writing, it

is my view that the following questions arise for my consideration;

(i) Whether the disciplinary action initiated by interdicting the Petitioner by P11 is ultra

vires and unreasonable;

(ii) Whether the delay of the preliminary investigations vitiates the preliminary

investigations and charge sheet based thereupon;

(iii) Whether the disciplinary inquiry conducted against the Petitioner after charge-sheeting

him was conducted properly, or whether there was a violation of the rules of natural

justice in conducting the same;

(iv) If the above is answered in the affirmative, whether the final conclusion arrived to is

vitiated;

(v) Whether finding the Petitioner guilty in any case is justified.

Now I will consider each issue segment-by-segment.

Letter of interdiction

On a perusal of the document P11, it shows that the disciplinary authority has not taken the

decision contained therein; it was by the 1st Respondent, the Zonal Director, who is not the

disciplinary authority. Therefore, P11 itself had to be ratified, which the Southern Provincial

Public Service Commission had done by P12.

In addition to that, the Petitioner has been placed after interdiction without any salary, which

also in itself is unreasonable.

13

Despite the ratification of P11 by P12, the Public Service Commission of the Southern

Province did not make any direction as to the Petitioner's salary which is in violation of Clause

13:12 of the Establishment Code.

For further clarity, the relevant provision of the Establishment Code reads thus;

13:12. In the case of instances not falling under subsection 31:11 above the disciplinary

authority may decide either not to pay the emoluments or to pay ½ of the emoluments

in consideration of the seriousness of the charge, period prior record of service of the

officer, his financial needs etc.

However, it was argued on behalf of the Respondents that in terms of Section 31:12 of the

Establishment Code, the disciplinary authority may decide the sort of emoluments should be

paid; nevertheless, the non-payment of salary to the Petitioner in the said letter of interdiction

marked as P11 was authored not by the disciplinary authority, but by a subordinate officer.

Thus, P11 is ultra vires. In addition to that, even by P12, the disciplinary authority has not

taken a decision in terms of Clause 13:12 of the Establishment Code to pay any emoluments;

therefore, P12 is also ultra vires and irrational.

Accordingly, it is my view that a writ lies against P11 and P12, as sought by the Petitioner,

because when considering both of the said documents together, they are ultra vires and

unreasonable.

It is further noted that when P11 and P12 are considered together, P11 had been ratified by

P12 in terms of Clause 31:1 of the Establishment Code which reads thus;

31:1. Where it is disclosed, prima facie, that a public officer has committed either one

or some or all of the following acts of misconduct, the relevant disciplinary authority,

or the relevant disciplinary authority, or the relevant secretary to the ministry or Head

of Department not holding disciplinary authority, may forthwith interdict the officer

14

concerned subject to the covering approval of the Disciplinary Authority. However as

soon as the letter of interdiction is issued by the disciplinary authority should be

informed sending also a copy of such letter to the purpose of obtaining covering

approval. [Emphasis is mine]

However, it must be noted that the 1st Respondent, being the Department Head or acting in the

capacity of the Department Head, could not have interdicted the Petitioner by P11 because

there could not have been any prima facie offence supposed to have been committed by the

Petitioner without even recording a statement from the Petitioner or any other witness.

Thus, without conducting any preliminary investigations, the decision taken by the 1st

Respondent to interdict the Petitioner by P11, and later to be ratified by P12, is irrational and

ultra vires.

Delay of the Preliminary Inquiry

The next question to be decided is whether there was an inordinate delay on the part of the

investigators to complete the preliminary investigation to ascertain whether there is a

maintainable charge of allegation against the Petitioner.

After the interdiction by letter marked as P11, dated 11.10.2018 and ratified by P12, the

Petitioner's first statement was recorded on 10.09.2020, as reflected in P13(a), which is after

one year eleven months and several days later. Thereafter, the Preliminary Investigation Report

came on 14.10.2021, which is three years and five months after the interdiction. The date of

the first charge sheet is 31.03.2022, which is after three years and five months, and the amended

charge sheet is dated 02.11.2022, which is four years after the interdiction. The inquiry

commenced on 22.09.2023, which is exactly four years and eleven months from the date of the

interdiction.

15

Therefore, the Petitioner challenges the lackadaisical way in which the investigation process

was conducted by the investigating officers to frame a charge sheet and the service of the

amended charge sheet.

It must be noted that the Petitioner was interdicted with effect from 11.10.2018 by P11, and

the Respondents, as argued by the Petitioner, have not adhered to the relevant procedure or the

provisions of the Establishment Code which was also cited to us in the course of the arguments

as well as in the Written Submissions.

Clause 13:2 of the Establishment Code as it stood at the time of the interdiction of the Petitioner

by P11, ratified by P12, reads thus;

13:2. An Authority ordering a preliminary investigation into an act of misconduct

should, at the same time that such order is issued, specify the time limit within which

the officer or committee of officers of the preliminary investigation to complete the

preliminary investigation taking into consideration the nature of each act of

misconduct, as the case may be. However, where the officer conducting the preliminary

investigation adduces reasons that there are valid obstacles to completing the

investigation within the specified time limit, the relevant authority may grant an

appropriate extension of time. Nevertheless, all relevant parties should ensure that such

preliminary investigations are carried out and are completed with the least possible

delay. [Emphasis is mine]

Although it is later amended by a Public Administration Circular No. 30/2019, marked as P15,

I only rely on the original Clause 13:2 as cited above, as it stood as the Rule at the time of the

Petitioner's interdiction.

16

According to it, a time period should be specified when ordering a preliminary inquiry to

conclude the investigation or preliminary inquiry; and if there is any time extension to be made,

it must be done only after application to the Public Service Commission.

However, there is no direction of any timeframe, nor is there any evidence to show that such

application has been made to extend the time period or that such timeframe has been adhered

to by the Respondents.

The investigators have failed to adhere to the strict principles of conducting or carrying out the

investigations within a reasonable time frame. In fact, the Provincial Public Service

Commission is also responsible for not giving proper guidance to investigators to complete it

within a reasonable period.

Therefore, there is an inordinate delay, and such inordinate delay has caused tremendous

hardships to the Petitioner, who is a married man having three school-going children under his

care, and without his salary for five years; thus, the hardships so caused are grave. As such, the

Petitioner was subject to undue punishment.

In this regard, I wish to cite a proposition of law in the judgement of His Lordship Justice Mark

Fernando in the case of Jayasinghe v. Attorney General, 1 where in a very much similar

circumstances, His Lordship has held with authority as thus;

I therefore hold that there has been a denial of one important protection which the law

provided for the Petitioner, upon interdiction without pay, in respect of his livelihood:

that disciplinary proceedings, which could have resulted in depriving him of his

livelihood, should have been completed without inordinate delay. I make no attempt to

demarcate the dividing line, between permissible and unacceptable delay: howsoever

1 [1994] 2 Sri L.R. 74.

17

that boundary is demarcated, in this case it is clear - as night from day - that the delay

of 16 years which had occurred when the Petitioner came into Court was manifestly

excessive. As to the effect of that delay, it will be seen that the Petitioner when

interdicted was yet in his prime, and will have passed the normal age of retirement by

the time the validity of his dismissal is determined in a court or tribunal of first instance.

Delay has robbed him, and his family, of half his working life (at pages 87 – 88).

This long-drawn preliminary inquiry has taken place while the Petitioner was under interdiction,

despite his repeated requests to be reinstated, as borne out by the document marked as P35(a),

P35(b) and P37.

Therefore, I wish to also follow the following dictum of His Lordship Justice Prasanna

Jayawardana in the case of T.G.J.L. Amarasinghe v. Dr. N.C.D. Ariyarathna and Others,2

which reads thus;

When seeking to determine the point at which the Disciplinary Authority and the

respondents lost the right to claim the benefit of the aforesaid first exception to the

second limb of section 22:1:1 of the Code, it is my view that by 05th May 2015 at the

very latest - ie: when the Disciplinary Authority ignored the petitioner’s request that he

be reinstated in service in view of the very long delay in even commencing the

disciplinary inquiry and the very substantial prejudice caused to the petitioner by his

being kept on interdiction without pay for many years - the Disciplinary Authority and

the respondents had lost the entitlement to rely on the first exception to the second limb

of section 22:1:1 and keep the petitioner under interdiction and without pay. The

unreasonable delay on the part of the Disciplinary Authority and the respondents to

commence the disciplinary inquiry was painfully clear by then and was inexcusable,

2 SC/FR/15/2017 (SC Minutes 02.09.2019).

18

and stripped them of any right to claim that the first exception to the second limb of

section 22:1:1 entitled the Disciplinary Authority to continue to keep the petitioner

under interdiction and without pay. The fact that the Charges against the petitioner

were of misconduct involving dishonesty falling within the ambit of section 31:11 of the

Code does, where necessary and particularly where the collation and production of

comprehensive evidence at the disciplinary inquiry is time consuming, give a

Disciplinary Authority a reasonable period time beyond the usual twelve months to

conclude disciplinary proceedings. However, in the present case, the delay in even

commencing the disciplinary inquiry by 05th May 2015 was manifestly unreasonable

and unjustified (at pages 25 – 26).

Therefore, I hold that the inordinate delay in conducting the investigations and having the

preliminary inquiry to ascertain whether the Petitioner is charge-sheeted or not, have caused

tremendous hardship to the Petitioner because after the interdiction, without reinstating him or

payment of a proper salary, he has suffered a lot.

However, after the inquiry, there is an amended charge sheet served on him; therefore, I am

not going to set aside or quash the said charge sheet, solely on the grounds of delay.

Nevertheless, I wish to indicate the serious concern of the delay caused by the lackadaisical

manner in conducting the preliminary inquiry.

Such delay shocks the conscience of this Court on what sort of relief should be granted to the

Petitioner. I will discuss this matter towards the end of this judgement.

Unfairness of the disciplinary inquiry

The Petitioner's next allegation is based on bias of the Inquirer, the 14th Respondent, based on

the procedure adopted by the 14th Respondent.

19

On 24.10.2023, when the evidence of the witness, Ms. Kalupahana, was recorded and after the

contradiction was marked in the course of the cross-examination, her evidence in re-

examination was recorded in a bias manner, where she was permitted to give evidence looking

at the documents and her notes which had not been referred to. Therefore, an objection was

raised by the 15th Respondent on the manner in which it was recorded.

Thereafter, the Petitioner walked out with the 15th Respondent. Only thereafter did the

Petitioner and the 15th Respondent make a formal complaint to the Southern Provincial Public

Service Commission by letters marked as P31(a) and P31(b). Having accepted the said

complaint, the Southern Provincial Public Service Commission has commenced an inquiry into

the complaint, while allowing the 14th Respondent to proceed with the inquiry against the

Petitioner. Therefore, this itself shows that the entire inquiry procedure is tainted with bias.

Since thereafter, the Petitioner and the 15th Respondent did not participate in the inquiry, the

14th Respondent proceeded with inquiry ex parte.

Accordingly, the Petitioner and the 15th Respondent had not participated in the inquiry, namely,

on the following dates;

(a) 23.11.2023

(b) 05.12.2023

(c) 19.12.2023

(d) 09.01.2024

Thus, the Petitioner was deprived of observing whether the evidence against him was recorded

properly, and whether there is any objection to be raised on the evidence recorded on those

dates of the inquiry; in addition to that, the witnesses were never subjected to cross-

examinations during the said dates.

20

Therefore, this shows not only bias on the part of the 14th Respondent, but also a serious

violation of the rules of natural justice for having conducted the proceedings from the point the

Petitioner and 15th Respondent walked out of the inquiry proceedings up until the final

determination of the 14th Respondent against the Petitioner.

In addition to that, I wish to rely on the following passages found in the classic textbook,

"Administrative Law" by Wade and Forsyth,3 with regard to the procedure to be followed by

such panel of inquiry, in support of my conclusion on the manner in which the Inquirer (the

14th Respondent) had conducted the disciplinary inquiry against the Petitioner, in the absence

of proper cross-examination and in the absence of proper representation of the Petitioner where

oral hearings took place;

Where an oral hearing is given, it has been laid down that a tribunal must (a) consider

all relevant evidence which a party wishes to submit; (b) inform every party of all the

evidence to be taken into account, whether derived from another party or

independently; (c) allow witnesses to be questioned; (d) allow comment on the evidence

and argument on the whole case. Failure to allow the last two rights, which include the

right of cross-examination, has led to the quashing of punishments awarded by prison

visitors in a series of cases. In one, the visitors refused to allow prisoners to call

witnesses because of the administrative inconvenience of bringing them from distant

prisons to which they had been dispersed after a riot, and for other inadequate reasons.

In another, a prisoner was not allowed to question his own witness or to comment on

the evidence. In a third, there was a witness unknown to the prisoner but known to the

investigating officer, who failed to inform the visitors. This last case shows that natural

justice may be violated by the conduct of those who bring forward the accusation as

3 Wade, W., & Forsyth, C. (8th Edition, Oxford University Press 2000). Administrative Law, Part VI Chapter 15.

21

well as by that of the tribunal - as also does a later case, where a prosecution was

instigated by a detective who concealed his own criminal record. But a mistake by the

applicant's own advisers, even though it deprives him of the opportunity to be heard in

an appeal against deportation, will not entitle him to relief, since he has not been the

victim of unfair procedure. Where there is a charge of serious misconduct it is

especially important, as the Court of Appeal has emphasised, that procedural fairness

should be carefully observed. In various other contexts, however, it is scarcely less

important.

Failure to allow cross-examination by an objector at a statutory inquiry has led to the

quashing of the Secretary of State's decision, .... (at pages 512 – 513).

Thus, the entire proceedings is tainted and liable to be quashed; in addition to that, the

conclusion arrived at, by the 14th Respondent, based on the evidence so recorded after the

objection was raised, is also tainted with bias and is liable to be quashed.

However, the Respondents are at liberty to hold a fresh inquiry with minimum delay.

Nevertheless, due to the delays on the part of the Respondents, particularly the Public Service

Commission of the Southern Province and those who conducted the preliminary investigations

and preliminary inquiry before the charge sheet was prepared, I further direct the Respondents

that the Petitioner can be charged on the same amended charge sheet, but if there is any

disciplinary inquiry, it must be conducted without any undue delay.

Accordingly, it is my view that the Petitioner should be reinstated as the delay was not caused

by him since the delays of the preliminary investigation, followed by the disciplinary inquiry,

service of the charge sheet and amended charge sheet, amount to the delay of more than four

years (closer to five years) from the date of interdiction to come to the final conclusion.

Therefore, the delay was caused solely due to the lackadaisical manner of conduct of the

22

proceedings by the Respondents, and not due to any delaying tactics of the Petitioner. Thus, it

is my view that the Petitioner should be immediately reinstated, pending the final inquiry.

In that regard, I wish to cite the following proposition of law by His Lordship Justice Prasanna

Jayawardana in the judgement of T.G.J.L. Amarasinghe v. Dr. N.C.D. Ariyarathna and Others,

which reads thus;

In these circumstances, I hold that, in terms of the provisions of the Establishments

Code, the Disciplinary Authority was bound and obliged to reinstate the petitioner and

pay his monthly salary from 05th May 2015 onwards until the conclusion of the

disciplinary inquiry and the making of the disciplinary order. If the Disciplinary

Authority was of the view that reinstating the petitioner to service and permitting him

to exercise the functions of his office was not in the interests of the disciplinary inquiry,

the Disciplinary Authority was entitled to act on the lines of section 31:16 of the Code

which provides for placing the petitioner on compulsory leave or attaching the

petitioner to another suitable post pending the conclusion of the disciplinary inquiry

(at pages 26).

'1R1' and the guilt of the Petitioner

Furthermore, it is argued for and on behalf of the Respondents that though there may be certain

flaws in the procedure, even due to delay and ex parte proceedings, no writ should lie in view

of the fact that by 1R1, the Petitioner has admitted that he had misappropriated a certain sum

of money belonging to the School Development Society.

However, countering that argument, it was argued on behalf of the Petitioner that even the

worst criminal should be accorded with the proper procedure and due process of law, unless

the rules of natural justice is also observed in conducting investigations, inquiry, and finally in

23

arriving at a conclusion, according to the law; otherwise, the justice system will end up in a

chaotic situation if this is not done.

In fact, it is my view that if the argument of the Respondents is accepted without any

reservations, since the so-called admission by 1R1 made by the Petitioner amounts to an

admission which involves moral turpitude; in addition to that, 1R1 is addressed to a person in

authority. Therefore, if the guilt of a person is sealed based on such an admission, which

amounts to confession in terms of Section 17(2) of the Evidence Ordinance, and if a confession

is made to a person in authority, it is not acceptable in Criminal Law.

Further, I wish to state that based on 1R1, if the writ is denied, it amounts to a further denial of

his justice, which may fall into the category of another worse scenario conduct to achieve so-

called justice without following the proper Procedural Law, as that of Sir Walter Ralegh's trial.4

4 Rt Hon Lord Denning, Landmarks in the Law (First Edition 1984, Aditya Books Private Limited 1993), 4 –27.
