JUDGMENT

P. Kumararatnam, J.

The above-named Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as the

Appellant) was indicted by the Attorney General on the following charge:

• On or about the 23rd June 2017 the accused committed the murder of

Mudiyanselage Lakmal Sanjeewa Kumara Dissanayake alias Sanka

which is an offence punishable under Section 296 of the Penal Code.

As the Appellant opted for a non-jury trial, the trial commenced before a

judge and the prosecution had led seven witnesses and marked productions

P1-3 and closed the case. The Learned High Court Judge having been

satisfied that the evidence presented by the prosecution warrants a case to

answer, called for the defence and explained the rights of the accused. The

Appellant made a dock statement and closed his case.

After considering the evidence presented by both the prosecution and the

defence, the Learned High Court Judge had convicted the Appellant for

culpable homicide not amounting to murder under Section 297 of the Penal

Code and he was sentenced to 15 years rigorous imprisonment with a fine of

Rs.100,000/-. In default, 02-years simple imprisonment was imposed. The

Appellant was also ordered to pay a compensation of Rs.1,000,000/- to the

dependants of the deceased. In default 01-year simple imprisonment

imposed.

Being aggrieved by the aforesaid conviction and the sentence, the Appellant

preferred this appeal to this court.

4 | P a g e

The Learned Counsel for the Appellant informed this court that the Appellant

has given consent to argue this matter in his absence. At the hearing the

Appellant was connected via the Zoom platform from prison.

The following Grounds of Appeal were raised on behalf of the Appellant.

1. The Learned High Court Judge has failed to consider the absence of

mens rea as a fundamental element of the alleged offence and thereby

failed to consider the failure of the prosecution to prove the charge

against the Appellant beyond any reasonable doubt.

2. The Learned High Court Judge has failed to appreciate the evidence

on the inherent blind spots of the Wheel-Loading Machine and the

duty of bystanders to maintain a safe distance and thereby caused a

great prejudice to the Appellant.

3. The Learned High Court Judge has misdirected himself by drawing

inferences against the Appellant on surmises and conjectures.

The background of the case albeit briefly is as follows:

According to PW1, Wijesinghe, they had raised objections with regard to the

cleaning of a land called ‘Gonumale Land’ which has been a part of a land of

the highway construction site. PW1 with some others had raised objections

at the cleaning site, which was a prohibited area for outsiders other than the

workers assigned in the construction site. The Appellant was a Wheel-Loader

Operator who was assigned by his employer to clean the said area. PW1 with

others, had been agitated, stating that they had not been paid the

compensation due to them. As stated, the area in which the incident

happened had been declared as a restricted area at the time of the incident.

The defence’s position is that the Appellant, as a Wheel-Loader Operator,

was lawfully engaged in construction work within a restricted area where

unauthorised persons were not permitted to enter. PW1 had entered the said

area and engaged in an altercation with the Appellant, objecting to the

ongoing construction work. As a tense situation arose when PW1 and others

agitated for cleaning the said land, the Appellant had tried to calm them

down, however, the crowd including PW1 had behaved in a disturbing and

provoking manner and had assaulted the Appellant. Fearing his safety, as

the only option available to him was to leave the site, he had moved the

machine in order to move away from the site. In the meantime, PW1 and his

son PW2 had climbed onto the machine and begun to strike the windows of

the machine which were closed at that time. In the course of the melee, when

the Appellant moved the machine, the deceased, who was allegedly

positioned recklessly within the vehicle’s blind spot, was run over and, as a

result, had died at the scene. The defence consistently took up the position

that the Appellant had only made an attempt to leave the scene upon

observing the aggressive conduct of PW1 and PW2 along with others.

Therefore, the defence had argued that this was not a case of murder but an

accident beyond the control of the Appellant.

The evidence led by the prosecution clearly demonstrates that the Appellant

had no intention of causing the death of the deceased at that time. His

intention was only to leave the place for his own safety and to safeguard the

machine which had been entrusted to him. The evidence further revealed

that the deceased was a bystander who had no enmity with the Appellant.

He was standing without any knowledge of the danger of a heavy machine in

operation within a restricted area.

According to the submissions made by the defence, the model of the machine

is Dosan SD300 Wheel-Loader, which is classified as a heavy and inherently

dangerous construction vehicle. An operator controlling the machine from

within the cabin is unable to see individuals who enter the specified blind-

spot areas, thereby posing a serious risk to anyone who approaches the

vehicle while it is in operation.

CA/HCC/299/2025

6 | P a g e

The learned Counsel for the Appellant, by submitting a diagram, submitted

that a blind spot is an area around a vehicle or piece of construction

equipment that is not visible to the operator, either through their direct line

of sight or indirectly through the use of internal or external mirrors. The

presence of such blind spots creates a substantial risk, as an operator may

inadvertently run over persons, strike materials, or collide with other

vehicles or equipment without being aware of their presence.

The prosecution witnesses in their evidence admitted that a person who

stands in a blind spot is not visible to an operator of a machine of this nature.

Further, the prosecution witnesses admitted that the area where the incident

had happened was a restricted area which does not permit outsiders.

Further, they also admitted that they attempted to break the glass door of

the cabin of the Wheeler Loader while the Appellant was still inside.

PW6, the investigating officer also corroborated the fact that the people had

come to attack the Appellant on that date at that time.

Considering all these factors, the learned Counsel for the Appellant

submitted that he is only contesting the sentence, and contended that the

Appellant should have been given a lenient sentence in this case.

The essence of criminal law is said to lie in the maxim “actus non facit reum

nisi mens sit rea”. The essence of an offence is the wrongful intent, without

which it cannot exist. In this case it is very clear from the evidence given by

PW1 and PW2, that the Appellant had not acted with the intention of killing;

rather, the incident had occurred when he had attempted to flee with the

wheeler-Loader due to fear of assault and sabotage.

In Alo Singho v. Attorney General [1984] 1 SLR 30 the court held that:

“It is important to note that, in the instant case, the appellant had

inflicted only a single stab injury in circumstances indicative of the fact

that he had acted on the spur of the moment, without pre-meditation. No

motive was alleged by the prosecution against the appellant. Having

regard to the above facts, it seems to me that the misdirection

complained of, could well have caused prejudice to the appellant. In my

view, it is unsafe to assume that the jury would have found a murderous

intention to have been proved beyond reasonable doubt, had they been

told that the presumption was one of fact which could be rebutted on a

consideration of all the circumstances of the case. However, there could

be no doubt that the appellant had the knowledge that the injury was

likely to cause death, and was, therefore, guilty of the lesser offence of

culpable homicide, not amounting to murder”.

In King v. Kolonda 5 NLR 236 the court held that:

“The extent of his guilt must be determined by his intention when he

struck the blow, and not by its subsequent and possibly unforeseen

effects”.

In the case of Farook Vs. Attorney General [2006] 3 SLR 174 it was held:

“As regards the attempt to bring the case to one of culpable homicide not

amounting to murder mainly on the basis that there is no intention to cause

death, the intention that is required is to cause the injury in fact inflicted.

If the intended injury is sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of

nature, the offence is murder”

Considering this evidence, it clearly shows that the Appellant had not

entertained a murderous intention, which is a pre-requisite to find him guilty

of the murder charge.

At this stage, the learned Deputy Solicitor General in keeping with the

highest traditions of the Attorney General’s Department too agreed that this

is an appropriate case to re-consider the sentence imposed by the Learned

High Court Judge upon consideration of the circumstances of the case.

CA/HCC/299/2025

8 | P a g e

After the conviction of an Accused person, the discretion in imposing the

relevant sentence would lie with that particular Court, while being guided by

a number of factors, such as the circumstances under which the offence was

committed by the Accused. Where such specific sentencing guidelines would

not be available, it would be necessary to discuss the principles that must

be followed when imposing sentences.

In the case of The Attorney General v H.N.de Silva 57 NLR 121 Per

BASNAYAKE, A.C.J.-

" A Judge should, in determining the proper sentence, first consider the

gravity of the offence as it appears from the nature of the act itself and

should have regard to the punishment provided in the Penal Code or other

statute under which the offender is charged. He should also regard the

effect of the punishment as a deterrent and consider to what extent it will

be effective. If the offender held a position of trust or belonged to a service

which enjoys the public confidence that must be taken into account in

assessing the punishment. The incidence of crimes of the nature of which

the offender has been found to be guilty and the difficulty of detection are

also matters which should receive due consideration. The reformation of

the criminal, though no doubt an important consideration, is subordinate

to the others I have mentioned. Where the public interest or the welfare of

the State (which are synonymous) outweighs the previous good character,

antecedents and age of the offender, public interest must prevail. "

In the case of Don Percy Nanayakkara v The Republic of Sri Lanka [1993]

1 SLR 71 the Court held that:

“In assessing punishment, the Court has to consider the matter from the

point of both the offender and the public. The accused had held high public

office and exercised extensive statutory power in conducting public

examinations in this country. These examinations have to be conducted

fairly and the results declared accurately. Thousands of students who face

public examinations, every year, should have complete confidence in the

fairness and accuracy of every process of the examinations. The accused

has subverted the very basis of this confidence by his conduct in

dishonestly showing favour to persons with whom he was acquainted.

Therefore, public interest demands that he should be imposed a deterrent

punishment.”

In the case of Asan Mohammed Rizwan v Attorney General, CA [PHC] APN

141/2013 decided on March 25, 2015 it was held:

“Sentencing is an important aspect in the administration of criminal

justice system. A sentence ranges from death penalty to the mere

censure in the form of good behavior bond or probation. There are

multiple considerations relevant to the determination of a sentence. The

most important consideration is the seriousness of the crime.

Jurisprudentially, this position is persuasive despite pragmatic

difficulties associated with matching the harshness of the sanction to

the severity of the crime.

The judges are to pass lawful and appropriate sentence upon the

accused being convicted. In doing so, judges are to address their minds

to the objective of sentencing particularly when exercising the discretion

given to them under the law. Then only a correct sentence could be

passed upon a convicted accused. If not, criticism on lack of uniformity,

consistency and transparency in imposing sentences are bound to

surface. Therefore, it is necessary for the judges to keep in mind the

objectives of sentencing and also the sentencing guidelines, in order to

arrive at the correct and appropriate decision.

Objectives of sentencing include the following:

(i) To punish offenders to an extent and in a manner, which is

just in all the circumstances;

(ii) To protect the community from offender;

(iii) To deter offenders or other persons from committing offences

of the same or similar nature;

(iv) To establish conditions so that rehabilitation of offenders may

be promoted or facilitated;

(v) To signify that the court and the community denounce the

commission of such offences;

(vi) To maintain the required standards of societal expectations in

making decisions;

(vii) To prevent overcrowding prisons also could be considered as

one such objective particularly when it comes to developing

countries such as ours.”

In the case of Bandage Sumindra Jayanthi v. Attorney General (2015) 1

SLR 20 it was held:

“Sentencing belongs to the domain of the trial judge but that is no ground

for the prosecuting counsel to be complacent about his role in responding

to the mitigatory plea of a defense counsel.”

Considering all factors and the circumstances of the case, I set aside the

sentence imposed by the Learned High Court Judge of Kurunegala and

replace it with the following sentence.

The Appellant is sentenced to 02 years rigorous imprisonment with a fine of

Rs.25,000/-. This carries a default sentence of 06 months simple

imprisonment. A compensation of Rs.200,000/- is to be paid to the

deceased’s dependants, which is subject to a default sentence of 01-year

Further, the jail sentence imposed will be operative from the date of
