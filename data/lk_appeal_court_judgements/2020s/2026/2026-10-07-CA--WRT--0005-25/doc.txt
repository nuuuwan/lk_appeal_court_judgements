B. Sasi Mahendran, J.

The Petitioner, inter alia, seeks Writs of Certiorari quashing the Order dated 17th January 2024 marked

‘P18’, dismissing the Petitioner’s application, and the subsequent Order dated 12th June 2024 marked

‘P20’, refusing to review the said Order under Section 54(1) of the Debt Conciliation Board Ordinance.

The Petitioner further seeks Writs of Mandamus directing the 2nd to 6th Respondents to review the said

Order or, in the alternative, to conduct a re-preliminary hearing in Application No. 43732.

The following facts are relevant to this application

The Petitioner obtained a loan from the 1st Respondent and subsequently executed Deed No. 1000 dated

27th November 2012, which the Petitioner contends was in substance a mortgage securing a loan of Rs.

400,000, although it was described as a transfer. On 30th July 2015, the Petitioner applied to the Debt

Conciliation Board (herein after referred to as the Board) seeking settlement of his debt, claiming that

Deed No. 1000 was a mortgage and not an outright transfer.

The Petitioner gave evidence regarding the history of the property. The 1st Respondent disputed the

Petitioner's position, contending that Deed No. 1000 was an absolute transfer, not a mortgage. The 1st

Respondent produced a valuation report valuing the land at approximately Rs. 1,023,120, together with

two bank books, claiming that Rs. 1,000,000 had been paid for the purchase of the property. After

considering the evidence, the Board, by its Order dated 17th January 2024 marked ‘P18’’, dismissed the

Page 4 of 9

Petitioner's application, holding that the Petitioner had failed to establish that Deed No. 1000 was a

mortgage securing a loan.

The Petitioner thereafter applied for a review under Section 54 of the Debt Conciliation Ordinance

(hereinafter referred to as the Ordinance) on 09th April 2024. The Board, by its Order dated 12th June

2024 marked ‘P20’, refused the application for review.

The Petitioner thereafter invoked the writ jurisdiction of this Court, seeking, inter alia, a Writ of

Certiorari to quash both Orders ‘P18’ and ‘P20’.

The Petitioner's principal complaint is that the Board failed to properly evaluate the evidence adduced

by the Petitioner, particularly regarding the valuation of the property and his occupation of the said

property.

I am mindful that Section 21A of the said Ordinance sets out how the Board must act with regard to an

application relating to a transfer or conditional transfer of immovable property.

For easy reference, I reproduce Section 21A of the said Ordinance,

“Section 21A- Matters to be considered in deciding whether or not a conditional transfer of

immovable property is in reality a mortgage.

(1) In any Proceedings under the Ordinance in regard to an application relating to a transfer

or conditional transfer of immovable property, the Bond shall, notwithstanding anything to the

contrary in section 2 of the Prevention of Frauds Ordinance, or sections 91 and 92 of the

Evidence Ordinance and for the purpose of deciding whether or not such transfer or conditional

transfer is in reality a mortgage, take into consideration all the circumstances of the case and

in particular the following matters

(a) The language of the notarial instrument of transfer and where provision in regard

to the right of the transferor or any other person to redeem or purchase the property

transferred is contained in any other notarial instrument.

(b) Any difference between the sum received by the transferor from the transferee and

the value of the property transferred;

(c) The continuance of the transferor’s possession of the property transferred and

(d) The existence of any agreement in whatever form between the transferor and the

transferee interest, or any sum which may reasonably be considered to be interest, on

the sum received by the transferor from the transferee

Page 5 of 9

(e) The burden of adducing evidence to show that a transfer of immovable property is

in reality a mortgage shall be on the transferor.”

The Board was required to consider the aforesaid matters in determining whether the transaction

constituted an outright transfer. It should be noted that, under subsection (2) of the said section, the

burden of proving the transfer of the mortgage always rests with the transferor.

Generally, our courts require a person who relies on a deed, claiming that it does not constitute an

outright transfer but rather a mortgage, to establish that fact by reference to the attendant circumstances.

For easy reference, I reproduce subsection 2 of the said section.

‘(2) The burden of adducing evidence to show that a transfer of immovable property is in reality

a mortgage shall be on the transferor.”

Having considered the evidence placed before the Board, I am of the view that the Board duly

considered the relevant circumstances of the transaction in terms of Section 21A of the Ordinance. The

Board considered, inter alia, the consideration stated in the deed, the value of the property, the

circumstances in which the Petitioner required the money, his subsequent conduct in advertising the

property for sale, the registration of the caveat, and the valuation evidence placed before it. The Board

was therefore entitled, upon an assessment of the evidence before it, to conclude that the transaction

was an outright transfer and not a mortgage.

In the present application, the Petitioner seeks a Writ of Certiorari to quash the decision of the Board

dated 17th January 2024. However, the Board acted within its jurisdiction, duly considered the relevant

matters under Section 21A and evaluated the evidence before it. There is no material to establish that

the Board acted without or in excess of jurisdiction or committed any error warranting the intervention

of this Court by way of Certiorari.

At this juncture, I am mindful of the observations made by Grero, J in Desmond Perera and Others v.

Karunarathne, Commissioner of National Housing and Others, [1994] 3 SLR page 316, at pages 329

and 330:

“As pointed out by the learned President's Counsel for the 3rd respondent, Lord Diplock in the

case of CCSV v. Minister for the Civil Service (1984) UKHL 9, clearly classified under three

heads the grounds on which administrative action could be subject to control by judicial review.

They are:

(i) Illegality;

(ii) Irrationality; and

(iii) Procedural impropriety.

Describing the aforesaid grounds, Lord Diplock said as follows:

“By 'Illegality' as a ground for judicial review, I mean that the decision-maker must

understand correctly the law that regulates his decision-making power and must give effect to

it. Whether he has or not is par excellence a justiciable question to be decided, in the event of

dispute, by those persons, by judges by whom the judicial power of the state is exercisable.”

In the present case, I find no material to establish that the Board failed to consider the relevant matters

required to be considered under Section 21A of the Ordinance. The reasons given by the Board

demonstrate that it considered and evaluated the evidence placed before it and arrived at its conclusion

on the basis of that evidence.

I'm satisfied that the Petitioner has failed to establish that he continued to remain in possession, or he

couldn’t prove that there is a gross disparity between the consideration in the deed and the market value

of the property. Also, I am mindful that the Petitioner couldn’t establish the fact that he paid the interest

to the 1st Respondent.

Generally, our courts hold that unless the above facts are established, it can be presumed that there is

an outright transfer. I hold that the particular order ‘P18’ considered all the relevant facts and came to

the correct conclusion, which has been affirmed in the order marked as ‘P20’.

Accordingly, I hold that the Petitioner has failed to establish that the said Orders marked P18 and P20

are illegal, irrational, or tainted by procedural impropriety, so as to provide any basis upon which a Writ

of Certiorari could be issued to quash the decisions of the Board dated marked ‘P18’ and ‘P20’.
