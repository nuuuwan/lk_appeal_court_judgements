“(3) The Supreme Court of the Republic of Sri Lanka and the Court of

Under section 3(1) of the Contempt of Court Act, a court, tribunal or

institution should satisfy itself that, alleged act or omission has been

committed by a person “with intent to” bring the authority of a court,

tribunal or institution and administration of justice into disrespect or

disregard; or interfere with, or cause grave prejudice to the judicial process in

relation to any ongoing litigation. [Emphasis is mine]

Hence, it becomes manifest that, for the proper constitution of an offence of

contempt of court under section 3(1) of the Contempt of Court Act, court,

tribunal or institution must satisfy itself not only of the falsity of the statement

made by the person alleged under oath or affirmation but also, must satisfy

itself that, the false statement had been made by that person “with intent

to” bring the authority of a court, tribunal or institution and administration

of justice into disrespect or disregard; or interfere with, or cause grave

prejudice to the judicial process in relation to any ongoing litigation.

Let me next, deal with the binding judicial precedents relevant and pertinent

to the matter at hand.

In Asnar vs. Andrew 15 NLR 406, the appellant was, at the conclusion of

the case, charged by the commissioner for giving false evidence by stating on

oath or affirmation that the defendant signed the plaintiff’s book when the

account was looked into. The appellant admitted that his statement was

untrue but stated, to quote his own words, “I admit that I lied but it happened

a long time ago.” On that, the learned Commissioner convicted him under

section 800 of the Civil Procedure Code and sentenced him to two months

rigorous imprisonment. It was held by the Supreme Court in appeal as

follows;

“Now it has been held several times in this Court that a mere false

statement on oath does not amount to a contempt of court punishable under

section 800 of the Civil Procedure Code. A false statement may in ‘some cases’,

be so transparently, false as to be disrespectful to the Court and so amount

to a contempt. But a false statement unaccompanied by any special

circumstances of this nature does not amount to a contempt of court within

the meaning of part LX of the Civil Procedure Code.”

In Wickramasekera vs. Dingiri Banda 16 NLR 458, the appellant, who was

a witness, appealed from an order of the District Judge convicting him of

contempt of Court and imposing on him a fine of Rs. 100. The alleged

contempt, according to the District Judge, consists in prevarication. He

records that the appellant is charged with "contempt of Court by trying to

mislead it by prevarication," and the prevarication referred to here is thus set

forth by the District Judge:." That he, on the 16th instant, denied that the

Tambligahawatta, which had been transferred to him by a notarial deed, had

been so transferred by the first defendant, and stated that she had no

connection whatever with that transaction. It was held by the Supreme Court

in appeal that,

“Now, deliberate prevarication no doubt, constitutes contempt of Court;

but what is prevarication, and was the appellant guilty of prevarication, or

CA/COC/0003/26

Page 14 of 23

merely of making a false statement when giving evidence on oath? To

prevaricate literally means to shift or turn from one side to the other. As

commonly used, it means to evade telling the truth, to shuffle, to quibble.

The underlying idea is an artful mode of escaping the scrutiny of an

inquirer by the use generally of loose and indefinite expressions. This

being so, it is. quite clear that the appellant was not guilty of

prevarication. He was asked a certain question, and he gave a clear and

definite though false answer. That was not prevarication, and it follows that

the conviction on the ground of prevarication cannot stand. Can it be said

that the appellant is guilty of contempt in giving false evidence? A Judge

cannot be too careful and cautious in proceeding as for contempt against

a witness who generally gives false evidence. If this course is freely

pursued, some one witness or another may have to be punished for

contempt of Court in almost every case. Ordinarily, a witness who gives

false evidence in a Court of justice intends no contempt. Intention may,

however, be presumed, and the witness may be guilty of contempt if the

evidence is so transparently false as to give rise to a reasonable suspicion

of an attempt on the part of the witness to trifle with the Court. The

position is clearly laid down by the Chief Justice in the case of Asanar v.

Andrew. I cannot say that the answer given by the witness in the present case

was evidence of the description mentioned above. I say nothing of the possible

ways in which the witness might have been dealt with. I set aside the order

appealed from. [Emphasis is mine]

It is my view that, the prevarication is implicit in section 3(1) of the Contempt

of Court Act too, inasmuch as the legislature deliberately, uses therein, the

phrase “with intent to” as being direct and essential corollary for the

constitution of a charge of contempt of court thereunder, for; as a general rule

a mere utterance of a false statement under oath or affirmation alone, would

not amount to contempt of court under section 3(1) of the Contempt of Court

Act, unless, it is accompanied by a clear intention to bring the authority of a

court, tribunal or institution and administration of justice into disrespect or

disregard; or interfere with, or cause grave prejudice to the judicial process in

relation to any ongoing litigation for; the Legislature in incorporating the

phrase “with intent to” into the section 3(1) of the Contempt of Court Act

clearly, intended that it was so as being direct and essential corollary for the

constitution of a charge of contempt of court thereunder. [Emphasis is mine]

Hence, it becomes manifest upon a careful scrutiny of section 3(1) of the

Contempt of Court Act and the binding judicial precedents of the Supreme

Court as cited above, that, two essential ingredients must be present at the

same time for the constitution of an offence of a contempt of court under

section 3(1) of the Contempt of Court Act, namely;

a) Commission of an act or omission;

b) Commission of an act or omission as such must be in such a

nature that the person alleged to have committed the offence of a

contempt of a court, tribunal or institution, had by the

commission of the alleged act or omission, clearly, and

unequivocally, intended to bring the authority of a court, tribunal

or institution and administration of justice into disrespect or

disregard; or interfere with, or cause grave prejudice to the

judicial process in relation to any ongoing litigation.
