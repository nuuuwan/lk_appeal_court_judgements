Judgment

1. The accused-appellant (hereinafter referred to as the appellant) has been

indicted in the High Court of Embilipitiya together with another in High

Court Case No. HC 85/2013. The charges in the indictment are as follows;

01. On or around the 18th of July, 2007, within the jurisdiction of

this Court, in Katagal Ara, you have caused injury to one

Egodawaththa Aarachchige Weerarathna, using deadly

weapons, particularly a gun and a knife, while threatening to

rob him of a motorbike in his possession; thereby you have

committed an offence punishable under Section 382 of the Penal

Code of Sri Lanka read with Section 32.

02. At the same time, place, and course of action as the above first

charge, you did stab one Egodawaththa Aarachchige

Weerarathna with a knife, with such intention or knowledge and

3

under such circumstances that had you by such an act caused

the death of said Egodawaththa Aarachchige Weerarathna, you

would have been guilty of murder, and that you by such an act

caused hurt to the said Egodawaththa Aarachchige

Weerarathna; thereby you have committed an offence

punishable under Section 300 of the Penal Code.

03. At the same time, place, and course of action as the above first

charge, you did commit the robbery of a motorbike valued at Rs.

25,000.00 in the possession of Amudawana Aarachchige

Jayarathne, thereby committing an offence punishable under

Section 380 of the Penal Code and in the commission of such

offence by using deadly weapons, particularly a knife or a gun,

you have committed an offence punishable under Section 383 of

the Penal Code read with Section 32.

2. At the conclusion of the trial, the learned High Court Judge has convicted

the appellant of the charges and sentenced him as follows;

i. 15 years rigorous imprisonment and a fine of Rs.15,000.00

together with a term of 12 months simple imprisonment in

default, for the first charge.

ii. A term of 20 years rigorous imprisonment and a fine of

Rs.15,000.00 together with a term of 12 months simple

imprisonment in default, for the second charge

iii. Term of 10 yrs rigorous imprisonment and a fine of Rs.15,000.00,

together with a term of 12 months simple imprisonment in

default for the third charge.

It has also been directed that the terms of rigorous imprisonment

in respect of first and second charge run concurrently.

4

The appellant has been further directed to pay a sum of

Rs.100,000.00 as compensation to PW02, with a term of 12

months simple imprisonment in default.

3. The appellant aggrieved by the conviction, disputed judgement together

with the sentencing order has preferred the instant appeal to this court.

Case of the Prosecution

4. PW01 has been directly and adversely affected by the incident described

in the third charge and PW02 has been affected by the incidents

described in the first and second charge.

5. On the date stated in the charges, PW01 has set out to attend Court on

his motorbike. On the way ,he has been accosted by two individuals,

one armed with a firearm and the other with a sharp edged weapon

(knife).

6. When the individuals approached PW01, the person with the sharp

edged weapon has held it against PW01’s stomach. Sensing danger,

PW01 has got off the motorbike. At that moment, the two individuals

have fled the scene on PW01’s motorbike.

7. Thereafter, PW01 has run in the direction the individuals had fled with

his motorbike. A short distance away, he has observed another

motorbike on the road and a bloodied person in a nearby ditch. PW01

has informed this person of his intention to file a police complaint and

then departed from the location. PW01 has subsequently identified the

individual who was armed with the sharp edged weapon as the

appellant and the other person as the one who carried the firearm.

5

8. PW02 has been returning to “Akkara Pansiya” area from Embilipitya,

when he had been accosted by two persons. One of them has pointed a

firearm at him and demanded that the motorbike PW02 was riding, be

handed over to them. At that point PW02 has switched off the motorbike

and thrown its keys. A scuffle has then ensued and PW02 stabbed. To

escape from the attackers, PW02 has jumped into a nearby canal.

9. Thereafter, PW02 has witnessed his attackers pushing his motorbike

away from the location. Sometime later, PW02 has come out of the

canal. At that point, he has also witnessed a person walking past the

location of the incident and has informed PW02 that he was going to

make a police complaint.

Case of the Appellant

10. The appellant has maintained that he was not involved in the incidents

stated in the charges.

Grounds of Appeal

11. When the matter was taken up for argument, the counsel for the

appellant, urged the following grounds of appeal

I. Have the victims identified the appellant properly?

II. Has the learned High Court judge applied the principles relevant

to a Section 27 recovery in the proper perspective?

III. Is the sentence imposed on the appellant excessive?

12. The identification of an offender is a pivotal aspect of the criminal

justice system. It serves not only to ensure the accountability of

6

individuals who commit crimes but also to uphold the rule of law and

maintain societal order.

13. First and foremost, the identification of an offender is necessary to

facilitate the prosecution of crime. Without the ability to identify

offenders, the judicial process becomes ineffective, and victims may find

themselves denied justice. Also, wrongful accusations can occur

without proper identification methods, leading to potentially

devastating consequences for innocent individuals.

14. There are various methods by which offenders can be identified.

Traditional approaches include eyewitness testimony, where

individuals who witness a crime can help in the identification process.

15. Forensic science has revolutionised offender identification introducing

advanced techniques, such as fingerprint analysis, DNA profiling and

ballistics. Fingerprint analysis remains one of the most established

methods, enabling law enforcement to match prints discovered at the

crime scenes with those in existing databases. DNA profiling offers an

even higher degree of accuracy, as DNA found at a crime scene can be

compared against databases containing genetic information from

known offenders. These forensic methods reduce the margin of error in

offender identification and increase the likelihood of apprehending the

correct individual.

16. In addition to forensic techniques, modern technology plays a crucial

role in offender identification. Surveillance systems equipped with high-

definition cameras have become commonplace in urban settings. They

provide visual evidence that can aid investigations. Facial recognition

technology further enhances the ability to identify offenders by

automating the comparison of images captured by security cameras

against those in digital databases.

7

17. In the current case, the prosecution has attempted to utilise the

traditional approach in the identification process. Eyewitness testimony

of individuals who have witnessed the crime have been assisted in

identifying the appellant and the other individual allegedly involved in

the offences stated in the charges. In that regard, PW01 and PW02 have

testified as follows;

18. PW01 has identified the appellant as the individual who was armed with

the sharp edged weapon and held it against the witness’s stomach

during the incident described in the third charge.

19. After the appellant was arrested, taken into police custody and accused

of the offences detailed in the charges, investigators have presented him

for an identification parade. During this parade, PW01 serving as a

witness has identified the appellant. PW01 has further explained the

appellant’s involvement, stating that the appellant was armed with a

sharp edged weapon and held it against PW01’s stomach before fleeing

the scene on his motorbike. Hence, PW01’s identification of the

appellant has been consistent and is corroborated by the idetification

parade notes.

20. Regarding PW02’s testimony, he has stated that it was not the

appellant, but the other individual present, who was armed with the

sharp edged weapon. However, during the identification parade, PW02

serving as a witness has explained that the appellant was in fact in

armed with the sharp edged weapon.

21. Furthermore, while testifying in Court, PW02 has also referred to the

specific features he relied upon to identify the offenders. During cross-

examination, however, the Courts attention has been drawn to several

material omissions regarding these identifying features in PW02’s

statement to the investigators.

10

22. The learned High Court Judge has identified the material discrepancies

in PW02’s testimony. He has consequently, decided against relying on

it. Which has resulted in PW02 being unable to identify the offenders

involved in the incidents described in the first and second charges.

23. Although, PW01 has consistently identified the appellant as one of the

individuals involved in the incident described in the third charge, the

learned High Court Judge has also considered the discovery of PW01’s

motorbike, which was found following information received from the

appellant during his police custody for an offence. The learned High

Court Judge has further concluded that this discovery allows the court

to draw a presumption, as outlined in the illustration (a) of Section 114

of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895.

24. Section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895 (as Amended)

provides as follows;

“27.

(1) Provided that, when any fact is deposed to as discovered in

consequence of information received from a person accused of

any offence, in the custody of a police of;icer, so much of such

information, whether it amounts to a confession or not, as

relates distinctly to the fact thereby discovered may be proved.

(2) Subsection (1) shall also apply mutates mutandis, in the case of

information received from a person accused of any act made

punishable under the Forest Ordinance, or the Excise

Ordinance, when such person is in the custody of a forest of;icer

or an excise of;icer, respectively.”

11

25. As such, Section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895 (as

Amended), allows a specific portion of a statement made by a person

accused of an offence, in police custody to be admitted as evidence, if it

directly leads to a discovery/recovery of a physical fact or item.

Although Sections 25 and 26 of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895

(as Amended), state that confessions made to a Police officer, Forest

officer, Excise officer, or while in police custody, are generally not

admissible, Section 27 of the Ordinance acts as a vital exception.

26. When any fact deposed to, is discovered in consequence of information

received from a person accused of an offence, in the custody of a police

officer, so much of such information, whether it amounts to a

confession or not, as relates distinctly to the fact thereby

discovered/recovered may be proven.

27. The key requirements for the application of the provisions in Section 27

of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895 (as Amended), are that the

accused must be formally in the custody of a police officer when giving

the information. Only the exact words that point directly to the

discovered object or place are allowed to be produced as evidence.

Though, general confessions or unrelated admissions are barred,

physical facts such as a hidden weapon, stolen property or an item of

clothing worn by a victim must be newly found, as a direct result of the

information.

28. Furthermore, a recovery made under Section 27, establishes that the

information given by a person accused of an offence, and in police

custody, was true and the person providing it had knowledge of the

existence and location of the fact discovered.

29. In the case of H.M Heen Banda vs. The Queen 75 NLR 54, Sirimane J,

held,

12

“Where part of a statement of an accused person is put in evidence under

section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance, it is the duty of the trial Judge to

explain to the Jury that such a statement is only evidence of the fact that

the accused knew where the article discovered could be found, and nothing

more.”

30. Similarly, in Ranasinghe vs. Attorney-General [2007] Sri LR 218, Sisira

de Abrew J, has held,

“...discovery in consequence of a section 27 statement only leads to the

conclusion that the accused had the knowledge as to the weapon being

kept at the place from which it was detected.”

31. In the case of Samson Atygala vs. Attorney General [1986] 1 Sri. LR 390,

Bandaranayake J, has held,

“For the purposes of s.27 of the Evidence Ordinance the person making the

statement should be a person accused of an offence, and be in police

custody”

32. Section 114 of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895 provides as

follows;

114. The court may presume the existence of any fact which it thinks likely

to have happened, regard being had to the common course of natural

events, human conduct, and public and private business in their

relation to the facts of the particular case.

Illustrations

13

The Court may presume- (a) that a man who is in possession of stolen

goods soon after the theft is either the thief or has received the goods

knowing them to be stolen, unless he can account for his possession;

33. Under section 114 illustration (a), of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of

1895, a Court may presume that a person in possession of stolen goods

after the theft is either the thief or has received the goods knowing them

to be stolen unless he can reasonably account for their possession.

34. For a court to safely apply the presumption under illustration (a) the

prosecution must conclusively establish three criteria;

i. The prosecution must prove that the property in question was

indeed stolen or robbed.

ii. The stolen items must be found in the direct, conscious and

exclusive possession of the accused.

iii. The length of time between the theft and the discovery of the

items is critical. “Soon after” is relative, depending on whether

the items were easily transferable or distinct and

cumbersome.

35. Once the prosecution establishes recent possession a Court can

evaluate the situation based on how the accused responds. If the

accused offers no explanation or, gives an explanation that a Court

finds completely false, the Court may safely infer guilt of theft or

retaining stolen property.

36. An accused does not need to prove his innocence beyond a reasonable

doubt. If he offers an explanation that might reasonably be true, the

presumption is rebutted.

14

37. In the case of King vs. Jayasena 48 NLR 241, Howard C.J., held;

“That even if the theft and dishonest receiving were not committed in the

same transaction the joinder of all the accused in one charge was in order,

and that it was open to the court under sections 181 and 182 of the

criminal procedure code to ;ind some of the accused guilty of theft and

some of dishonest receiving.

...

Where, in a prosecution for dishonest receipt of stolen property, It is

established that accused was in possession of goods stolen the burden is

on the accused to give an explanation which in the opinion of the court,

might reasonably be true and which is consistent with innocence.”

38. As stated elsewhere in the judgement, a recovery made under

Section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance No. 14 of 1895 primarily

establishes that the individual providing information possessed

knowledge of the discovered item’s existence and location. Such a

recovery does not however, permit a Court to attribute exclusive

possession of the item to that person. Therefore, in these

circumstances, the learned High Court Judge has erred in law by

presuming that the motorbike stolen from PW01 was in the

appellant’s possession at a particular point of time.

39. However, the prosecution has established beyond a reasonable

doubt, through the eyewitness account of PW01, that the

appellant was involved in committing the offence stated in the

third charge. As for the first and second charges, PW02 has been

unable to identify the offenders. Consequently, the prosecution

has failed to establish, through direct evidence, the appellant’s

involvement in those particular offences. Furthermore, no

15

circumstantial evidence has been produced to infer the appellant’s

involvement in committing those offences.

40. In those circumstances, I affirm the conviction of the appellant in

respect of the third charge and acquit the appellant of the first

and second charges.

41. I also adjust the sentencing order accordingly.

42. The sentence imposed in respect of the third charge shall remain

unchanged and the substantial term of 10 years rigorous

imprisonment shall commence from the date of conviction. The

sentence imposed in respect of the first, together with the second

charge and the compensation ordered to be paid to PW02 is set

aside.

Appeal partly allowed.

43. I make no order regarding costs.
