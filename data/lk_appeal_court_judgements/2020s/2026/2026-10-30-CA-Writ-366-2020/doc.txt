12] Section 49 of the Land Development Ordinance (LDO) enacts

“Upon the death of a permit-holder who at the time of his or her death was

paying an annual sum by virtue of the provisions of subsection (3) of section

19A,* or of an owner of a holding, without leaving behind his or her spouse,

or, where such permit-holder or owner died leaving behind his or her

spouse, upon the failure of such spouse to succeed to the land alienated to

that permit-holder on the permit or holding or upon the death of such spouse,

a person nominated as successor by such permitholder or owner

shall succeed to that land or holding.” [Emphasis is added]

13] The effect of succussion has been mentioned in section 62 of LDO. It says;

Section 62 (1)

“After the registration of a document whereby a person is nominated as

successor to a holding or a land alienated on a permit, a document which

purports to nominate any other person as successor to that holding or land

shall not be registered unless the nomination effected by the registered

document has been duly cancelled by the registration of a document of

cancellation.

Provided that it shall be lawful in one and the same document to

cancel a registered nomination and to make some other nomination

in lieu thereof; and in that event, notwithstanding anything in this section

contained, the document in which such cancellation and nomination are

combined may be registered and shall upon due registration operate both

of a new nominee.” [ Emphasis is added]

19] Section 68(2) of LDO shows how a successor loses his or her right to succeed.

It shows;

“68 (2) A nominated successor fails to succeed to the land held on a permit

by a permitholder who at the time of his or her death was paying an annual

sum by virtue of the provisions of subsection (3) of section 19A* or to the

holding of an owner if he refuses to succeed to that land or holding, or, if

the nominated successor does not enter into possession of that land or

holding within a period of six months reckoned-

(i) where such permit-holder or owner dies without leaving behind his or her

spouse, from the date of the death of such permit-holder or owner; or

(ii) where such permit-holder or owner dies leaving behind his or her spouse,

from the date of the failure of such spouse to succeed, such date being

reckoned according to the provisions of paragraph (b) of subsection (I), or of

the death of such spouse, as the case may be.”

20] The 1st Respondent admits the Petitioner applied within six months as

stipulated. By R5 he has mentioned that the Petitioner duly succeeded and she

(the Petitioner) agreed to a subdivision. When the nominated successor duly

succeeded there cannot be further inquiry under section 72 of LDO. It should

be noted that in terms of Section 72 of the LDO, if the nominated successor fails

to succeed, the land or holding shall devolve as per the Rule 1 of the Third

schedule. Section 72 states thus;

Page 10 of 15

“If no successor has been nominated, or if the nominated successor fails to

succeed, or if the nomination of a successor contravenes the provisions of

this Ordinance, the title to the land alienated on a permit to a permit-holder

who at the time of his or her death was paying an annual instalment by

virtue of the provisions of section 19 or to the holding of an owner shall,

upon the death of such permit-holder or owner without leaving behind his

or her spouse, or,

where such permit-holder or owner died leaving behind his or her spouse,

upon the failure of such spouse to succeed to that land or holding, or upon

the death of such spouse, devolve as prescribed in rule 1 of the Third

Schedule.”

21] The Petitioner, being the eldest child, was formally nominated and registered

as the lawful successor to a plot of land granted to her mother by a Presidential

Grant in 1995. Following the deaths of her mother in 2009 and her father (the

original permit holder's surviving spouse who occupied the land until his

passing) in March 2019, the Petitioner applied to the 1st Respondent within

three months to be named the official successor. Despite the Petitioner

consenting to allocate portions of the land to other family members occupying

it, the 1st Respondent named the 6th Respondent (the surviving eldest son) as

the sole successor in January 2020 on the grounds that the Petitioner was not

in occupation.

22] It is further seen that given the validly registered nomination, the Petitioner’s

prompt application within 3 months, and consent from occupants, the 1st

Respondent's interpretation that the possession of the Petitioner must be

considered for valid nomination is against the law and unreasonable.

23] Therefore, registration of the 6th Respondent’s ownership under Section 72

and Rule 1 of the Third Schedule to the LDO by P11(a) and P11(b) because the

Petitioner failed to take physical or constructive possession of the land within

six months of the spouse’s death cannot be tenable.

24] In PIYASENA v. WIJESINGHE & OTHERS, [2002] SLR 242, it was held;

Page 11 of 15

“The nomination of a successor under the permit becomes converted to

nomination made by her as the owner of the land. At the time of her death

B was entitled to be considered as owner by virtue of the fact that she had

been awarded a grant.”

“The nomination in the Permit itself shall stand valid until it is subsequently

cancelled by the Permit Holder. The nomination of a successor under the

Permit becomes converted to nomination made by the permit holder as the

owner of the land.”

25] In Mohideen Bawa Abdul Cassim vs Weeragoda Arachchige Pathmasiri

Silva and others, SC Appeal 48/2013, Decided on: 04.09.2019, His Lordship

Vijith K. Malalgoda PC J held;

“In the said circumstances it is clear, that according to the above provisions

of the Land Development Ordinance, a nominated person can only succeed

to a land to which he is lawfully nominated, is either after the death of the

spouse or when the said spouse failed to succeed to the said property. When

going through the above provisions it is further observed that the nominee

is not entitled by law to succeed to a property until the death (or failure to

succeed to the land) of the spouse of the original permit holder. In other

words he becomes entitled by law to succeed to the property only after the

death (or failure to succeed) of the spouse of the original permit holder.”

26] In the instant case, the Petitioner duly submitted the application for

succession according to the stipulated time. Further, in Mallehe

Vidanaralalage don Agostino v. Divisional Secretary and others SC Appeal

No. 30/2004 decided on 23.03.2005 His Lordship Justice S.N. Silva CJ observed

that;

“it is clear from the provisions of the law that the change in the nature of

the holding from that of a permit to a Grant is one purpose and it should not

be taken as two distinct processes for the purpose of annulling a nomination

that has been previously made.”

Page 12 of 15

27] Thus, the original nomination carries out until proper cancellation, if not the

nominated successor would be entitled to the ownership. This position was

reiterated in the case of Ranasinghe Arachchige Nimal Ranasinghe v.

Commissioner of Lands and Development CA Writ 17/2020 decided on

31.08.2023 where Justice Mohammed Laffar held;

“In light of the aforementioned judicial pronouncements and the provisions

of the Land Development Ordinance, I hold that the nomination of

succession made by the Permit Holder, Ranasinghe, in the Permit, to the 3rd

Respondent extends its validity to the Grant as well.”

28] In M.W.D. Dayaratne vs. M.W.D. Agosinno, (supra) His Lordship S.N. Silva

C.J. has stressed the importance of giving effect to the wish of the original allotee

who was already indicated by the grantee to the relevant authority. In the said

case Supreme Court stressed upon the fact that “the change in the nature of the

holding from that of a permit to a grant is one process and it should not be taken

as two distinct processes for the purpose of annulling a nomination that has been

previously made”.

29] In Galappaththi Vs. Commissioner General Of Lands And Others [2020]

3 SLR 130, His Lordship SAMAYAWARDHENA, J. clearly held the relationship

of permit and grant and held;

“

1. A grant has no independent genesis without a permit. The permit is

the precursor to the grant; the two are intrinsically linked. It is the

permit which matures into a grant upon the fulfilment of certain

conditions.

2. If the owner of a holding has not nominated a successor in the grant,

the successor nominated in the permit preceding the grant is entitled

to succeed to the land.

Page 13 of 15

3. Rule 1 of the Third Schedule read with section 72 of the Land

Development Ordinance has no application in such situations. This

Rule becomes applicable only if no successor has been nominated or

the nominated successor fails to succeed.”

30] In Weeravairodi Mudiyanselage Agala Kumbure Gedara Jayathissa v.

Weeravairodi Mudiyanselage Agala Kumbure Gedara Punchi Banda,

SC/APPEAL/109/2020, Decided on: 17.12.2025, His Lordship

Samayawardhena, J. Considered the possession related to LDO permits and

held;

“It is necessary to state at this stage that possession is mainly of two kinds,

namely actual possession and constructive possession. Section 68 shall

be construed as encompassing both forms of possession. To hold

otherwise would defeat the statutory scheme and place a surviving

spouse, particularly one of advanced age, at an unfair disadvantage, as

such a spouse may be incapable of entering into physical possession of the

land upon the death of the permit-holder or owner.” [Emphasis is added]

31] the possession relates to lands has been considered in Iqbal v. Majedudeen

and Others [1999] 3 SLR 213. In that case the Court of Appeal of Sri Lanka

establishes key legal principles regarding the definition of possession and

forcible dispossession under the Primary Courts' Procedure Act.

Actual

Possession

When a person has direct, immediate physical control over a

corporeal thing (such as a house or land) at a given time.

Constructive

Possession

When a person is not in physical possession but retains both

the power and intention to exercise dominion, control, or

general management over the property (either directly or

through someone else).

32] The impugned decision was made by the 1st Respondent considering the

possession. In my view, for Land Development Ordinance, possession of a land

has no material or relevance. It is important to consider wish of the permit holder

or grantee. If the grantee wants to do a fresh nomination, there are provisions

under Land Development Ordinance. If he or she does not change the original

nomination, despite who are in the possession, the wish of the grantee or permit

holder should prevail under law. It should be remembered and stressed that

under section 161 of the Land Development Ordinance no one can acquire lands

subject to LDO by prescription. It prohibits the acquisition of lands by

prescription. It enacts;

“161. No person shall, by possession of any land alienated on a permit,

acquire any prescriptive title thereto against any other person or against the

State.”

33] In Lebbe vs Umma [1999] 3 SLR 367, His Lordship WIGNESWARAN, J., held

that the restrictions in the Land Development Ordinance are absolute. Because

the LDO explicitly requires the Government Agent's prior written consent before

an allotment can be alienated, any transfer executed without it holds no legal

validity. No title ever passed if no sanction has been taken, the long possession

does not give the title.

34] In granting P11(a), the 1st Respondent, Divisional Secretary of Neluwa has

considered the possession of the parties. Divisional Secretary cannot override

the provisions of Land Development Ordinance. Thus, the impugned decision is

illegal against the law and untenable. It should be noted that if the land has

been developed by another party, other than the owner, permit holder, grantee

or nominated successor with permission of the owner, the Divisional Secretary

has power to make subdivisions and grant portion of lands to the party who

made the developments or improvements with the approval of the owner,

grantee, permit holder or nominated successor such as R5, in view of

justification of the improvements and developments made to the land. If the

development and improvements of the land made with the approval, it has to be

Page 15 of 15

compensated in some manner, possibly by way of subdivision with the

agreement of the owner, permit holder or nominated successor. This consent

can be implied. It is a burning issue that parents (Owner, Grantee or Permit

holder) keep silence when improving and developing the land subject to LDO by

the other siblings despite nominated successor is available. After their demise

(parents), this would turn to long standing legal battle. If the land was developed

by someone else of the family other than the nominated successor, he or she

(the developer) should be nominated by the Owner, Grantee or permit holder as

fresh nomination. If not the nominated successor should be given the

ownership. Otherwise, Divisional Secretaries hands are tight, they cannot do a

justice in good faith to the developer who developed and improved the land. In

the instant case, by R5 agreement, the Petitioner, is not in possession, has

agreed for subdivision and she now cannot blow hot and cold, approbate and

reprobate, her stance. Thus, this judgment has no bar to carry out the

agreement of R5 by the 1st Respondent in accordance with the law.

35] Hence, in view of the above considered law and the judgements, I grant the

Petitioner the reliefs as prayed for in paragraphs (d) issuing Writ of Certiorari

quashing the certificate marked as P11(a) and (e) Writ of Mandamus directing

1st Respondent to issue a certificate naming the Petitioner as the lawful

successor and register it in the same folio which P11(a) registered, of the prayer

to the petition. The application of the Petitioner is allowed without costs.
