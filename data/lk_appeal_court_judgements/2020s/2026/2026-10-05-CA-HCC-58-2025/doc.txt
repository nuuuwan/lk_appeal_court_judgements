13. First and foremost, consistency in a prosecutrix’s statement serves as

a fundamental indicator of truthfulness. In cases of sexual abuse, it is

not uncommon for victims to initially confront the trauma through

varied accounts as they grapple with the emotional and psychological

implications of the experience.

14. However, when a prosecutrix’s account remains consistent across

different instances, whether in police statements, Court testimonies or

other settings, such consistency reinforces the validity of her claims.

Courts must recognise that, in situations laden with psychological

complexity, mere existence of a consistent narrative can be a powerful

testament in the absence of additional corroborative evidence.

15. Moreover, trustworthiness is a critical factor that influences the

evaluation of a prosecutrix’s testimony. A trustworthy prosecutrix is

10

forthcoming about her experiences and maintains a clear recollection

of events. This factor becomes particularly relevant in sexual abuse

cases, where societal scepticism can often cloud judgement regarding

victims. The governing principle should be that, in assessing credibility

of a prosecutrix, the Court should prioritise her assertiveness,

coherence, and the emotional sincerity with which she expresses her

ordeal.

16. In the case of Premasiri vs. Attorney General [2006] 3 Sri. LR 106, Eric

Basnayake J, has held,

“There is no rule that there must be corroboration in every case, before

a conviction can be allowed to stand. It is well settled law that a

conviction for the offence of rape can be based on the sole testimony of

the prosecutrix, if it is reliable, unimpeachable and there is no

in<irmity;”

17. Further, in the case of Sunil and Another vs. Attorney General [1986] 1

Sri. LR 230, Dheeraratne J, has held,

“I think it is perfectly legitimate for a judge, in a case of this nature,

to direct a jury that if they <ind the evidence of the complainant

so convincing, they could act on that evidence alone, even in the

absence of her evidence being corroborated.”

21. The appellant in his dock statement has asserted that he did not engage

22. However, despite the appellant suggesting these instances of third party

23. In the case of Pilippu Mandige Nalaka Krishantha Kumara Thisera v A.G

(CA87/2005) decided on 17.05.2007, Sisira de Abrew J, has held

“...I hold that whenever evidence given by a witness on a material point

is not challenged in cross examination, it has to be concluded that such

evidence is not disputed and is accepted by the opponent subject of

course to the quali<ication that the witness is a reliable witness.”

24. Further, in the case of Sivathasan vs. Attorney General [2021] 2 SLR

290, Kodagoda, J, has stated,

“It would therefore be seen that suggestions to prosecution witnesses

are very important, and inter-alia serve as an opportunity for the

defence to place before prosecution witnesses the position of the

defence. It enables the Court to take early cognizance of the position of

13

the defence and also enable prosecution witnesses to respond to the

defence position. That the defence placed before Court their position at

the <irst available opportunity also enables it to satisfy the ‘test of

spontaneity’. Therefore, it is of importance that the defence uses this

opportunity to speci<ically and in unambiguous terms indicate both to

the Court and to prosecution witnesses, the position of the defence.

Should the defence not make use of this opportunity and take up a

particular position for the <irst time during the case for the defence,

that position will suffer from the weakness of ‘belatedness’.”

25. In those circumstances I am not inclined to interfere with the

conviction, disputed judgement together with the sentencing order and

proceed to affirm the same. The substantive terms of rigorous

imprisonment shall run concurrently and begin from the date of

conviction, i.e. September 3rd, 2024.

26. I dismiss the appeal but make no order regarding costs.

Appeal dismissed.
