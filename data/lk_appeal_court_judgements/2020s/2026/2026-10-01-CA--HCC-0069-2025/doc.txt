Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentences dated 22.01.2025 delivered by the

Learned High Court Judge of Hambantota. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred

to as “the Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Hambantota on three

charges of committing grave sexual abuse against a boy of 9 years old, offences

punishable under Section 365B(2)(b) of the Penal Code, as amended by Acts No. 22 of

1995, No. 29 of 1998, and No. 16 of 2006.

2. At the trial, six witnesses, including the interpreter of the High Court, testified for the

prosecution. At the conclusion of the prosecution case, the Appellant gave evidence on

his own behalf and closed the case for the defence. Upon convicting the Appellant, the

learned Trial Judge imposed the sentences as follows:

3. In respect of each of the first, second, and third counts, the Appellant was sentenced to

eight years’ rigorous imprisonment and a fine of Rs. 5,000, with a default sentence of

six months’ imprisonment. The sentences of rigorous imprisonment were ordered to run

concurrently. In addition, the Appellant was ordered to pay compensation in the sum of

Rs. 75,000 to the victim, with a default sentence of one year’s simple imprisonment.

4. The grounds of appeal advanced by the Appellant are as follows:

a. PW1’s evidence was not corroborated by medical evidence; and

b. The accused-Appellant’s evidence is devoid of any contradictions and has not

Background to the appeal:

5. The victim is a boy who is presently 12 years of age. At the time of the alleged offences,

he was nine years old and attending school. The Appellant was a neighbour of the

victim, and according to the victim’s evidence, he referred to the Appellant as “Podi

Mama”. The offences set out in the indictment are alleged to have been committed by

the Appellant after taking the victim to his house at a time when the Appellant’s mother

was not at home. It is also evident from the evidence that, during the period relevant to

the incident, the victim’s father was employed as a driver at the Housing Authority in

Gampaha and returned home only once a month.

6. The victim’s evidence is that the Appellant subjected him to grave sexual abuse on

several occasions and that, on each such occasion, the victim’s mother was not at home

and he was taken to the Appellant’s house. The victim further stated that the Appellant

threatened to kill him if he disclosed the incidents of sexual abuse to anyone. When the

victim experienced pain in his genital area, he disclosed the incident to his mother, who

subsequently lodged a complaint with the police.

Whether the victim’s testimony has not been corroborated by the Judicial Medical

Officer’s findings?

7. In his testimony, the victim stated that, following the alleged incident of abuse by the

Appellant, he noticed bleeding and redness in his genital area. He further stated that

this prompted him to inform his mother of the incident, following which she

accompanied him to the police station and lodged a complaint.

8. Nevertheless, according to the Medico-Legal Report marked as P2, the Judicial Medical

Officer observed no injuries to the victim’s genitals. It is also noteworthy that the

Judicial Medical Officer did not testify before the High Court, and the Medico-Legal

Report was marked in evidence through the interpreter of the High Court.

9. According to the Medico-Legal Report, the victim was examined by the Judicial

Medical Officer on 12.03.2019. The evidence of PW2 is that the victim disclosed the

incident to her on 23.02.2019, while according to PW5, the complaint was lodged with

the police on 10.03.2019.

10. Thus, it appears that the complaint was made approximately 15 days after the last

alleged incident, and that the victim was examined by the JMO approximately 17 days

after the last alleged incident. It is also noteworthy that the JMO was not called to testify

before the High Court. However, the Medico-Legal Report prepared by him was

admitted in evidence by consent of both parties under Section 420 of the Code of

Criminal Procedure Act and marked in evidence through the interpreter of the High

Court.

11. The question to be determined is whether the absence of injuries to the genitals of the

victim, as observed by the JMO, undermines the credibility of the victim’s testimony.

A careful examination of the MLR does not completely rule out the possibility that the

acts alleged by the victim had occurred. The JMO’s finding was that he could not

observe any injuries or redness on the victim’s genitals. It is also significant that the

history provided to the JMO was given not by the victim, but by his mother.

12. According to the evidence of PW2, the victim initially informed her that he was

experiencing pain in his genital area and, when questioned about the cause, first stated

that he had struck himself against a tree. He subsequently disclosed to her what had

allegedly occurred. PW2 further stated that, when she examined the victim’s genital

area, she observed redness.

13. It is evident from the evidence of PW2 that she observed redness in the genital area of

the victim, but did not observe any bleeding. In these circumstances, having regard to

the lapse of time between the alleged incident and the date of the medical examination,

it is understandable that the JMO may not have observed any injury or redness on the

victim’s genitals. Furthermore, the evidence of the victim indicates that the Appellant

had subjected him to sexual abuse on several occasions. Although the victim testified

that he had sustained an injury and experienced bleeding from his genitals, PW2’s

evidence establishes that, when she examined the relevant area, she observed only

redness and no bleeding.

14. This discrepancy between the account given by the victim and the observations made

by PW2 is a matter that requires consideration when assessing the credibility and

reliability of the victim’s testimony. In my view, it indicates that the victim may have

exaggerated the extent of the physical injury when giving evidence before the High

Court.

15. Since the JMO was not called to testify, there was no opportunity to seek clarification

regarding his precise opinion. Nevertheless, it is significant that, in the Medico-Legal

Report, he did not completely rule out the possibility that the alleged acts had occurred.

16. The High Court in order to ascertain how and in what manner the incident had taken

place which led to the abuse of the victim, accepted the testimony of PW1 and PW2 to

be true, trustworthy and reliable. The learned High Court Judge has found that although

there had been a delay in making the complaint, yet the same, by itself, cannot be a

ground to reject or doubt the victim’s testimony as an afterthought or unbelievable.

17. No question was put by the accused to the victim in this regard, nor any suggestion was

put to him that he deliberately recorded a belated statement only to create evidence

against the accused.

18. Accordingly, the Trial Court vide its judgment and order dated 22.01.2025 held that the

prosecution had succeeded in proving its case beyond a reasonable doubt, and convicted

the Accused-Appellant for the offences punishable under Section 365 B (2)of the Penal

Code.

19. Since the Appellant has placed considerable reliance on the absence of medical

corroboration and contended that it would be unsafe to sustain the conviction solely on

the uncorroborated testimony of the victim, it becomes necessary to consider the legal

principles governing the assessment of evidence given by a child witness. In particular,

the question arises whether the uncorroborated testimony of a child witness, if found to

be credible and reliable upon a careful assessment of the evidence, is by itself sufficient

to sustain a conviction, or whether corroboration is an essential requirement. It is

therefore appropriate to examine the nature and evidentiary value of child testimony

and the circumstances in which a court may safely act upon such evidence in the

absence of corroboration.

20. It is trite law that corroboration of the testimony of a child witness is not a sine qua non,

but is rather a matter of caution and prudence. A child of tender years may be

particularly susceptible to tutoring or influence. However, that possibility, by itself, is

not a sufficient ground to reject the evidence of a child witness. The Court must subject

such evidence to careful and searching scrutiny and, in particular, consider whether

there is any reasonable possibility that the child has been tutored or otherwise

influenced. Accordingly, the evidence of a child witness must be evaluated with due

care and caution, having regard to the circumstances of each case.

21. Therefore, before proceeding to record the evidence of a minor, the learned Trial Judge

should conduct a preliminary examination of the child by putting appropriate questions

to ascertain whether the child is capable of understanding the questions put to him or

her and is able to give rational answers. The Judge must also satisfy himself that the

child understands the importance of speaking the truth. Such a preliminary examination

is necessary to determine whether the child possesses the requisite capacity to

understand the questions and to respond to them in a rational and meaningful manner

before his or her evidence is recorded.

22. In the present case, the learned Trial Judge put several questions to PW1 in order to

ascertain whether he was capable of understanding the nature of the questions put to

him and responding to them rationally. Only after satisfying himself that PW1 was able

to comprehend the questions and provide rational answers did the learned Trial Judge

proceed to record his evidence.

23. In Ratansinh Dalsukhbhai Nayak v. State of Gujarat reported in (2004) 1 SCC 64,

the Indian Supreme Court explained that although child witnesses are considered as

dangerous witnesses as they are pliable and liable to be influenced easily, shaped and

moulded, yet it is an accepted norm that if after careful scrutiny their testimony is found

to inspire confidence and be truthful, then there is no obstacle in accepting the evidence

of such child witness. The relevant observation reads as under:

“The decision on the question whether the child witness has sufficient intelligence

primarily rests with the trial Judge who notices his manners, his apparent

possession or lack of intelligence, and the said Judge may resort to any examination

which will tend to disclose his capacity and intelligence as well as his

understanding of the obligation of an oath. The decision of the trial court may,

however, be disturbed by the higher court if from what is preserved in the records,

it is clear that his conclusion was erroneous. This precaution is necessary because

child witnesses are amenable to tutoring and often live in a world of make-believe.

Though it is an established principle that child witnesses are dangerous witnesses

as they are pliable and liable to be influenced easily, shaped and moulded, but it is

also an accepted norm that if after careful scrutiny of their evidence the court comes

to the conclusion that there is an impress of truth in it, there is no obstacle in the

way of accepting the evidence of a child witness.”

24. In Panchhi v. State of U.P., reported in (1998) 7 SCC 177, the Supreme Court of India

held that the evidence of a child witness should not be outrightly rejected, but the

evidence must be evaluated carefully and with greater circumspection because a child

is susceptible to be swayed by what others tell him and an easy prey to tutoring.

25. In Suryanarayana v. State of Karnataka, reported in (2001) 9 SCC 129 held that the

evidence of a child witness who has withstood the test of cross-examination should not

be rejected per se if his testimony is found to be free from any infirmity. It reiterated

that corroboration to the testimony of a child witness is not a rule but a measure of

caution and prudence. The Court further held that while assessing the evidence of a

child witness, courts must rule out the possibility of tutoring. However, in the absence

of any allegation of tutoring or an attempt to use the child witness for ulterior purposes

by the prosecution, the courts must rely on the confidence-inspiring testimony of such

a witness in determining the guilt or innocence of the accused.

26. The Supreme Court of India in State of M.P. v. Ramesh in (2011) 4 SCC

786 summarized the principles pertaining to the appreciation of evidence of a child

witness as follows:

(i) First, it held that a child witness must be able to understand the sanctity of giving

evidence on oath and the import of the questions that were being put to him. The

evidence of a child witness must reveal that he was able to discern between right

and wrong, and the court may ascertain his suitability as a witness through either

cross-examination or by putting questions to the child in terms of Section 165 of the

Evidence Act or by determining the same from the evidence or testimony of the child

itself.

(ii) Secondly, if the evidence of the child explains the relevant events of the crime

without improvements or embellishments, and the same inspire confidence of the

court, his deposition does not require any corroboration whatsoever.

(iii) Thirdly, even if the courts find that the child witness had been tutored, even then the

statement of a child witness can be relied upon if the tutored part can be separated

from the untutored part and the remaining untutored part inspires confidence. In

such cases, the untutored part can be believed or at least taken into consideration

for the purpose of corroboration as in the case of a hostile witness.

(iv) Lastly, it held that an inference as to whether child has been tutored or not, can be

drawn from the contents of his deposition. If the deposition of a child witness

inspires the confidence of the court and there is no embellishment or improvement

therein, the court may rely upon his evidence. The evidence of a child witness must

be evaluated more carefully with greater circumspection because he is susceptible

to tutoring. Only in case there is evidence on record to show that a child has been

tutored, the court can reject his statement partly or fully and look for corroboration.

27. In The State of Madhya Pradesh v. Balveer Singh (2025 INSC 261), the Supreme

Court of India held:

“It is clear that the evidence of a child witness for all purposes is deemed to be on

the same footing as any other witness as long the child is found to be competent to

testify. The only precaution which the court should take while assessing the evidence

of a child witness is that such witness must be a reliable one due to the susceptibility

of children by their falling prey to tutoring. However, this in no manner means that

the evidence of a child must be rejected outrightly at the slightest of discrepancy,

rather what is required is that the same is evaluated with great circumspection.

While appreciating the testimony of a child witness the courts are required to assess

whether the evidence of such witness is its voluntary expression and not borne out

of the influence of others and whether the testimony inspires confidence. At the same

time, one must be mindful that there is no rule requiring corroboration to the

testimony of a child witness before any reliance is placed on it. The insistence of

corroboration is only a measure of caution and prudence that the courts may

exercise if deemed necessary in the peculiar facts and circumstances of the case.”

28. In the present case, although the victim was subjected to cross-examination, his

testimony does not disclose any material inconsistencies or contradictions that would

undermine its credibility. It was suggested to the victim during cross-examination that

he was merely repeating in Court what his mother had tutored him to say. The victim,

however, denied this suggestion.

29. The evidence of PW2, the victim’s mother, discloses that there had been some

altercations between her and the Appellant over several matters. However, it would be

difficult to infer that such disagreements would have provided PW2 with a sufficient

motive to tutor PW1 to falsely implicate the Appellant in an offence of this nature. It is

also evident that, notwithstanding those disagreements, the Appellant maintained a

close association with the victim’s family. In fact, the Appellant had stood as a surety

for the bond executed when the victim’s parents obtained a financial facility to purchase

a vehicle.

30. In these circumstances, having regard to the nature and quality of the victim’s evidence,

I find it difficult to accept the suggestion that the victim had been tutored by his mother

to falsely implicate the Appellant in an offence of such gravity.

31. The Appellant also contended that the learned Trial Judge had failed to apply the

principles governing the evaluation of his evidence and that, consequently, the rejection

of his evidence was erroneous. In his evidence-in-chief, the Appellant merely denied

the allegations against him. It is noteworthy that, although he stated that there had been

some disputes between him and the victim’s mother, he did not suggest that the

allegation against him had been made as a result of those disputes.

32. Significantly, the Appellant admitted that, on one occasion, he had gone to the victim’s

residence and scolded the victim’s mother. However, according to his evidence, that

incident occurred after the alleged offences. He further stated that, although he had

demanded Rs. 17,000 from the victim’s parents in respect of a sum of Rs. 150,000 that

they had borrowed from him, no altercation or dispute had arisen on that account. More

importantly, the Appellant admitted that, notwithstanding whatever differences had

arisen between them, he continued to associate with the victim’s family as though they

were his own siblings and that they had always remained on good terms.

33. Hence, even if the learned Trial Judge did not consider the Appellant’s evidence at

length, such omission has not adversely affected the ultimate conclusion reached in the

case, nor has it caused any substantial prejudice to the Appellant. It is significant to note

that, when the Appellant’s evidence is considered in its entirety, it does not raise any

reasonable doubt regarding the prosecution case. In these circumstances, I am of the

view that the learned Trial Judge cannot be faulted for rejecting the Appellant’s

evidence and proceeding to convict him.

34. The learned Trial Judge has adequately analyzed and evaluated the evidence of both the

prosecution and the defence before finding the Appellant guilty of the charge set out in

the indictment. It is well established that, having had the opportunity to observe the

demeanour of the witnesses and assess their credibility at first hand, an appellate court

is slow to interfere with the findings of a trial Judge, unless such findings are shown to

be perverse or vitiated by some patent error.

35. In Alwis vs. Piyasena Fernando (1993) 1 Sri. L R 119, it was held that:

"It is well established that findings of primary facts by a trial Judge who hears and

sees witnesses are not to be lightly disturbed on appeal."

36. Similar sentiments were articulated in Dharmasiri vs. Republic of Sri Lanka [2010] 2

Sri. L.R.241, where it was held that:

“Credibility of a witness is mainly a matter for the trial Judge; the Court of Appeal

will not lightly disturb the findings of a trial Judge with regard to the credibility of

a witness unless such findings of the trial Judge are manifestly wrong.”

37. For the foregoing reasons, I find no basis to interfere with the findings of the Learned

Trial Judge. Accordingly, I affirm the conviction and sentence imposed by the Learned

High Court Judge of Hambantota on 22.01.2025. In the circumstances, the appeal is

dismissed.

38. Considering all the circumstances, I order the sentence to be operative from the date of

conviction, namely, 22.01.2025.
