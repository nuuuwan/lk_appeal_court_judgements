Judgment

1. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as “the Appellant”) was indicted

before the High Court of Tangalle on one count of murder and sixteen counts of

attempted murder, offences punishable under Sections 296 and 300 of the Penal

Code, respectively. The trial was conducted before the learned High Court Judge of

Tangalle without a jury.

2. At the conclusion of the trial, the Appellant was found guilty on the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th,

7th, 8th, 9th, 10th, 11th, 13th, 14th, 15th, and 17th counts and was accordingly convicted

and sentenced to 3 years’ rigorous imprisonment in respect of the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 8th,

9th, 10th, 11th, 12th, 13th, 14th, 15th, and 17th counts, together with a fine of Rs. 5000/-

on each count, with 3 months’ rigorous imprisonment in default of payment of the

fine. Further, the Appellant was sentenced to death in respect of the first count.

3. It is against the said conviction and sentence that the Appellant has preferred the

present appeal. The Appellant relies on the following grounds;

a. Has the prosecution failed to prove the case against the Appellant beyond

reasonable doubt;

21. It is also significant that Ajith Priyantha, who was allegedly involved in the quarrel

preceding the incident and was named as a prosecution witness, could not be traced

following his hospitalization as a result of the incident. His absence deprived the

court of the opportunity to hear his account of the alleged quarrel and the events

surrounding the explosion. In the circumstances, his unexplained absence,

considered together with the evidence of PW1 and PW6, warrants careful

consideration when evaluating the prosecution’s case.

22. These matters, when considered cumulatively with the inconsistencies in the

evidence of PW1 and PW6, give rise to legitimate questions concerning the

sequence of events, the identity of the person who threw the explosive device, and

whether the prosecution has established the Appellant’s involvement beyond

reasonable doubt.

23. Notably, the infirmities in the prosecution case relate, inter alia, to the manner in

which PW1 and PW6 claimed to have identified the Appellant as the person who

threw the bomb towards the crowd. Although both witnesses implicated the

Appellant in their testimony before the Trial Court, neither referred to the Appellant

in the history provided to the JMO, as reflected in their respective MLRs.

24. Furthermore, PW1 had failed to mention the Appellant’s name in his first and second

statements to the police, identifying him only in his third statement. These omissions

and the subsequent identification of the Appellant raise legitimate concern regarding

the consistency and reliability of the identification evidence.

25. It is noteworthy that the Appellant was not a stranger to PW1. In these

circumstances, had PW1 correctly identified the Appellant at the scene of the

incident, there would have been no apparent reason for him to omit the Appellant’s

name from his first two statements to the police. No plausible explanation was

forthcoming from PW1 for this omission. This unexplained failure to identify the

Appellant at the earliest opportunity raises a reasonable doubt as to the correctness

of PW1’s identification. In my view, such doubt renders the conviction unsafe.

26. Moreover, in the absence of corroborative evidence from other witnesses who were

present at the scene, the evidence identifying the Appellant as the perpetrator

requires particularly careful scrutiny. In the circumstances, the question arises as to

whether the identification evidence of PW1 and PW6, when considered in its

entirety and in the context of other discrepancies in the prosecution case, is

sufficiently reliable to establish the identity of the perpetrator beyond reasonable

doubt.

27. This is a pivotal point in the trial given that PW1 and PW6 are the sole eyewitnesses

to the crime. The issue of identification is paramount and necessitates rigorous

scrutiny, especially in a criminal trial where the stakes are exceedingly high and

could significantly impact the prosecution case.

28. In Dassanayake Lekamlage Somapala alias Gangabada Sudu and Other Vs.

Attorney General, 1 CA 208-2010/2011, decided on 02.09.2014, by Anil

Goonaratne J., held that;

“A very important aspect of a criminal trial is that the perpetrators of the crime

need to be identified with certainty. Absence of identity of accused would be fatal

to the prosecution case. The learned High Court Judge has merely referred to

the items of evidence of the declaration (P5) but has not considered its probative

value.”

29. In Opatha Widanapathiranege Wasantha and 3 Others Vs. Attorney General,

CA/179/2006, decided on 29.04.2010, by Ranjith Silva, J, held that;

“The Learned Trial Judge ought to have followed the standard guidelines with

regard to his directions to the jury. On the issue of identification evidence the

judges must give accurate directions regarding the identification evidence and

direct the jury that they must be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the

accused were correctly identified and give the benefit of any doubt to the

accused. The Jury must be directed as to the possibility of a mistaken identity

even by honest witnesses and if they cannot make up their minds as to whether

the witnesses were lying or mistaken the accused must be given the benefit of the

doubt and should be acquitted. The trial judge must direct the jury to examine

closely the circumstances under which the identification came to be made and

the means of identification. The trial judge should direct the jury on the rules

laid down in Rex v. Turnbull.”

30. The Turnbull guidelines provided in the case of Regina Vs. Turnbull (1977) QB

224, laid down important guidelines for judges as to the manner in which they

should assess evidence in relation to a situation where the identity of an accused

person comes into question. The said guidelines read as follows;

It requires a trial judge to be mindful that;

i. Caution is required to avoid the risk of injustice.

ii. A witness who is honest may be wrong even if they are convinced, they are

right.

iii. A witness who is convincing may still be wrong.

iv. More than one witness may be wrong.

v. A witness who recognizes the defendant even when the witness knows the

defendant well may be wrong.

Some of the circumstances a Judge should direct the jury to examine in order to find

out whether a correct identification has been made include;

i. The length of time the accused was observed by the witnesses.

ii. The distance the witness was from the accused.

iii. The state of the light.

iv. The length of time elapsed between the original observation and the

subsequent identification.

31. In Sucha Singh Vs. State of Punjab, (2003) 7 SCC 643, it was held inter alia:

“.......normal discrepancies in evidence are those which are due to normal errors

of observation, normal errors of memory due to lapse of time, due to mental

disposition such as shock and horror at the time of occurrence and those are

always there however honest and truthful a witness may be. Material

discrepancies are those which are not normal, and not expected of a normal

person. Courts have to label the category to which a discrepancy may be

categorized. While normal discrepancies do not corrode the credibility of a

party's case, material discrepancies do so......Where it is not feasible to separate

truth from falsehood, because grain and chaff are inextricably mixed up, and in

the process of separation an absolutely new case has to be reconstructed by

divorcing essential details presented by the prosecution completely from the

context and the background against which they are made, the only available

course to be made is to discard the evidence in toto.”

32. Upon scrutinizing the evidence of PW1 and PW6, along with the above

observations, I am of the view that their testimonies regarding identification cast

doubt due to a lack of cogent evidence concerning the identification of the

Appellant. In this instance, the learned High Court Judge has convicted the

Appellant of the alleged offences primarily based on the evidence of PW1 and PW6.

However, we find that this evidence is insufficient to establish the prosecution’s case

beyond reasonable doubt.

33. Further, since there exists a serious doubt as to whether PW1 and PW6 actually

witnessed the alleged incident in the manner they have described, their testimonies

regarding the Appellant as the real perpetrator become highly doubtful and unsafe

to rely upon. In such circumstances, the evidence of PW1 and PW6, being

uncorroborated on this crucial aspect and suffering from the issues of credibility and

reliability, cannot be accepted as a safe basis to sustain a finding against the

Appellant. Therefore, it is my view that such identification cannot be regarded as

sufficient to establish, beyond reasonable doubt, the participation of the Appellant

in the alleged incident.

34. Moreover, it was contended on behalf of the Appellant that the learned High Court

Judge failed to properly evaluate the dock statement of the Appellant. However, I

shall not consider this ground of appeal, as the above-mentioned grounds of appeal

are sufficient to dispose of this appeal.

35. Upon careful consideration of the totality of the evidence and the material

infirmities discussed above, it is my considered view that the conviction of the

Appellant is unsafe and unsustainable in law. When the Appellant is facing a capital

charge, every point in favour of the Appellant needs to be considered. Accordingly,

the conviction and sentence imposed on the Appellant are set aside, and the

Appellant is acquitted of all charges.

36. The appeal is accordingly allowed.
