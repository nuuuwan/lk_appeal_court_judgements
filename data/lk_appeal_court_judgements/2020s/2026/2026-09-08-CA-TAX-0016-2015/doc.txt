This is a Case Stated submitted for the opinion of this Court by the Tax Appeals Commission (hereinafter

referred to as “TAC”) under Section 11 of Act No.23 of 2011 (as amended). The Appellant, NDB Venture

Investments (Pvt) Ltd, is a limited liability company (hereinafter referred to as the “Appellant Company”)

incorporated in Sri Lanka. According to the Memorandum and Articles of Association, the primary objects

for which the company is established are:

1. To carry on the business of a venture capital company and an investment company in all aspects.

2. To participate in the growth of the venture capital business in Sri Lanka.

3. To take part, assist, encourage, sponsor, and facilitate the participation of different entities.

4. To carry on the business of an investment advisor

5. To provide financial, technical, and management support for the advancement of new products.

The Appellant Company was granted a tax exemption under Section 22DDD of the Inland Revenue Act

No. 28 of 1979 (as amended) for a period of ten years, commencing on 05th February 1992 and ending on

05th February 2002. The exemption applied to business income falling within the scope of Section 3(a) of

the Inland Revenue Act No. 28 of 1979 and No. 38 of 2000.

The Appellant Company has submitted their return for the period of 2005/2006 and 2006/2007, claiming

the following income as exempt income, that is, dividend income, profits on the sale of investments, interest

income, management fees, and legal expenses, and brought forward losses from the year 2004/2005.

However, the Assessor did not accept the returns on certain aspects, acted under Section 163 of the said

Act, and issued an assessment. Appellant Company appealed to the Commissioner General of Inland

Revenue (hereinafter referred to as the “Respondent”) under Section 165 (1) of the Inland Revenue Act

No.10 of 2006.

The Respondent, after considering the statements, records and evidence of both parties, determined that the

profits derived from the dividend and profit on the sale of investment in shares are liable to income tax in

the years 2005/2006 and 2006/2007 and revised the Assessment issued for the said years.

The Appellant Company, being dissatisfied with the aforesaid determination of the Respondent dated 18th

October 2010, preferred an appeal to the Board of Review on 05th November 2010. In the meantime, the

TAC was established under the Tax Appeals Commission Act, No. 23 of 2011. Pursuant to Section 10 of

the said Act, the present appeal was transferred to the TAC.

Upon considering the submissions made on behalf of both the Appellant and the Respondent at the hearing

of the appeal, the TAC confirmed the determination of the Respondent dated 08th September 2015. The

principal ground upon which the TAC made its determination against the Appellant Company was that the

income derived from dividends and profits arising from the sale of shares constituted gross receipts from

trade or business and was, therefore, subject to income tax.

The Appellant, being dissatisfied with the aforesaid determination of the TAC, by its communication dated

29th September 2015, requested the TAC to refer the Case Stated for the opinion of the Court of Appeal.

The Case Stated consists of eight questions of law, formulated by the TAC and forwarded to the Court of

Appeal for its opinion.

These questions of law, reproduced verbatim, are as follows;

1. Is the determination of the appeal made by the TAC time-barred by the operation of law?

2. Do dividends received by the Appellant form part of its statutory income?

3. Are gains arising on the sale of shares which have been held by the Appellant for a period in

excess of two years not liable to income tax?

4. Are dividends received by the Appellant from Board of Investment of Sri Lanka companies and

any gains arising on the sales of shares not liable to income tax in the hands of the Appellant, if

there are express stipulations to that effect in the relevant Board of Investment of Sri Lanka

agreement?

5. Is the interest income received by the Appellant taxable at the rate applicable to venture capital

companies?

6. Are management fees and legal expenses incurred by the Appellant deductible in full?

This position finds support from the case of The Ceylon Financial Investments Co., Ltd. Vs The

The Appellant Company is a Venture Capital Company, and its object is to invest money in shares of related

It is to be noted that dividend income is part of business income in the case of the Appellant Company. That

is, for the Appellant, dividend income is not aggregated as a separate source of statutory income, but is

included as part of business income. However, in the case of ordinary companies other than Investment

Companies, Section 63 of the Inland Revenue Act is applied. It is important to understand the distinction

between the source of profits from the business of an Investment Company and the source of dividend

According to the TAC decision, it considered that dividend income is part of business income under Section

3 of the said Act, and further held that Section 63 does not apply when the dividend income is earned as

part of statutory income. The reason given by TAC was that the Appellant is an Investment Company that

earns profit from investing its money in shares.

They relied on Ceylon Financial Investments Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income Tax, (Supra). This Court

has to consider whether the Respondent has correctly identified that the dividend income in question can

business of investment.

Upon perusing the brief, I find that the principal business of the Appellant Company is the investment of

funds in shares, and that dividends constitute its principal source of income. In the instant case, there is no

doubt that the Appellant Company is engaged in the business of investing in shares of related and other

companies, in accordance with the business objects of the Company as set out in its Articles of Association.

In these circumstances, it is clear that the Appellant’s investments in shares were made in the course of

carrying on its business. Accordingly, the income derived therefrom, including dividend income, ought to

be treated as income arising from a business falling within the scope of Section 3(a) of the Inland Revenue

Act.

To be eligible under Section 63 of No.10 of 2006, the Appellant should prove that the so-called dividend

received should fall under Section 3(e) and not under Section 3 (a). The Appellant Company has failed to

prove that investing in shares in other companies falls outside Section 3(a) of the Inland Revenue Act,

which means that the principal source of income is not derived from dividends.

On the other hand, the Respondent has established that the principal source of income of the Appellant

Company is derived from dividends. The letter of intimation dated 04th August 2007 indicates that 90% of

I note that the TAC determination was on 8th September 2015 and the said amendment No.09 of 2015 was

It is necessary to examine whether the amending Acts have any retrospective effect and, if so, to what

extent.

Regarding retrospective effect, it was discussed by Priyantha Jayawardena PC, J, in Seylan Bank PLC v.

The Commissioner General of Inland Revenue, SC Appeal No. 46/2016, decided on 16.12.2021, his

lordship held that;

“Retrospective operation of procedural laws

Article 75 of the Constitution confers power on the legislature to make laws, including laws having

retrospective effect. The general principle of interpretation of statutes is described in the Latin

maxim ‘lex proscipit non respicit’ (the law looks forward, not backward). Thus, amendments made

to substantive laws have a prospective effect, unless its effect has been made retrospective by

express provision in the Act or by necessary implication. On the other hand, amendments made to

procedural laws are presumed to have retrospective effect, unless its effect has been made

prospective by express provision in the Act or by necessary implication, or such construction is

textually not possible.

Page 13 of 19

A similar view was expressed in Maxwell on Interpretation of Statutes, 12th edition, at page 222,

which stated;

“It is perfectly settled law that if the legislature intended to frame a new procedure that,

instead of proceeding in this form or that, you should proceed in another and a different

way, clearly then bygone transactions are to be sued for and enforced according to the

new form of procedure. Alterations in the form of procedure are always retrospective

unless there is some good reason or other why they should not be.”

Further, the Supreme Court of India in Hitendra Vishnu Thakur & Others v State of Maharashtra

& Others (1994) 4 SCC 602 held;

“(i) A statute which affects substantive rights is presumed to be prospective in operation

unless made retrospective, either expressly or by necessary intendment, whereas a statute

which merely affects procedure, unless such a construction is textually impossible, is

presumed to be retrospective in its application, should not be given an extended meaning

and should be strictly confined to its clearly defined limits;

(ii) Law relating to forum and limitation is procedural in nature, whereas law relating to

right of action and right of appeal, even though remedial, is substantive in nature;

(iii) Every litigant has a vested right in substantive law, but no such right exists in

procedural law;

(iv) A procedural statute should not generally speaking be applied retrospectively, where

the result would be to create new disabilities or obligations or to impose new duties in

respect of transactions already accomplished; and

(v) A statute which not only changes the procedure but also creates new rights and

liabilities shall be construed to be prospective in operation unless otherwise provided,

either expressly or by necessary implication. [Emphasis added]”

His Lordship further held that, if the aforesaid amendments to the principal Act were procedural in nature,

the Court could presume that they were intended to have retrospective effect.

In Handunwala Devage Simon Fernando V Ranepura Hector Jayasiri and Others; SC Appeal

No.134/2014, decided on 14.07.2020, Amarasekara J, addressing the retrospective application of a statute,

observed that,

‘Therefore, it is necessary to examine whether the amending Act has any retrospective effect and,

if so to what extent.

In Colombo Apothecaries Limited Vs E.A. Wijesooriya 73 NLR 05 it was stated that “A statute may

be brought into operation after the date of its enactment and it can also, provided the language is

clear and unambiguous, be made to operate before enactment.”

It was held in Grocock Vs Grocock (1920) 1 KB 1 DC at page 9 that “ In the absence of an

expressed intention that a statute shall have a retrospective operation, the rule is nova constutio

futuris formam imponere debet non praeteritis.”( Every new rule ought to prescribe a form for

future, not for past acts; a new law ought to impose form on what is to follow, not on the past. )

Even on behalf of the Original Creditor, while referring to Grocock Vs Grocock (1920) 1 KB 1 DC,

R Vs Chandra Dharma (1905) 2 KB 335 and Director Public Prosecution Vs Lamb (1941) 2 All

words are clear, precise and quite free from ambiguity”. It was further submitted that statutes have

retrospective effect when the declared intention of the legislature is clearly and unequivocally

manifest from the language employed in the particular law or in the context of connected

provisions.

In this regard following decisions and texts have also been brought to the attention of this court.

“The rule against retrospective operation is a presumption only. It may be overcome, not only by

express words in the Act but also by circumstances sufficiently strong to displace it.” -- Vide page

225 of the 12th Edition of Maxwell on Interpretation of Statutes by P. St. J. Langan referring to

Maxwell on the Interpretation of Statutes (12th Edition) at pages 215 and 216 –

“Upon the presumption that the legislature does not intend what is unjust rests the leaning against

giving certain statutes a retrospective operation. They are construed as operating only in cases or

on facts which come into existence after the statutes were passed unless a retrospective effect is

clearly intended. It is a fundamental rule of English law that no statute shall be construed to have

a retrospective operation unless such a construction appears very clearly in the terms of the Act,

or arises by necessary and distinct implication.

The statement of the law contained in the preceding paragraph has been “so frequently quoted

with approval that it now itself enjoys almost judicial authority.”

One of the most well-known statements of the rule regarding retrospectivity is contained in this

passage from the judgment of R.S. Wright J. in Re Athlumney;

“Perhaps no rule of construction is more firmly established than this – that a retrospective

operation is not to be given to a statute so as to impair an existing right or obligation, otherwise

than as regards matter of procedure, unless that effect cannot be avoided without doing violence

to the language of the enactment. If the enactment is expressed in language which is fairly capable

of either interpretation, it ought to be construed as prospective only.” The rule has, in fact, two

aspects, for it “involves another and subordinate rule, to the effect that a statute is not to be

construed so as to have a greater retrospective operation than its language renders necessary.”

If however, the language or the dominant intention of the enactment so demands, the Act must be

construed so as to have retrospective operation, for “the rule against the retrospective effect of

statutes is not a rigid or inflexible rule but is one to be applied always in the light of the language

of the statute and the subject matter with which the statute is dealing.”

Hence, I hold that the amendments introduced in 2014 and 2015 were not intended to operate

retrospectively, as the State has no obligation to revise taxes already assessed or charged. Accordingly, I

hold that the said amendments to Section 63 have no application to the present matter and do not have

retrospective effect. Therefore, I hold that the dividends received by the Appellant Company constitute

statutory income and are liable to be treated accordingly.

the factual and legal circumstances of the present case are distinguishable from those considered in CA

TAX 20/2022 and CA TAX 21/2022.

For the aforesaid reasons, I hold that the decisions in both cases have no bearing on the present case.

Question of Law No. 03 is ‘Are gains arising on the sale of shares which have been held by the Appellant

for a period in excess of two years not liable to income tax?’

In this matter, as I have already dealt with Question of Law No. 02, it is evident that the Company had

declared the sale of shares as its main source of income under Section 3(a) of the Inland Revenue Act and

had already enjoyed a tax exemption in respect of such trading income for a period of ten years. Therefore,

in terms of Section 38(d) of the Inland Revenue Act, the profit arising from the sale of shares could not be

exempted from tax. The exemption could only be granted in respect of separate sources of income.

It is pertinent to refer to the relevant portion of the determination, wherein the TAC has decided this issue.

Page 22 of X3,

“It was also submitted by the Representative for the Appellant that the gains arising on the sale of

shares held for a period in excess of two years are not liable for income tax and referred to section

14(1) (xvii) of the Inland Revenue Act, No. 38 of 2000(as amended) and section 38 (D) of the Inland

Revenue Act, No. 38 of 2000 as amended by Inland Revenue (Amendment) Act, No. 12 of

2004. In respect of the year of assessment 2006/2007, Representative referred to section 44 of the

Inland Revenue Act, No. 10 of 2006, (as amended) and submitted that gains received from the sale

of shares are not liable to income tax by operation of law.”

Further held that

“Even in the case of profits on the sale of shares, like in the case of

dividends, exemptions are granted for the separate sources. Therefore, as stated above, in the case

of dividends and profits on the sale of shares from investment in shares should be considered as

Gross Receipts from trade or business in terms of section 3(a) of the Inland Revenue Act, for the

purpose of computing statutory income.”

It should be noted that the Appellant Company is an investment company, and its main object involves the

buying and eventual sale of shares. Therefore, any gains arising from the sale of such shares constitute

regular business profits and are liable to tax at the standard income tax rates.

Page 17 of 19

The Court observes that the intention underlying the acquisition of the shares is a material consideration.

In the present case, the primary intention of the Appellant Company, an Investment Company that invests

money in shares at the time of acquisition, was to earn profits. Accordingly, any profits arising from the

subsequent sale of such shares cannot be regarded as constituting a separate source of income.

Consequently, such profits fall within the ambit of Section 3(a) of the Inland Revenue Act.

Accordingly, the answer to Question of Law No. 03 is in the negative. I observe that the TAC has treated

the proceeds from the sale of shares as gross receipts arising from ordinary trading profit/ business income

within the meaning of Section 3 of the said Act.

Question of Law No. 04 is ‘Are dividends received by the Appellant from Board of Investment of Sri Lanka

companies and any gains arising on the sales of shares not liable to income tax in the hands of the

Appellant, if there are express stipulations to that effect in the relevant Board of Investment of Sri Lanka

agreement?’

This question has already been answered in relation to Question of Law No. 02. I am also of the view that

the TAC has rightly concluded that the Appellant failed to produce the relevant documents to establish

whether the dividends received were from BOI companies.

The general rule is that a party who seeks to claim an exemption bears the burden of establishing the facts

necessary to support such exemption and must adduce sufficient evidence in that regard. I am also mindful

that the amendments to the relevant section have no retrospective effect. Accordingly, the answer to

Question of Law No. 04 is in the negative.

Question of Law No. 5 is ‘Is the interest income received by the Appellant taxable at the rate applicable to

venture capital companies?’

The TAC has rightly taken into consideration the fact that, upon the expiry of the ten-year tax exemption

period, the applicable rate of tax, in terms of Part IB of the Second Schedule to the Inland Revenue Act no

38 of 2000(as amended by Act No. 37 of 2003), was 20%. It should be noted that the income earned by a

venture capital company from fixed deposits and Treasury bills does not fall within the scope of the

exemption. The exemption is available only in respect of income derived from investments made in the

course of venture capital activities involving an element of risk. Accordingly, the assessment imposed by

the Respondent is correct.

Question of Law No. 6 is “Are management fees and legal expenses incurred by the Appellant deductible

in full?”

Page 18 of 19

In this matter, the Respondent allowed a deduction of 10% of the Company's gross receipts as management

fees for the years of assessment 2005/2006 and 2006/2007, together with the legal expenses claimed.

The Respondent has stated that the Appellant has failed to substantiate, by way of evidence or supporting

documents, the management fees or legal expenses claimed. Even at this stage before the TAC, the

Appellant has failed to produce any documentary evidence in support of such claims. Therefore, the answer

to Question of Law No. 6 is negative.

Questions of Law No 7 and 8 are “Is the amount assessed, as confirmed by the TAC, against the weight of

evidence, excessive and without lawful justification?” and “In view of the facts and circumstances of the

case, did the TAC err in law when it came to the conclusion that it did?”

Since I have already answered Questions of Law Nos. 2 and 6 in the negative, I hold that the TAC has

correctly arrived at its conclusion in affirming the decision of the Respondent. Hence, the answers to

Questions of Law No 7 and 8 are negative.

For the reasons set out above, this Court holds that the TAC did not err in law in reaching its conclusion.
