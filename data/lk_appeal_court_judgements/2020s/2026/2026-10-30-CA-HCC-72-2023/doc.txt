12. Discrepancies in statements or testimony refer to inconsistencies or

variations in accounts provided by individuals regarding an event or a

circumstance. Material discrepancies are those that hold significant

implications for the case at hand. These inconsistencies can alter the

meaning of the testimony, influence the credibility of the witness, or

directly impact the outcome of the proceeding.

13. For example, if a witness testified about an event that had occurred but

subsequently changes his account about the nature of such event, this

inconsistency can be classified as material. The nature of an event can

potentially affect the viability of a prosecution’s argument. Therefore,

material discrepancies have to be closely scrutinised to assess their

relevance to the evidentiary framework.

9

14. Determining whether a discrepancy is material involves a careful

evaluation of its context and the evidence as a whole. In legal contexts,

the integrity of testimony is paramount to achieving justice and truth.

When a testimony is fraught with material discrepancies, it undermines

these foundational goals, leading to a lot of negative consequences.

15. The legal system relies on accurate and credible testimonies to establish

facts and render decisions. When a witness presents conflicting

testimonies/narratives, the reliability of such testimony/narrative is

called into question. In legal settings each inconsistency can introduce

reasonable doubt, creating an environment where truth becomes

obscured. Moreover, the individual credibility of the witness is severely

compromised in the face of material discrepancies.

16. A witness is often seen as the cornerstone of evidence. If his account is

found to be inconsistent or contradictory, it can lead to questions

regarding his reliability.

17. In the case of Padmatilake (SGT) vs. Director General, Commission to

Investigate Allegations of Bribery/Corruption [2009] 2 Sri. LR 151,

Chandra Ekanayake J., has held,

“Credibility of prosecution witnesses should be subject to judicial evaluation

in totality and not isolated scrutiny by the Judge. When a witness makes

inconsistent statements in their evidence either at one stage or at two stages,

the testimony of such witnesses is unreliable and in the absence of special

circumstances no conviction can be based on the testimony of such witnesses.”

18. Further, in the case of Sucha Singh Vs. State of Punjab, (2003) 7 SCC

643, it has been held,

“...normal discrepancies in evidence are those which are due to normal errors

of observation, normal errors of memory due to lapse of time, due to mental

10

disposition such as shock and horror at the time of occurrence and those are

always there however honest and truthful a witness may be. Material

discrepancies are those which are not normal and not expected of a normal

person. Courts have to label the category to which a discrepancy may be

categorized. While normal discrepancies do not corrode the credibility of a

party's case, material discrepancies do so... Where it is not feasible to separate

truth from falsehood, because grain and chaff are inextricably mixed up, and

in the process of separation an absolutely new case has to be reconstructed

by divorcing essential details presented by the prosecution completely from

the context and the background against which they are made, the only

available course to be made is to discard the evidence in toto.”

21. Regarding the statement or the short history provided by the patient,

i.e. PW01, to Dr. Senasinghe, there has been no mention of the appellant

allegedly fondling her breasts or engaging in intercrural sex with her.

Similarly, concerning the note/letter marked පැ 02, PW01 has not

recorded any incident stated in the charges. Such discrepancies being

regarding the incidents described in the charges are material. Such

discrepancies are material as they can influence the perception of the

events and the credibility of the claims made. When the details

surrounding these incidents are inconsistent, they raise questions

about the validity of the testimony presented.

22. The learned Deputy Solicitor General has sought to clarify the

discrepancies referred to above by highlighting portions of the

testimony of PW01. The learned Deputy Solicitor General has

contended that PW01’s mother has translated the statement given by

PW01 to the consultant Judicial Medical Officer. Furthermore, it has

been suggested that the mother may have withheld certain details as a

means of damage control. In addition, that PW01 has indicated

uncertainty regarding whether she provided detailed or brief written

12

notification/letter to her teachers. That this raises questions about the

completeness about the information communicated to the relevant

parties.

23. Dr. Senasinghe has not indicated that the statement made by PW01 was

translated by PW01’s mother in a summarised manner. Furthermore,

the prosecution has not included the mother of PW01 as a witness, nor

presented her testimony to establish this fact. Additionally, regarding

the note or letter authored by PW01, the relevant teachers have not

testified that they instructed PW01 to summarise the alleged incidents

involving the appellant. In fact, it appears that PW01 had been directed

to present the facts in a detailed written format.

24. Moreover, it is important to note that PW01’s claims have emerged after

a considerable delay. This significant passage of time arises the

possibility that these claims may have been an afterthought rather than

an immediate recollection of events.

25. Consequently, the contention made by the learned Deputy Solicitor

General must be regarded as weak and unsupported.

26. Based on the findings outlined above, it is clear that the sixth ground

of appeal does not require further examination. The first to fifth grounds

of appeal support the Court’s conclusions, making additional

discussion on the sixth ground redundant.

27. In those circumstances, I am inclined to interfere with the conviction,

disputed judgement, together with the sentencing order, and proceed

to acquit the appellant of the charges, and set aside the disputed

judgement together with the sentencing order.

Appeal allowed but I make no order regarding costs.
