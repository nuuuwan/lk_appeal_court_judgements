Judgement

Introduction

The Petitioner is a body corporate incorporated under the Companies Act, No. 07 of 2007, and

therefore, is capable of suing and being sued in its corporate name. Presently, the Petitioner

manages and runs the operations of an estate called "Craig Estate" of Bandarawela, which is in

extent of approximately 281.99 Hectares.

The Petitioner by this Application challenges the second acquisition notice and immediate

possession taken thereof marked as P11, as there had been a first acquisition in respect of 11.99

Acres from and out of the said land on the basis of public purpose, and thereafter, the 1st

Respondent has initiated to take over another Two Acres from and out of the said Estate,

directing the 3rd Respondent to take steps on that behalf. In those circumstances, the Petitioner

challenges the second acquisition process initiated by the 1st Respondent through the 3rd

Respondent.

The 1st Respondent is the Minister of Lands, while the 2nd Respondent is the Secretary thereof.

The 3rd Respondent is the Divisional Secretary of Ella, within whose area the proposed parcel

of land to be acquired is situated. The 4th Respondent is the Janatha Estates Development Board,

while the 5th Respondent is the Sri Lanka State Plantations Corporation. The 6th Respondent is

the Secretary to the Ministry of Plantation Industries.

After the issuance of formal notice, this was argued before me on 05.03.2026 and 23.03.2026;

hence, this judgement.

Factual matrix

The Petitioner asserts that it had been established in terms of Section 2 of the Conversion of

Public Corporations or Government Business Undertakings into Public Companies Act, No.

23 of 1987; the relevant Gazette is marked as P1, by which the Petitioner was incorporated in

terms of Section 2 of the said Act of 1987. Thereafter, with the promulgation of the Companies

Act, No. 07 of 2007, the Petitioner had been re-registered as a Public Limited Company, as

borne out by P2.

The Petitioner asserts that initially the estate that is now entrusted to the Petitioner on a lease,

had been initially vested with the Land Reforms Commission (LRC); thereafter, the LRC had

handed over its possession to the Sri Lanka State Plantation Corporation, established by Act

No. 04 of 1958, and the State Plantation Corporation thereafter, by indenture of lease marked

as P3 annexed to the Petition, had leased out the entirety of the "Craig Estate" to the Petitioner

and handed over possession thereof as well. The Petitioner has further annexed the Power of

Attorney, marked as P4 annexed to the Petition, to manage the said Estate. Further, the

Petitioner has annexed the documents marked as P5 and P5(a) to establish the fact that it has

paid annual lease rental in respect of the said Estate.

In the meantime, on the direction of the Government, the Divisional Secretary of Ella has

written to the CEO of the Petitioner, his proposal to acquire twelve (12) acres of land from and

out of the said Estate for the purpose of relocating people who had been rendered homeless by

reason of the Uma Oya project. Accordingly, the Petitioner asserts that notice of Section 38(a)

of the Land Acquisition Act was issued by the Minister by the Gazette marked as P7; taking

possession of the said land in extent of 4.8562 Hectares (approximately 11.99 Acres) is also

marked as P8. The said acquisition and taking of possession had occurred in 2020.

Two years thereafter, a Section 7 notice had been issued by the 3rd Respondent, marked as P9

annexed to the Petition, to acquire a further Two-Acres from the Petitioner. Thereafter, as borne

out by P10 and P10(a), the possession thereof proposed to be taken by the said Gazette dated

21.10.2022. The said two-acre is depicted in the cadastral plan marked as P11. The purpose of

the said acquisition is referred to in the letter sent by the 3rd Respondent to the CEO of the

Petitioner marked as P12.

Thereafter, the Petitioner had protested and objected to the said proposed second acquisition

by P13 and P14.

The Petitioner's complaint to this Court is that despite the said protests and objections against

the second acquisition, the Respondents have taken further steps to acquire the said land of

acquisition by the Respondents; the Petitioner has marked P15 to establish that even the Land

Manual issued by the Ministry of Land, particularly in respect of land acquisition, clearly

indicates that there should not be a second acquisition of land from and out of an owner of a

land which is already subjected to an acquisition, as it will come in the form of an irrational

punishment against the owner of a land.

The Respondents' Objections

The 1st to 3rd Respondents by way of their Statement of Objections have denied the allegations

levelled against them in the Petition. However, by way of preliminary objections, the

7

Respondents state that the Petitioner is misconceived in law and has suppressed and

misrepresented material facts; therefore, the Petitioner is not entitled to any relief and moved

for the dismissal of this Application.

It is the position of the Respondents that they have acted in good faith and never acted mala

fide; therefore, they deny any allegations based thereon.

Having admitted that the Petitioner is a duly incorporated company which can sue and be sued,

it is the Respondents' position that despite the proposed acquisition initially issued in respect

of fourteen (14) acres, only less than 12 acres had been acquired, as reflected in the documents

marked as 1R1 to 1R5 annexed to the Statement of Objections.

In addition to that, the initial taking over of less than 12-acres for the purpose of relocating

homeless people who had been dislodged due to landslides in the Bandarawela area that

occurred in 2014, had not taken place so far; therefore, now the Respondents are taking steps

to relocate the people who have been rendered landless due to the said landslide. Thus, it is

quite justified to take over the said land.

The Respondents rely upon the documents marked as 1R7 to 1R15, to establish the steps taken

to further the said object; therefore, the Petitioner is well-aware of what had taken place and

what is going to take place. As such, it is the Respondents' position that the Petitioner cannot

have and maintain this Application as it is formulated.

After filing Counter Affidavits, the following arguments were advanced by the counsel at the

hearing.

Arguments

The thrust of the main argument of the learned Counsel for the Petitioner, Mr. Jayawardena, is

that the Respondents should not have taken steps to go for a second acquisition as it is against

8

the same landholder, who is the Petitioner, since the Petitioner is deprived of further land by

the second acquisition which is good, arable, and productive land. As such, the Petitioner is

entitled to the relief as prayed for in the Petition.

On the other hand, Mr. Aluwihare argues that there is no second acquisition as it is the same

acquisition that is in the process of completion since the initial Section 2 notice was issued for

the purpose of acquiring 14 acres, out of which, only less than 12 acres has currently been

acquired. Therefore, the Respondents are merely attempting to acquire the remaining two acres

of land, as depicted in the cadastral plan. Thus, it is well-within the original Section 2 notice

issued.

The oral submissions advanced by the counsel appearing on behalf of both the Petitioner and

the Respondents were further elaborated by their respective Written submissions filed.

It is the argument of Mr. Jayawardena that since the 1st and 3rd Respondents were already

responsible for acquiring 11.99 Acres from and out of the land held by the Petitioner, the second

proposed acquisition of another 2 acres is against the directions given in the Land Manual,

which is followed in every acquisition proceedings conducted by the Land Ministry and

divisional secretaries, and has been accepted as subordinate legislation in several judgements.

Therefore, taking steps to acquire another portion of land of the larger land that had already

been acquired is ultra vires because it violates the directions given in the Land Manual.

In addition to that, it is argued that when the 11.99 Acre portion of land was acquired for the

expansion of the Town, the Petitioner had never objected and willingly gave up its rights.

However, the Petitioner only objected when it came to the second round of acquisition, as

reflected by the documents marked as P13 and P14.

9

Mr. Jayawardena relies on the judgement of Ranawickrema v. Minister of Agriculture and

Lands and Others, 1 to establish that the Land Manual has been accepted as subordinate

legislation by the Courts.

In addition to that, he further relies on His Lordship Justice Arjuna Obeysekere's judgement in

the case of Kotagala Plantations PLC v. H.A. Kamal Pushpakumara, Divisional Secretary and

Others,2 where it is held that when there are other lands available to be acquired other than for

the land proposed to be acquired, from which certain acquisition had already taken place by

the Ministry, writs lie as it violates the Land Manual. It also further held that the decision and

steps taken by the Minister to take immediate possession of such proposed land can be reviewed

by the Court.

It is further Mr. Jayawardena's argument that the proposed second round of acquisition is

oppressive since there had already been an acquisition.

On the other hand, elaborating the oral arguments advanced by Mr. Aluwihare, he argues by

way of Written Submissions that the said judgement given by His Lordship Justice Obeysekere

can be distinguishable as the facts are not the same.

In addition to that, he argued that the immediate issue that has arisen in this case has to be

addressed since there is an urgent requirement for land to be allocated for the housing project

of the displaced people due to the landslide caused by the Uma Oya project. One Hundred and

Seventeen (117) families have been identified for the said project in which ten-perch plots of

land will be allocated from and out of the said land proposed to be acquired.

Issues to be considered

In view of the facts stated in the Petition, the facts stated in the Statement of Objections to

counter the allegations in the Petition, as well as by the arguments advanced orally and by way

of Written Submissions by the counsel on behalf of the parties, it is my view that the following

questions arise for my consideration;

(i) Whether the Petitioner has any locus standi to assert his rights and seek relief as sought

by way of this Application;

(ii) In those circumstances, whether the second acquisition proposed by the Respondents

violates the Land Manual;

(iii) Whether acquisition had already taken place;

(iv) In view of the documents marked as 1R6 and 1R7 filed by the Respondents, whether

the project proposed by the Respondents is urgent;

(v) If the above is answered in the affirmative, do the writs lie as sought by the Petitioner?

Locus standi of the Petitioner

The Petitioner is a body corporate incorporated under the name and style of "Maskeliya

Plantations PLC" in terms of the Companies Act, No. 07 of 2007. However, initially the

Petitioner had been incorporated in terms Section 2 of the Conversion of Public Corporations

or Government Business Undertakings into Public Companies Act, No. 23 of 1987. The

Petitioner had been floated as a company to manage the assets belonging to and held by public

corporations; thus, the Petitioner was empowered by the provisions of the said Act of 1987 to

enter into a lease agreement, and accordingly, the Petitioner had entered into the lease

agreement marked as P3. In addition to that, there is a Power of Attorney as well.

In terms of the said lease agreement, the State Plantations Corporation, which held and

possessed and became entitled to the "Craig Estate" in extent of approximately 281.99 Hectares

11

of land in Bandarawela area (previously held by the LRC and vested under the Land Reforms

Law No. 01 of 1972), leased it to the Petitioner for a period of 99 years. Thus, the Petitioner

became entitled to the leasehold interest for 99 years.

Therefore, for 99 years, the Petitioner is empowered and has every right to hold onto the

entirety of the rights to the said Estate. Accordingly, the Petitioner has locus standi, as in similar

circumstances, His Lordship Justice Arjuna Obeysekere has also held in the case of Kotagala

Plantations PLC v. H.A. Kamal Pushpakumara, Divisional Secretary and Others,3 that an

incorporated company similar to the Petitioner of this Application has similar rights.

decided with the approval of its Board of Directors, to renunciate its leasehold rights derived

from P3 in respect of Field No. 5A and 5B as identified as low-yielding tea plantation areas.

04. එබැවින් මෙම ඉඩමින් අක්කර 02 ක ඉඩම් ක ොටස අත්කර ගැනීමට අදාලව ඉඩම් අත්කර

ගැනීමේ පනතේ 2 වන වගන්තිය යටතේ දැන්වීම භාෂාත්‍රයෙන් පල කර අති අතර, දැන්වීම්

කට්ටලයේ පිටපතක් මේ සමඟ ඔබ වෙත ඉදිරිපත් කරන බව කාරුණිකව දන්වා සිටිමි.

Thereafter, due to a landslide caused by the Uma Oya project, certain families were rendered

homeless, causing the Government to require further lands to relocate the same. In those

circumstances, the Government decided to re-acquire the balance 2-acre land that had been left

out from the 14-acre land that was originally proposed to be acquired, and which the Petitioner

had already renunciate its rights over the 14-acre land. As such, there is no second acquisition;

it is only the continuation of the same initial acquisition process that has occurred in two stages.

Thus, there is no violation of the Land Manual.

As such, in those circumstances, the Petitioner has not been affected by such an acquisition.

Urgency of acquisition

As reflected in the document marked as 1R6, a landslide had been caused due to the ongoing

Uma Oya project, rendering 117 families homeless; out of which, 115 families have been

identified as 'seriously in danger' and therefore, they need to be relocated.

Thus, the Government decided to propose to allocate a ten-perch land to each affected family

to relocate to the 2-acre land proposed to be acquired from the Petitioner. Consequently, the

3rd Respondent caused the 1st Respondent, as the Divisional Secretary, to take immediate

possession of the 2-acre land proposed to be acquired by the Government.

14

Therefore, when the Section 38(a) notice was published in respect of the proposed 2-acre land,

the Petitioner has protested against it on the basis that already 12 acres of land had been

acquired, and thus, is unfair by the Petitioner to acquire further land since it violates the Land

Manual.

Nevertheless, the Government had initially proposed 14 acres of land to be acquired, in respect

of which the Petitioner was willing to relinquish its rights over. However, the Government had

initially only acquired 12 acres instead of the proposed 14 acres of land, for the purpose of

relocating the Town affected by the Uma Oya project. Thereafter, only did the Government

need further land to relocate those affected by the landslide.

Now the question arises whether the Government has violated the Land Manual, as argued by

the Counsel for the Petitioner.

Application of the Land Manual

It is provided in the Land Manual that persistent or recurrent acquisition of land from the one

and the same person should not be pursued or followed as it may amount to oppression. This

has been accepted by the Court as held in several judgements, as argued on behalf of the

Petitioner, that acquisition on several occasions from and out of the same owner of private land

is against the provisions of the Land Manual.

In fact, when there is no proper guideline in the Land Acquisition Act, the Land Manual has

been accepted and adopted as the only subordinate legislation by the Land Ministry and

admitted by the courts for the purpose of filling the vacuum created by the non-existence of

statutory provisions. Thus, though the Land Manual was not passed or adopted by the

Legislature as a piece of legislation, it remains as a subordinate legislation; therefore, the

provisions thereof are accepted by the Court.

15

As such, it is the duty of the Executive or administration to follow the same in the process of

acquisition of lands, as the Judiciary has also recognised the same.4

Accordingly, in this case, now the question arises whether the Respondents have violated the

same.

It must be noted that initially the Government had decided to acquire 14 acres of land and the

Petitioner willingly decided to relinquish its rights over the low yielding 14-acre land of tea

plantations, as reflected in 1R1. Nevertheless, the Government only took immediate possession

under Section 38(a) in respect of 12 acres for the relevant authorities to relocate the Town, as

reflected in P6 and P7. Now, the balance 2-acre land is to be acquired.

Thus, it is apparent that no prejudice is caused to the Petitioner as the facts of this case as

unfolded reveal. Therefore, it is my view that no prejudice is caused to the Petitioner.

Urgent necessity

In addition to that, it was argued on behalf of the Petitioner that though there was a decision to

take immediate possession of the land in terms of the Section 38(a) notice published by the

Minister, there is no urgent necessity for the Government to take such immediate possession.

However, on a perusal of the document marked as 1R6, there is a report prepared by the

National Building Research Organisation, by which it clearly indicates that there is an urgency

in relocating the said 117 homeless families in a suitable land. Thus, these families are seriously

affected, and their lives are under threat, it is the duty of the Government to relocate them, and

therefore, there is an urgent necessity of the Government to acquire land and take the necessary

actions. The names of the allottees are mentioned in the said report marked as 1R6.

This urgency has been further revealed by the very documents marked by the Respondents as

1R6 and 1R7 along with the Statement of Objections. However, in the course of oral arguments

as well as in the Written Submissions, the said two documents have not been properly explained

by the parties. Nevertheless, what is reflected in the said two documents 1R6 and 1R7 is the

landslide caused by the ongoing Uma Oya project

Therefore, it is my view that hindering by judicial process of such urgent need of the

Government should not be condoned. Accordingly, it is my view that no writ lies in this case;

although several questions raised by this Court have been answered in favour of the Petitioner,

the said urgent need of the land by Government is of foremost and paramount importance, I

refuse to interfere with the said decision of the Respondents to acquire the proposed 2-acre

land.

Conclusion

For the reasons adumbrated above, I dismiss this Application, without costs.
