Upon a careful analysis of section 81 of the Ordinance in its totality, read in

the offence upon oath or affirmation and by a majority verdict to punish the

In the light of the law enumerated above, it is beyond an iota of doubt that,

the Prison Tribunal so constituted under section 81 of the Ordinance has been

clothed with jurisdiction and/or power and/or authority so granted to it by

the Ordinance, to try “only” a person who is cloaked in the garb of a

prisoner within the definition assigned to it by section 104 of the

Ordinance as quoted above, for an offence listed under section 78 thereof,

and “only”, inflict punishment on a prisoner as such upon conviction.

This inevitably, follows that, the Prison Tribunal so constituted under section

81 of the Ordinance has not in any manner, been clothed with jurisdiction

and/or power and/or authority so granted to it by the Ordinance, to try a

person who is not cloaked in the garb of a prisoner within the definition

assigned to it by section 104 of the Ordinance as quoted above for an

offence listed under section 78 thereof, nor does it have any jurisdiction

and/or power and/or authority to convict and inflict punishment on a

prisoner as such nor does it have any jurisdiction and/or power and/or

authority to make an order remanding a person who is not coming within the

definition of “a Prisoner”, to the prison custody until final conclusion of the

inquiry and/or trial to be commenced or already commenced under the

provisions of the Ordinance in respect of an offence committed by a prisoner

during his detention in the prison custody inasmuch as the Law as it stands

today, once a prisoner ceases to be a prisoner by reason of him being admitted

to bail by a Court of competent jurisdiction and/or by reason of the Prisoner

being released from the prison upon completion of the sentence inflicted upon

him by a Court of competent jurisdiction, a Prison Tribunal will lose its

jurisdiction and/or power and/or authority over a prisoner as such for; a

prisoner as such cannot in law, be in any manner, considered a prisoner any

further for the purposes of the exercise of the jurisdiction and/or power

and/or authority vested in a Prison Tribunal to try a prisoner and inflict

punishment on him upon conviction as provided for by section 81 of the

As observed above, situation as such would in law, arise in any event that the

prisoner being admitted to bail by an order of Court or else a prisoner being

released from a prison custody upon completion of the sentence imposed

upon him by a Court etc; for; both those instances, the warrant of committal

issued by Court to the Superintendent of Prisons conferring upon him power

and/or extinct and therefore, it would be of no force nor valid in law any longer

resulting in the person who is cloaked in the garb of a prisoner within the

definition assigned to it by section 104 of the Ordinance as quoted above,

being no longer a prisoner within the meaning of the Ordinance capable in

law, to be detained any further in the prison. [Emphasis is mine]

The resultant position would be that, if a person as such is detained in the

prison any further without a warrant of committal so issued by a Court, such

detention will no doubt, become illegal and/or unlawful in the eyes of the law

as rightly, contended by the learned Counsel for the Petitioner.

It was held by the Supreme Court in the decision in Patrick Lowe & Sons

and Others Vs. Commercial Bank of Ceylon LTD 2001 [1] SLR 280 at page

284, “It is a fundamental principle of law that a person who functions in terms

of statutory power vested in him is subject to an implied limitation that he

cannot exceed such power or authority. The ultra vires doctrine, now

recognized universally, evolved in England on this premise (vide Ashbury)

Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd., vs. Hector Riche and the Attorney General

vs. The Great Eastern Railway.' It follows that what is not permitted by the

provisions of the enabling statute should be taken as forbidden and struck

down by Court as being in excess of authority.”

Hence, the central principle of administrative law is that, a public authority

may not act outside its powers (ultra vires).

In view of the above, the Prison Tribunal had clearly, acted outside its

jurisdiction and/or authority and/or powers vested in it by section 81 of the

Ordinance to be read with section 78 thereof, when it had proceeded to

remand the Detenu and continue with the inquiry and/or trial already

commenced by it against the Detenu in respect of the offence alleged to have

been committed by him under section 78 of the Ordinance notwithstanding

the fact that, the Detenu had already, been admitted to bail by the said

Magistrate’s Courts as manifest from Y1 and Y3 and therefore, the Detenu

had no longer been a prisoner within the meaning of section 104 of the

Ordinance and as such, the Detenu was not amenable to the jurisdiction of

the Prison Tribunal.

However, under those circumstances, all what the learned Prison Tribunal

ought to have done was to forthwith abate the proceedings with regard to the

inquiry and/or trial already, commenced against the Detenu and direct the

Superintendent of Prisons to forthwith take steps to prosecute the Detenu in

a Court of competent jurisdiction for the offence alleged to have been

committed by him under section 78 of the Ordinance in terms of the law,

“To apply for a Writ of Mandamus, it is not necessary to have a personal

It was held by this Court in A. R. Perera and Others v Central Freight

“As Lord Denning noted in R v Inland Revenue Commissioners ex p.

matter to the attention of court to vindicate the rule of law and get the

The rationale for the expanding canvas of locus standi in the context of

certiorari and prohibition was explained by H. W. R. Wade -

Administrative Law (8th Edition) pages 362 to 363 in the following

words;

“The prerogative remedies, being of a 'public' character as

emphasized earlier, have always had more liberal rules about

standing than the remedies of private law. Prerogative remedies

are granted at the suit of the Crown, as the titles of the cases

show; and the Crown always has standing to take action against

public authorities, including its own ministers, who act or

threaten to act unlawfully. As Devlin J said: Orders of certiorari

and prohibition are concerned principally with public order, it

being the duty of the High Court to see that inferior courts confine

themselves to their own limited sphere". In the same sense Brett

J had said in an earlier case that the question in granting

prohibition "is not whether the individual suitor has or has not

suffered damage, but is, whether the royal prerogative has been

encroached upon by reason of the prescribed order of

administration of justice having been disobeyed". Consequently

the court is prepared to act at the instance of a mere stranger,

though it retains discretion to refuse to do so if it considers that

no good would be done to the public.”

Wade further goes on to observe at page 683 that "...the House of Lords

is clearly now determined to prevent technicalities from impeding

judicial review so as to protect illegalities and derelictions committed by

public authorities".

Sri Lankan Courts too have been quick to recognize standing of any

citizen to seek relief against public authorities that stray outside their

legitimate bounds.”

It was inter-alia, held by this Court in the case in CA/WRIT/0440/2014-

Decided on 17.03.2021 at page 19 that, “Who can file a writ application?

The short answer is - any "person" (as defined in section 2(s) of the

Interpretation Ordinance) who has "sufficient interest" as opposed to the

outdated requirement of "personal interest" because of the element of "public

interest".”.

It was inter-alia, held by this Court in the decision in CA/WRIT/338/2009-

Decided on 28.05.2025 at page 12 that, “Learned Counsel for the 3rd

Respondent submits that the Petitioners have no locus standi to file this

application. I cannot agree. High technical objections regarding locus standi

have no place in modern administrative law. A writ application can be filed by

any person, as defined in section 2(s) of the Interpretation Ordinance, who

has "sufficient interest" in the matter, notwithstanding a lack of "personal

interest" because of the element of "public interest" involved in the subject

matter of controversy. The Petitioner in such an application can even be a

stranger. If the Petitioner comes to Court genuinely, as a public-spirited

citizen concerned to see that the law is obeyed in the interest of all, his

application shall be entertained. (Vide Lanka Viduli Podu Sevaka Sangamaya

v. Ceylon Electricity Board.”

Hence, the law as it stands today, with regard to the locus standi in an

application for a prerogative writ is that, a writ application can be filed by any

person, as defined in section 2(s) of the Interpretation Ordinance, who has

"sufficient interest" in the matter, notwithstanding a lack of "personal

interest" because of the element of "public interest" involved in the subject

matter of controversy. The Petitioner in such an application can even be a

stranger. If the Petitioner comes to Court genuinely, as a public-spirited

citizen concerned to see that the law is obeyed in the interest of all, his

application shall be entertained.

It appears from the averments in the Petition of the Petitioner that, he had

come to Court genuinely, as a public-spirited citizen concerned to see that the
