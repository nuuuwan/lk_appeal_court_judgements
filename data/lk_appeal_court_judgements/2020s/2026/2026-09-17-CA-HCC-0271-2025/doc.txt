Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentence dated 21.08.2025 delivered by the

Learned High Court Judge of Chilaw. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to

as “the Appellant”) was indicted before the High Court of Chilaw on a charge of

committing grave sexual abuse against one Liyanage Prathana Thikshani, an offence

punishable under Section 365B(2)(b) of the Penal Code, as amended by Acts No. 22 of

1995, No. 29 of 1998, and No. 16 of 2006.

2. At the trial before the High Court, the prosecution called five witnesses, while the

Appellant opted to make a dock statement. Upon the conclusion of the trial, the Learned

Trial Judge found the Appellant guilty of the charge and convicted him accordingly.

3. Consequently, the Appellant was sentenced to nine years’ rigorous imprisonment and

ordered to pay a fine of Rs. 50,000, with a default sentence of three months’

imprisonment. In addition, the Learned Trial Judge directed the Appellant to pay Rs.

300,000 as compensation to the prosecutrix, with a default sentence of two months’

imprisonment.

4. Being aggrieved by the said conviction and sentence, the Appellant has preferred the

instant appeal. The Appellant relies on the following grounds of appeal;

a. The Learned Trial Judge has drawn wrong inferences from the matters in

evidence as to the probability of the prosecution version.

b. The Learned Trial Judge has failed to consider the inconsistency in the evidence

between PW1 and JMO.

c. The Learned Trial Judge has not considered all the pieces of evidence

cumulatively, which would affect the test of spontaneity.

d. The sentence imposed by the Learned High Court Judge is excessive.

5. According to the testimony of the prosecutrix, she was ten years of age at the time of

the alleged incident. On the day in question, she was not attending school as it was

during the Aurudu vacation. While the prosecutrix was at home with her siblings, the

Appellant invited them to accompany him to the paddy field to pluck green leaves at

approximately 11.00 a.m. to 12.00 noon.

6. Accordingly, with the permission of the prosecutrix’s father, the prosecutrix,

accompanied by her two siblings and the Appellant’s daughter, went with the Appellant

to the paddy field for the purpose of plucking green leaves.

7. Thereafter, the Appellant took the siblings of the prosecutrix to a location some distance

away from the place where he had remained with the prosecutrix and his daughter in a

bushy area within the paddy field. The Appellant placed his daughter on his pair of

shoes and seated the prosecutrix on his lap. Thereafter, the Appellant touched the

prosecutrix’s genital area with his hands.

8. After some time, when the siblings of the prosecutrix returned to the scene, the

Appellant withdrew his hand and made the prosecutrix get up from his lap. Thereafter,

all of them returned home. At the time the siblings returned to the scene, the Appellant

instructed the prosecutrix not to disclose the incident to anyone.

9. Subsequently, according to the testimony of the prosecutrix, she disclosed the alleged

incident to her mother. Prior to such disclosure, the siblings of the prosecutrix had

PW2 insofar as it relates to the presence of the Appellant’s wife.

17. In these circumstances, the evidence does not establish that the Appellant’s wife was

present at the particular location where the alleged incident took place. Therefore, the

Appellant’s contention that he had no opportunity to commit the alleged offence, and

that the prosecution version was consequently improbable, cannot be sustained.

18. Evidently, during cross-examination, not a single contradiction was marked in the

evidence of the prosecutrix.

19. At this stage, it is essential to understand how the testimony of a child witness should

be looked into and appreciated.

20. In Dattu Sakhare Vs. State of Maharashtra (1997) 5 SCC 341, held that;

“There is no requirement or condition that the evidence of a child witness must be

corroborated before it can be considered, and rather the insistence of any

corroboration is only a rule of prudence that would depend upon the peculiar facts

and circumstances of each case.”

21. In Suryanarayana Vs. State of Karnataka (2001) 9 SCC 129, held that;

“The evidence of the child witness cannot be rejected per se, but the court, as a rule

of prudence, is required to consider such evidence with close scrutiny and only on

being convinced about the quality of the statements and its reliability, base

conviction by accepting the statement of the child witness.”

22. In Rameshwar Vs. State of Rajasthan (1952) AIR 54; the landmark judgment held that

child testimony, much scrutinized, does not require compulsory corroboration in the

absence of grounds for suspecting tutoring or fabrication.

23. In The State of Madhya Pradesh Vs. Balveer Singh (2005) INSC 261, the Supreme

Court of India revisited fundamental principles regarding the testimony of child

witnesses and the application of Section 106 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872

(“Evidence Act”) in cases involving suspicious deaths within the confines of a home.

This decision carries paramount significance as it clarifies the standard for assessing

the credibility of child witnesses, addresses the implications of delayed recording of

such testimony, and underscores the shifting burden of proof under certain factual

scenarios.

“The Trial Court must also record the demeanour of the child witness during the

course of its deposition and cross-examination and whether the evidence of such

child witness is his voluntary expression and not borne out of the influence of

others.”

“There is no requirement or condition that the evidence of a child witness must be

corroborated before it can be considered. A child witness who exhibits the

demeanour of any other competent witness and whose evidence inspires confidence

can be relied upon without any need for corroboration and can form the sole basis

for conviction.”

24. Having regard to the consistency of the prosecutrix’s testimony, the absence of any

material contradiction elicited in cross-examination, there is no sufficient basis to

regard the prosecution version as inherently improbable. On the contrary, the evidence,

when considered as a whole, supports the probability of the prosecution version and

does not give rise to a reasonable doubt as to its credibility.

25. Moreover, it should be noted that the learned Trial Judge observed the demeanour and

deportment of the prosecutrix and formed a view that she was a credible and probable

witness even before the cross-examination had been concluded. Significantly, at this

stage, in assessing the credibility of the prosecutrix, this Court does not place reliance

on that observation, as it was made prematurely, prior to a cumulative assessment of

the entirety of the evidence.

26. In this stage, however, it is also desirable to emphasize that the Learned Trial Judge had

the advantage of observing the demeanour of the witnesses. Accordingly, the Appellate

Courts are slow to interfere with the findings of a trial judge unless it is demonstrated

that the trial judge has manifestly misdirected herself on a material issue, thereby

causing prejudice to the Appellant.

27. In Alwis vs. Piyasena Fernando (1993) 1 Sri. L R 119, it was held that:

"It is well established that findings of primary facts by a trial Judge who hears and

sees witnesses are not to be lightly disturbed on appeal."

28. Similar sentiments were articulated in Dharmasiri vs. Republic of Sri Lanka [2010] 2

Sri. L.R.241, where it was held that:

“Credibility of a witness is mainly a matter for the trial Judge; the Court of Appeal

will not lightly disturb the findings of a trial Judge with regard to the credibility of

a witness unless such findings of the trial Judge are manifestly wrong.”

29. In the Court of Appeal decision of Kumar de. Silva and 2 others vs. Attorney General

[2010] 2 Sri LR 169, Sarath de. Abrew J had held that;

“Credibility is a question of fact, not of law. The acceptance or rejection of evidence

of witnesses is therefore a question of fact for the Trial Judge.”

30. In Ariyadasa vs. Attorney General [2012] 1 Sri LR 84, the Court of Appeal held that;

“Court of Appeal will not lightly disturb a finding of a Judge with regard to the

acceptance or rejection of a testimony of a witness, unless it is manifestly wrong,

when the Trial Judge has taken such a decision after observing the demeanour and

deportment of a witness…….”

31. Accordingly, while this Court recognizes that the learned Trial Judge had the advantage

of observing the demeanour and deportment of the prosecutrix and that such an

advantage is ordinarily entitled to due consideration by the appellate court, any

assessment of her credibility and probability must nevertheless be made upon a proper

and cumulative consideration of the entirety of the evidence, including the evidence

elicited in cross-examination. In the present case, the learned Trial Judge observed that

the prosecutrix was a credible and probable witness before the conclusion of her cross-

examination and before the evidence had been assessed cumulatively. Accordingly, this

Court does not rely on that observation in determining the credibility and probability of

the prosecutrix.

32. Moreover, PW2 and PW3 testified that the Appellant placed his own daughter on a pair

of shoes while she was exposed to the heat of the sun, whereas the prosecutrix was

seated on the lap of the Appellant. Such conduct on the part of the Appellant is highly

unusual and inconsistent with the conduct ordinarily expected of a reasonable parent.

This evidence lends further support to the account given by the prosecutrix regarding

the circumstances in which the alleged incident took place.

40. It is further significant to note that the history furnished by the prosecutrix to the JMO

was also consistent with the JMO’s findings regarding the possibility of penetration by

a finger. The account given by the prosecutrix to the JMO, therefore, lends further

support to the medical findings.

41. Moreover, the evidence of PW2 and PW3 further corroborates the testimony of the

prosecutrix, particularly as PW3 had witnessed part of the incident. Accordingly, the

testimonies of PW1, PW2 and PW3 are materially consistent with the medical evidence.

The credibility of PW1’s testimony remained unshaken notwithstanding the extensive

cross-examination to which she was subjected. The defence failed to elicit any material

contradiction or omission in her evidence sufficient to give rise to a reasonable doubt

regarding the truthfulness or reliability of her testimony. Accordingly, I find no

sufficient basis upon which to place reliance on the alleged inconsistency between the

evidence of PW1 and the medical evidence.

42. Further, it was contended by the Appellant that the Learned Trial Judge failed to

consider all the pieces of evidence cumulatively, which would affect the test of

spontaneity.

43. PW2, the mother of the prosecutrix, testified that, on the day of the alleged incident, at

approximately 11.00 a.m., she became aware that her children, together with the

prosecutrix, had gone to the paddy field to pluck green leaves with the Appellant, his

wife and his daughter. They returned home at about 12.30 p.m. Thereafter, the siblings

of the prosecutrix informed PW2 that the Appellant had seated the prosecutrix on his

lap. Upon hearing this, PW2 questioned the prosecutrix regarding the alleged incident,

whereupon the prosecutrix disclosed the incident to her mother.

44. However, at the time, PW2 did not immediately disclose the alleged incident to her

husband, as she feared that he might cause further trouble due to his addiction to liquor.

Subsequently, on the same day, PW2 informed her husband of the alleged incident,

following which he confronted the Appellant and a quarrel ensued. Thereafter, they

proceeded to the police station and lodged a complaint.

45. The delay in lodging the complaint is reasonably explained by the evidence of PW2,

who testified that she was apprehensive that the father of the prosecutrix, due to

addiction to liquor, might cause further trouble. Accordingly, the delay in making the

complaint cannot, in the circumstances, be regarded as undermining the credibility of

the prosecution case.

46. In these circumstances, I find no basis to accept the Appellant’s contention that the

Learned High Court Judge failed to properly analyze and evaluate the evidence. On the

contrary, the judgment demonstrates a careful consideration of both the prosecution and

defence evidence. Accordingly, I find that the grounds of appeal advanced by the

Appellant are devoid of merit and must fail.

47. For the foregoing reasons, I find no basis to interfere with the findings of the Learned

Trial Judge. Accordingly, I affirm the conviction and sentence imposed by the Learned

High Court Judge of Chilaw on 21.08.2025. In the circumstances, the appeal is

dismissed.
