Introduction

The Respondent-Petitioner-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as the “Appellant”) appeals from the judgment dated 22.03.2018, of the High Court of the North Central Province holden in Anuradhapura, dismissing his application to revise the order of the Magistrate’s Court of Anuradhapura dated 23.02.2018 in Case No. 91215, by which order Appellants ejectment had been directed under the State Lands (Recovery of Possession) Act, No. 7 of 1979, as amended (hereinafter referred to as the “Act”) by the learned Magistrate.

Factual Matrix

The Applicant–Respondent-Respondent (hereinafter sometimes referred to as the

“Respondent”) the Divisional Secretary had instituted the ejectment proceedings in

respect of the land described in the application filed in the Magistrate’s Court under

section 5 of the Act (at page 26 of the brief). The Appellant had maintained that he and

his predecessor had occupied the land since 1980 and that his predecessor had been

issued a document which the Appellant described as a “license”. The Appellant further

state that officers responsible for State lands had directed or recommended the issue of

a license to him and that he had obtained water and electricity connections and made

improvement to the land.

In accordance with the procedure under the Act, the learned Magistrate had afforded the

Appellant an opportunity to show cause against ejectment. The Respondent had been

permitted to file counter-objections, and both parties had been given an opportunity to

tender written submissions. The Appellant had based his claim on two documents

4

submitted by him, marked ව1 and ව2. The learned Magistrate rejected document ව1,

being an expired permit issued to a lady named Alice Nona, and document ව2, being an

agreement, as not amounting to a valid permit or other written authority of the State as

envisaged by section 9 of the Act. Having considered the material placed before Court,

the learned Magistrate had made order ejecting the Appellant from the relevant land.

Being aggrieved by the order of the learned Magistrate, the Appellant invoked the revisionary jurisdiction of the High Court. The learned High Court Judge dismissed the revision application at the stage of support, without issuing notice to the Respondents by the order dated 22.03.2018. The Appellant being dissatisfied with the order of the High Court has filed the instant appeal to this Court.

Grounds of Appeal

The instant appeal challenges the High Court's treatment of the documents, the

identification of the land, the conclusion that the Appellant's occupation was

unauthorized, and its failure to give effect to what the Appellant describes as a legitimate

expectation arising from the conduct and correspondence of land officials. The written

submissions add objections to the Divisional Secretary's authority to institute the

proceedings and to the form of the application and supporting affidavit. The central

question is whether these matters disclose an error in the High Court's refusal to revise

the ejectment order.

Statutory framework

The Act provides a summary procedure for the recovery of possession of State land.

Upon forming the opinion contemplated by section 3, a competent authority may issue a

quit notice. If it is not complied with, an application for ejectment may be made under

section 5. At the inquiry, section 9(1) permits the person summoned to establish that he

occupies the land upon a valid permit or other written authority of the State, granted in

accordance with written law, which remains in force and has not been revoked or

otherwise rendered invalid. Section 9(2) prohibits the Magistrate from calling for

5

evidence from the competent authority in support of the section 5 application. The

burden of establishing the statutory defence rests on the occupier1.

The limited nature of that inquiry must be kept distinct from a claim to title, a request for

an allotment, or a complaint about the wisdom of an administrative decision concerning

land. In Urban Development Authority v. Wejayaluxmi2, this Court held that a list of

persons selected for a proposed allotment, which had not been finalized, was not a valid

permit within section 9(1). Section 10(2) excludes an appeal against an order of

ejectment made under section 10(1). Revisionary jurisdiction remains available in

exceptional circumstances, including to correct a manifest error of law or a miscarriage of

justice; it does not provide an occasion to determine the merits of a proposed allotment.

Analysis

The Appellant's reliance on the license said to have been issued to his predecessor in

1980 does not, without more, establish his own entitlement under section 09. It was

necessary to show that the instrument covered the land described in the ejectment

application, authorized his occupation, and remained in force at the relevant time. The

petition identifies no transfer, succession, renewal or other instrument by which that

license became a subsisting authority for the Appellant himself. The directions and

recommendations upon which he relies contemplate the future issue of a license; they

are not themselves shown to be a license or a present grant of occupation under written

law.

Long occupation, expenditure on improvements and the supply of utilities may explain

the Appellant's circumstances. They do not, either separately or together, supply the

written State authority required by section 09. Nor can an expectation that a license will

be issued be treated as if the license had already been issued. Any complaint that the

responsible officials departed from an assurance or failed to decide his request lawfully is

a different question. It cannot enlarge the defence available in the Magistrate's inquiry.

The contention concerning the identity of the land likewise does not establish an error

warranting revision. Section 09 does not permit a general contest of the competent

authority's assertions in the section 5 application, including the assertion that the

scheduled land is State land. Identification may matter where an occupier produces a

subsisting permit and must show that it applies to the very land sought to be recovered.

No such permit covering the Appellant's occupation has been established on the case

advanced in the petition and written submissions. An assertion that the land should have

been more fully identified cannot in these circumstances defeat the ejectment order.

Although the petition of appeal does not identify the Divisional Secretary’s authority as a

separate ground, the Appellant expressly challenges it in the written submissions. The

Appellant contends that, if the premises belonged to the Provincial Engineering

Department, the Divisional Secretary lacked authority to institute and maintain the

application. That contention cannot be sustained on the grounds advanced. In Banda v.

President, M.P.C.S. Ltd., Medirigiriya and Another3, this Court recognized that a Divisional

Secretary may exercise the powers of a competent authority under the State Lands

(Recovery of Possession) Act. The asserted connection between the premises and the

Provincial Engineering Department does not, by itself, establish that the Divisional

Secretary was legally precluded from exercising those powers. “Banda” concerned an

application made by the president of a co-operative society under an invalid

authorization from the Divisional Secretary. No comparable issue of authorization arises

here, as the application was made by the Divisional Secretary himself.

Counsel for the Appellant in his written Submissions further submits that the application

failed to identify the Act by its number and year, and that the supporting affidavit was

unstamped as such the affidavit was defective. Section 5 prescribes an application

supported by affidavit and accompanied by the quit notice. A proved failure to comply

with a material statutory requirement could warrant intervention. The submissions,

however, do not identify how the asserted omission of an enactment number misled the

Appellant, who appeared, filed objections and addressed the application under the Act.

Nor does the assertion about a stamp or the form of the affidavit, as presented in this

appeal, establish a defect that deprived the Magistrate of jurisdiction or caused a

miscarriage of justice. The Court cannot set aside concurrent orders upon an

unsubstantiated allegation of a formal defect.

The petition of appeal does not expressly identify any exceptional circumstance warranting the exercise of revisionary jurisdiction. It alleges errors in the Magistrate’s

assessment of the evidence, the identification of the land and the application of the Act. The written submissions develop those objections but do not explain why they called for revisionary interference. The assertion of grave and irreparable loss appears in support of the application for an interim stay.

The complaint that the High Court dismissed the revision application without issuing notice does not, of itself, establish an error. The High Court was entitled to decline notice if the material placed before it disclosed no exceptional circumstance warranting interference. The matters urged here do not establish that the learned Magistrate applied an impermissible test or that the High Court overlooked a subsisting written authority within section 9 of the Act. I therefore find no basis to disturb the judgment of the High Court.

Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the appeal is dismissed with a cost fixed at Rs. 25,000/-

payable to the state by the Appellant, and the judgment of the High Court dated

22.03.2018 is affirmed. The order of the Magistrate's Court dated 23.02.2018 accordingly

stands.
