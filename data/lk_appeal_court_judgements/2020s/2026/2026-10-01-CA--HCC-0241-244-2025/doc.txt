Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentence dated 22.07.2025 of the

learned High Court Judge of Polonnaruwa. Initially, 6 accused were indicted in

the High Court of Polonnaruwa on three counts, namely:

a. On or about 14.05.2006, within the jurisdiction of the High Court of

Polonnaruwa, the Accused, being members of an unlawful assembly with

the common intention of causing injury to one Mandalawalli Kuda Radala

Patabadi Hangiligedara Nimal Jayathilaka, thereby committing an

offence punishable under Section 140 of the Penal Code;

b. During the same time, place, and in the course of the same transaction as

mentioned in the first count, being members of the unlawful assembly

caused the death of Mandalawalli Kuda Radala Patabadi Hangiligedara

Nimal Jayathilaka and that this offence was committed in furtherance of

the common object of the said unlawful assembly, or alternatively, that

the members of the said unlawful assembly knew that such offence was

likely to be committed, and therefore, by being members of the said

unlawful assembly at the time of committing the offence, thereby

committed an offence punishable under Section 296 read with Section

146 of the Penal Code; and

a. During the same time, place, and in the course of the same transaction,

the Accused, by causing the death of Mandalawalli Kuda Radala Patabadi

Hangiligedara Nimal Jayathilaka, committed an offence of murder

punishable under Section 296 read with Section 32 of the Penal Code.

2. Before the commencement of the trial, the 5th and 6th accused were reported dead.

Thus, the trial proceeded only against the 1st to 4th accused. At the conclusion of

the trial, the learned High Court Judge found them guilty of the 1st and 2nd counts,

and accordingly convicted them. Consequently, all the accused were sentenced

to death.

3. Being aggrieved by the said convictions and sentences, the 1st to 4th accused-

Appellants (hereinafter referred to as the Appellants) have preferred the present

appeal.

4. The grounds of appeal advanced by the Appellants are as follows:

Consideration of the grounds of appeal:

Has the testimony of PW1 failed the test of spontaneity?

5. Evidently, PW1, who claimed to be the sole eyewitness to the incident, made his

statement to the police four months after the alleged incident. The Appellants

contended that the testimony of PW1 did not satisfy the test of spontaneity. There

was, admittedly, a considerable delay on the part of PW1 in making his statement

to the police. However, delay alone does not necessarily corrode the probative

value of a witness's testimony, provided that a plausible explanation is

forthcoming to justify such delay.

CA/HCC/0241-244/2025

5 | P a g e

6. It is also significant to note that, where there is a delay in making a statement to

the law enforcement authorities, there is always a possibility of false material

being introduced or evidence being fabricated in the intervening period.

Therefore, a Trial Judge should exercise greater caution before placing reliance

on such evidence. The trial Judge must be satisfied that the delay has been

satisfactorily explained by a plausible explanation acceptable to the Court.

7. Evidently, there was an illicit affair between the deceased and Buddhika, the wife

of the 2nd Appellant. Buddhika and the deceased were accustomed to meeting in

secret locations. PW1 was in the habit of stealthily watching the couple during

these encounters.

8. According to PW1, on the day of the incident, he secretly followed the deceased

to the meeting spot and climbed a nearby coconut tree to observe the couple.

While there, he heard a whistle and identified it as coming from Roshan, the 3rd

Appellant. Five individuals then arrived at the scene: Kadira, Jalor, Rasika,

Sisira, and Wijerathna. As the deceased approached, Kadira, the 6th Accused,

shone a torchlight directly into his face. The deceased asked who they were,

whereupon the witness heard a noise and saw the deceased collapse.

9. Shortly thereafter, a red three-wheeler arrived from the direction of the 1st

Appellant’s house, and the deceased was loaded into it. All the individuals,

except the 2nd Appellant, got into the three-wheeler and drove away. The 2nd

Appellant then took the deceased’s bicycle and proceeded toward a paddy field

belonging to the deceased. Subsequently, the witness climbed down from the

coconut tree and went to his workplace at a rice mill.

10. It is noteworthy that PW1 merely stated he did not report the incident to the

police due to fear instilled by the Appellants on a different occasion.

Significantly, PW1 was a laborer at a local rice mill who, by his own admission,

routinely traveled across the island in lorries distributing rice. According to his

testimony, he reported to work the day after the alleged incident and departed in

a rice delivery lorry. Had he been distressed by witnessing the alleged incident,

it is highly improbable that he would have reported to work and traveled to

distant locations as usual. Furthermore, had he truly witnessed the incident, he

could have informed at least one of the deceased's relatives rather than waiting

four months to speak out.

11. It is pertinent to note that, when PW1 was questioned as to why he had waited

four months before making a complaint, he simply stated that he had been in a

state of shock and, consequently, had not disclosed the incident to anyone. In my

view, this explanation is difficult to accept for the following reasons.

12. Evidently, PW1 reported for work on the following day and continued to perform

his duties, including travelling in the lorry which distributed rice to outstation

areas. There is no evidence to suggest that PW1 had neglected or was unable to

perform his duties at his workplace as a result of the alleged state of shock

following the incident. It is also significant that the deceased was PW1’s next-

door neighbour and that PW1 attended the funeral of the deceased. These

circumstances, when considered cumulatively, make it difficult to accept that

PW1 remained in such a state of shock for a period of four months as to prevent

him from making a complaint or disclosing the incident to anyone.

13. When no plausible reason is forthcoming to justify the inordinate delay in

making the statement, it is unsafe to secure a conviction based on the evidence

of a belated witness.

14. In Dharmasiri vs Republic of Sri Lanka [2012] (1) Sri L.R. 268, it was held

inter alia:

“Two critical tests before considering belated evidence as reliable evidence

are: firstly, reasons for delay and secondly, whether those reasons are

justifiable.…”

15. In Udagama vs Attorney General [2000] (2) Sri L.R. it was held:

“Evidence is infirm, unsafe and unreliable to act upon considering the

following:

(i) the belated statement made to the Police with delay not explained with

acceptable reasons.

(ii) material questions and contradictions go to the very root of the

prosecution case.

(iii) failure to evaluate and consider the dock statement of accused.”

16. Accordingly, I am unable to accept that the four-month delay in making the

complaint was occasioned by the state of shock alleged by PW1.

17. It is also pertinent to note that, as admitted by the Appellants, he had previously

been charged with sexually abusing a cow belonging to the 6th Appellant. PW1

was subsequently convicted by the court and fined in respect of that offence. This

circumstance assumes significance when assessing the credibility of PW1,

particularly as most of the Appellants are siblings. It is therefore reasonably

possible that PW1 harboured animosity towards the Appellants and that such

animosity may have provided him with a motive to falsely implicate them in the

present case.

19. In R. v. Devlin [1997] EWCA Crim 739, decided on 14 March 1997, the English

Court of Appeal also considered the issue of voice identification. The Court

approached the evidence of recognition or identification in the same manner as

it would approach identification evidence in a case governed by the principles in

R v Turnbull. The Court concluded that hearing the two words, “money, money”,

was no more than the equivalent of a fleeting glance and that the identification

evidence was therefore poor. In the circumstances, the Court held that the trial

Judge ought to have withdrawn the case from the jury.

20. The Turnbull guidelines provided in the case of Regina Vs. Turnbull (1977) QB

224, laid down important guidelines for judges as to the manner in which they

should assess evidence in relation to a situation where the identity of an accused

person comes into question. The said guidelines read as follows;

It requires a trial judge to be mindful that;

i. Caution is required to avoid the risk of injustice.

ii. A witness who is honest may be wrong even if they are convinced, they

are right.

iii. A witness who is convincing may still be wrong.

iv. More than one witness may be wrong.

v. A witness who recognizes the defendant even when the witness knows the

defendant well may be wrong.

Some of the circumstances a Judge should direct the jury to examine in order to

find out whether a correct identification has been made include;

i. The length of time the accused was observed by the witnesses.

ii. The distance the witness was from the accused.

iii. The state of the light.

iv. The length of time elapsed between the original observation and the

subsequent identification.

21. I have carefully considered the submissions made on this issue and the evidence

relating to the alleged voice recognition. In my view, the identification of the

Appellants by their voices in the present case essentially depended upon the

whistle which PW1 stated had been blown by the 2nd Appellant and the

conversations which PW1 claimed to have overheard among the other

Appellants. Although PW1 stated that he identified the Appellants by their

voices, no specific words or expressions allegedly spoken by any of the

Appellants were elicited in his evidence. In these circumstances, the alleged

voice identification evidence is of such a limited and uncertain nature that it

cannot safely be acted upon and cannot provide a proper basis upon which to

sustain a conviction.

22. In these circumstances, the alleged voice identification evidence is of such a

limited and uncertain nature that it cannot safely be acted upon. I am therefore

of the view that such evidence cannot, either by itself or in the absence of other

reliable identification evidence, provide a safe basis upon which to sustain the

convictions of the Appellants.

23. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the possibility of genuine mistake

in voice identification is a matter which must be carefully considered,

particularly where the opportunity for observation or recognition is limited.

Having regard to the totality of the evidence, I am of the considered view that

the quality of the identification evidence, both visual and by voice, was poor. In

the circumstances, the learned Trial Judge ought not to have acted upon such

evidence to find the Appellants guilty and should have acquitted them of the

charges.

stated that he had followed the deceased up to the paddy field where the deceased

was working.

26. Furthermore, PW1 did not state in his statement to the police that he had been on

a coconut tree at the time of the incident. This omission was brought to the

attention of the Court during the trial. The omission assumes significance,

particularly as PW1’s evidence before the Court was that he had observed the

Appellants from the coconut tree and had identified them by their voices.

27. According to PW1, the red-coloured three-wheeler in which the deceased was

taken proceeded towards the hospital. Nevertheless, despite the fact that the

members of the deceased’s family were neighbours of PW1, he did not inform

any of them about the incident. This conduct of PW1 is also a circumstance

which requires consideration when assessing the reliability of his evidence.

28. According to the evidence of PW1, no one had witnessed his presence at the

scene of the crime. In these circumstances, it is difficult, if not impossible, to

infer that PW1 was under any fear or threat which prevented him from disclosing

the alleged incident to anyone.

29. PW2, Sarath Kumarasiri, is the brother of the deceased. According to his

evidence, the 1st Appellant had maintained a very close friendship with the

deceased. PW2 stated that he had come to know that the deceased was having an

affair with the wife of the 2nd Appellant, although he had not made any further

inquiries into the matter. According to PW2, the Appellants subsequently

distanced themselves from the deceased on account of this issue. Nevertheless,

the evidence of PW2 does not disclose any incriminating circumstance against

any of the Appellants.

30. PW6 is the wife of PW2. According to her evidence, when she attended a

meeting convened in connection with the granting of loans, the 1st Appellant

approached her and threatened that the deceased would be killed very soon. PW6

stated that she subsequently informed the deceased of the threat, but that the

deceased did not take the matter seriously.

31. It is, however, significant that PW6 did not disclose this alleged threat to the

police until 6 August 2024. This raises an important question: if PW6 had in fact

been threatened by the 1st Appellant that the deceased would be killed, and if she

genuinely believed that the threat was significant, why did she not inform the

police of the threat, particularly soon after the death of the deceased? No

plausible explanation has been forthcoming for this considerable delay.

32. This unexplained delay assumes significance when assessing the reliability and

probative value of PW6’s testimony. In my view, the failure to disclose the

alleged threat at the earliest opportunity materially affects the weight that can

safely be attached to her evidence.

33. PC 35964 Mahinda Abeykoon was the police officer who first proceeded to the

place where the body of the deceased was found. The body was lying on a cart

road, on which there was well-grown grass approximately two inches in height.

It is significant that, according to his observations, the grass had not been

trampled, pressed down, or otherwise disturbed.

CA/HCC/0241-244/2025

34. This observation assumes particular significance when considered in the light of

the evidence of PW1, who stated that, after assaulting the deceased, the

Appellants transported him in a three-wheeler. If the deceased had in fact been

transported in a three-wheeler to that location, or had been carried there by the

Appellants, one would reasonably expect some disturbance or compression of

the grass at or around the place where the body was found. However, according

to PW7, no such disturbance was observed. This circumstance is therefore

inconsistent with the account given by PW1 and further casts doubt upon the

reliability of his testimony.

35. Even the learned Trial Judge has observed in the judgment that the grass at the

place where the body was found showed no signs of having been trampled or

pressed. This observation further casts doubt on the evidence of PW1, who stated

that the deceased was placed in a three-wheeler and taken away by the

Appellants.

36. At the trial, the contradictions marked by the defence and the omissions

highlighted therein were admitted by the parties. The most significant omission,

from the standpoint of the prosecution case, is PW1’s failure to mention the place

from which he allegedly witnessed the assault on the deceased by the Appellants.

In his testimony, PW1 stated that he climbed a coconut tree and, from there,

witnessed the assault. However, he had failed to mention this material fact in his

statement to the police.

37. The learned Trial Judge misdirected himself when considering the above

omission. The learned Judge observed that the PW1 had initially been hiding in

a banana plantation and, upon seeing the deceased approaching the Appellants,

had climbed a coconut tree. However, this version was never supported by the

evidence of PW1. There was no evidence to establish that the Appellant was

hiding in a banana plantation and he climbed the coconut tree upon seeing the

deceased approaching PW1. The learned trial Judge therefore appears to have

attributed to the evidence a factual position which was not established by the

testimony of PW1.

38. The Judicial Medical Officer who conducted the autopsy also testified, marking

the Post Mortem Report as Y1. In the report said report, he opined that the cause

of death was trauma to the head and fall front, which is compatible with an

assault.

39. In the aforesaid circumstances, I conclude that it is not safe to affirm the

conviction against the Appellants.

40. For the reasons set out above, we allow the appeal and we proceed to quash the

convictions and sentences imposed on the Appellants by the Learned High Court

Judge of Polonnaruwa.

41. Accordingly, we acquit the Appellants of all the charges leveled against them.
