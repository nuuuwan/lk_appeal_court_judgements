Section 34 of the Inland Revenue Act deals with allowances in respect of

qualifying payments. Paragraph (s) of subsection (2), in the form

applicable to the relevant period, provides for an investment of not less

than fifty million rupees in the acquisition of fixed assets made on or

after 1 April 2011 but before 1 April 2015 in the expansion of an

undertaking which would have been qualified for exemption under

section 16C or section 17A had such undertaking commenced to carry

on business on or after 1 April 2011. The provision further contains a

proviso concerning certain high technology plant, machinery or

equipment acquired for specified purposes, including energy efficiency,

technology upgrading, introduction of new technology or power

Judgment CA Tax No. 124/2024 Page 10|16

generation using renewable energy resources, and extending the relevant

period in the circumstances specified therein.

It is therefore clear that section 34(2)(s)has not provided that every

investment in fixed assets exceeding fifty million rupees automatically

constitutes a qualifying payment. The investment must be an investment

in the acquisition of fixed assets, must satisfy the monetary and

temporal requirements of the provision, and, importantly for the present

appeal, must be an investment “in the expansion of any undertaking”

which satisfies the statutory provision incorporated by reference to

sections 16C or 17A.

There are four requirements of section 34(2)(s) of IRA for a claim of

“qualifying payment”. The investment must;

I. Be not less than fifty million rupees,

II. Be made in the course of an acquisition of fixed assets,

III. Be on or after 1st April 2011 but before 1st April 2015,

IV. Be done in the expansion of undertakings.

The investment made in this case is 450 million which is more than fifty

million rupees, this satisfies the first requirement of the fifty million

limit. Then, the investment of 450 million was made for acquisition of a

fixed machinery, this satisfies the second requirement of acquiring fixed

asset. The investment was made in the year of assessment 2013/2014,

this satisfies the third requirement of time period. Therefore, it can be

considered that the payment in this case must satisfy the 4 limbs under

Section 34(2)(s). Since the first three limbs have been satisfied, the fourth

limb of expansion will be discussed further in this case.

Section 34 (2)(s) proviso reads as:

“….

Provided however, where such investment is made in any high

The word expansion is not defined in IRA. The Oxford dictionary meaning

is:

“EXPAND – an act of increasing or making something increase in

size, amount or importance”

“The company is expanding growth.”

The word “expansion” is not defined in section 34(2)(s). The ordinary

meaning of the word is enlargement, extension or increase in the size, or

extent of something already existing. The Court must therefore determine

whether, on the facts established before the tax authorities, the

investment made by the Appellant amounted to an enlargement or

extension of its undertaking. In the present case the word which

assumes importance in expansion. The statutory requirement of

expansion is found in the provision itself.

In this case, the Respondent do not challenge or deny that new Kiln is

more efficient and contain new technology and thereby amount to an

upgrading. It will mean that the company that demolished an old factory

and built a new factory will not qualify, as it is a replacement of one

factory with another. The whole objective of the section is to encourage

undertakings to expand their operations, to modernize equipment. This

requirement has been satisfied.

Having granted total exemption to companies which commenced

business operations after April 1, 2011, in the areas of activities referred

to in section 16C(2) and 17A(2) and satisfies the exemption of section

16C or 17A as the case may be, they sought to encourage businesses

which had commenced carrying on such activities prior April 1, 2011 to

expand their existing business undertakings. The legislature, to promote

and develop the activities specified in section 16C(2) and 17A(2), decided

to grant qualifying payments to persons engaging in the referred to in

section 16C or section 17A if they satisfied the requirements of such

sections but had commenced business before 1 April, 2011.

A company which had invested not less than fifty million rupees in fixed

assets made by any person on or after April 1, 2011 but before April 1,

2015 in the expansion of any undertaking who would have been qualified

for exemption under section 16C or section 17A had such undertaking

commenced to carry on business on or after April 1, 2011 was granted a

qualifying payment.

Therefore, the increase in the scope of the activity or the scale of the

Questions of Law No. 1, 2, and 6

The above Questions of Law are dealt with reasoning and grounds of

appeal which are related to each other. Hence, the above questions are

dealt together.

In the determination of the CGIR, they have considered the amount of

investment, expansion of undertaking and the increase of the turnover.

From the said determination an appeal was preferred to the TAC. The

grounds of appeal are amount of investment, expansion of undertaking

and the increase of turnover. But in the TAC determination it has been

said that the grounds of appeal are; Amount of investment, Expansion of

undertaking, Increase in turnover and Eligibility of claim of qualifying

payment.

Accordingly, TAC has considered additional grounds of appeal which is in

respect of claim of qualifying payment.

TAC sat on appeal. The appeal is from the determination of the CGIR.

TAC cannot consider new materials which were not before the CGIR for

its determination. These are rules of appeal. Appeal should be considered

within the four corners of the appeal. New materials cannot be

introduced at the stage of appeal.

In Seylan Bank Ltd v. Samdo Macky Sportswear (Pvt.) Ltd. and

Others (2008) 1 SLR 96, the Supreme Court held that a taxing statute

that imposes a pecuniary burden must be subject to strict construction.

The Supreme Court held:

“There is no room for intention, construction or equity about duties or

taxations. The explicit language of the Statute must be the yard stick

presumptions must be strictly excluded. If the imposition of duty

upon a particular instrument is not expressly contemplated by the

The Supreme Court in the above case out frames the interpreter of taxing

statute. Statute must be interpreted with simple ready of the language of
