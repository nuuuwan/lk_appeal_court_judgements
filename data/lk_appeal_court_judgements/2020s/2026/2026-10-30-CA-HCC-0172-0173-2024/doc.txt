7. Accordingly, the learned High Court Judge has concluded that the

bodily injury inflicted upon the deceased was, in the ordinary course of

nature, sufficient to cause the death of the deceased. Consequently, the

appellants have been convicted of murder under the third limb of

Section 294 of the Penal Code of Sri Lanka.

8. The third limb of the Section 294 of the Penal Code provides as follows;

“ 294. Except in the cases hereinafter excepted, culpable homicide is

murder-

Thirdly - If it is done with the intention of causing bodily injury

to any person, and the bodily injury intended to be in>licted is

suf>icient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death.”

9. The third limb to Section 294 of the Penal Code of Sri Lanka states that

culpable homicide amounts to murder, if the act is done with the

intention of causing bodily injury to any person, and the bodily injury

intended to be inflicted is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to

cause death.

10. The offender must deliberately intend to inflict a specific bodily harm or

injury to a person. Unlike the first limb, the prosecution does not need

to prove that the accused had a specific conscious intent to cause

8

death. The injury that the offender intended to inflict must be

objectively fatal, meaning it is serious enough to cause death in the

ordinary natural course of events.

11. In the case of Vithana and Another Vs. The Republic of Sri Lanka [2007]

1 SLR 169, Sisira de Abrew J. held as follows,

“The ingredients that must be proved by the prosecution in order to prove

a charge of murder under the 3rd limb of section 294 are that:

(i) The accused in>licted a bodily injury on the victim.

(ii) The victim died as a result of the above bodily injury.

(iii) The accused had the intention to cause the bodily injury.

(iv) The above injury was suf>icient to cause the death of the victim in

the ordinary course of nature.”

12. “Sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature” is a critical

element to distinguish murder from culpable homicide. A Court is a

legal expert, not a medical one. Therefore, it relies heavily on the

testimony and reports of the forensic pathologist or medical officer who

conducted the postmortem examination.

13. The doctor’s opinion is relevant in this instance, and the prosecution

should explicitly ask the medical expert during examination, “Was this

injury sufficient to cause death in the ordinary cause of nature?”. A

court can thereafter evaluate medical details regarding the depth, width

and the location of the injury. Injuries to vital organs are almost always

ruled as naturally sufficient to cause death. The court would also look

at the capacity of the weapon to cause lethal damage.

30. The statement “contents of a document has to be established by

producing such document” reflects the best evidence rule in law,

meaning a document’s written contents must be proved by submitting

the actual document itself rather than relying on someone’s spoken

memory or descriptions. The law prefers primary evidence, which is the

original document produced to a Court for legal inspection.

31. In the case of Keerthi Bandara vs. The Attorney General [2000] 2 SLR

245, Niinian Jayasuriya J., has held as follows;

“The best evidence rule which is a fundamental tenet in the law of

evidence would totally exclude the oral evidence of a police of>icer in

regard to the contents of a matter which is required by law and which in

fact has been reduced to writing, to be led after refreshing his mind from

the document without the document being marked.”

32. The prosecution, through the testimony of PW21, who was the acting

headquarters inspector of the particular police station at the time of the

alleged incidents, has attempted to orally establish specific regulations.

These regulations concern an officer’s attire when on duty, and the

procedure to be followed when a suspect is brought to a police station

and detained. The prosecution has attempted to further assert that the

appellants violated these regulations, thereby making them guilty of

unlawful restraint of the deceased.

33. The regulations referenced must be documented adequately and it is

essential for the prosecution to produce the actual document

containing those regulations. Unfortunately, the prosecution has failed

to provide this necessary evidence. Furthermore, they have not offered

any explanation for this omission.

34. In cases where Court favours primary evidence, such as the original

document, containing the relevant regulations it becomes imperative for

the documents to be presented for legal scrutiny. Consequently, any

testimony provided by PW21, as previously mentioned, should be

disregarded and considered invalid. Without the primary evidence the

testimony lacks sufficient credibility to support the prosecution’s case.

“A satisfactory way to arrive at a verdict of guilt or innocence is to

circumstances of the particular case, he believes the accused guilty of

the charge or not guilty.”

39. With regard to the judgement under challenge, it appears that the

learned High Court Judge has assessed the witnesses’ testimony

piecemeal, rather than in its entirety and considered isolated

statements out of context. Further, the learned High Court Judge

appears to have concluded that the offences charged against the

appellants have been proved beyond a reasonable doubt, before

evaluating whether the testimony established the essential ingredients

of those offences. In doing so, the learned High Court Judge appears to

have prejudged the issue and thereby misdirected himself.

40. In those circumstances, I am inclined to interfere with the conviction,

disputed judgement, together with the sentencing order and proceed to

set aside the same.
