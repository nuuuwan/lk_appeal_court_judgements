Judgment

1. This appeal arises from the judgment and sentence dated 24.01.2025 delivered by the

learned High Court Judge of Kandy. The Accused-Appellant (hereinafter referred to as

"the Appellant") was indicted before the High Court of Kandy on a charge of

committing grave sexual abuse against an eight-year-old boy, an offence punishable

under Section 365B(2)(b) of the Penal Code, as amended by Act No. 22 of 1995, Act

No. 29 of 1998, and Act No. 16 of 2006.

2. At the trial before the High Court, the prosecution called four witnesses, while the

Appellant testified on his own behalf and called three witnesses for the defence. Upon

the conclusion of the trial, the learned Trial Judge found the Appellant guilty of the

charge and convicted him accordingly. The Appellant was consequently sentenced to

ten years' rigorous imprisonment and ordered to pay a fine of Rs. 10,000.00, with a

default sentence of twelve months' imprisonment. In addition, the learned Trial Judge

ordered the Appellant to pay Rs. 100,000.00 as compensation to the victim, with a

default sentence of twelve months' imprisonment.

3. Being aggrieved by the said conviction and sentence, the Appellant has preferred the

instant appeal. The Appellant relies on the following principal grounds of appeal:

a. The learned Trial Judge failed to properly evaluate the evidence adduced by the

defence;

b. The learned Trial Judge failed to comply with the requirements of Section 283 of

the Code of Criminal Procedure Act; and

c. The prosecution evidence is inherently improbable, and the learned Trial Judge

failed to consider the weaknesses in the prosecution case.

4. According to the testimony of the victim, the alleged incident occurred at a secluded

location along a gravel road. At the relevant time, the victim travelled to and from

school by bus. In order to board the bus, he had to walk a considerable distance from

his home along the gravel road.

5. On the day of the alleged incident, after returning from school by bus, the victim

alighted at his usual bus stop and proceeded to walk home along the same gravel road.

The Appellant, who was a resident of a neighboring village, had travelled on the same

bus and was seated with the victim. According to the victim, the appellant followed him

after they got off the bus and, at a secluded spot along the road, suddenly grabbed him,

pulled down his trousers, and bit his penis. The alleged incident took place in an isolated

area where no other persons were present.

6. According to the evidence of the victim, after the alleged incident, he returned home

and informed his mother of what had occurred. By that time, a cousin of the victim,

Vishwajith, had already informed the victim's mother about the incident. Thereafter, a

complaint was lodged with the police, and the victim was referred for a medical

examination.

7. I shall first address the Appellant's contention that the prosecution case is inherently

improbable and that the learned Trial Judge failed to give due consideration to the

deficiencies and weaknesses in the prosecution evidence.

8. At the time of the alleged incident, the victim was eight years old, and his testimony

was recorded before the Court eight years thereafter. In such circumstances, some

degree of inconsistency, omission, or discrepancy in his evidence is inevitable.

Nevertheless, unless those infirmities are material, affect the core of the prosecution

case, or indicate that the testimony is a product of fabrication, they cannot, by

themselves, render the evidence of PW1 unreliable or unworthy of credit. Therefore,

the evidence led by the prosecution in the present case has to be carefully analyzed and

evaluated bearing in mind the above considerations.

9. The Appellant further contended that the version of PW3 is inherently improbable.

According to PW3, when he witnessed the incident, he shouted “ඒයි,” upon which the

Appellant released the victim and left the scene. However, according to the testimony

of the victim, he did not see PW3 at the scene of the incident. Nevertheless, the victim

admitted that it was PW3 who first informed his mother about the incident.

10. Therefore, there appears to be some inconsistency between the testimonies of PW1 and

PW3 as to who first informed PW1’s mother of the incident. It was contended on behalf

of the Appellant that it was highly improbable that PW3 would have left the scene

without speaking to the victim if he had in fact witnessed the incident. It is to be noted

that the victim was eight years old at the time of the incident, while PW3 was only

twelve years old.

11. Hence, I find it difficult to discern any inherent improbability in PW3's conduct in

leaving the scene without speaking to the victim, particularly having regard to his tender

age. Needless to say, a child of PW3's age could not reasonably be expected to confront

an elderly man, especially when the latter was allegedly engaged in the commission of

an offence of the nature complained of, which is not one ordinarily encountered.

Accordingly, PW3's reaction and subsequent conduct after witnessing the alleged

incident do not disclose any inherent improbability that would render the prosecution

evidence untrustworthy or lacking in credibility, as contended by the defence.

12. According to PW3, upon witnessing the incident, he shouted and immediately went to

the victim's house, which was situated next to his own. By the time he arrived there,

the victim was already relating the incident to his mother.

13. According to the testimony of the victim, he did not see PW3 at the time the alleged

offence was being committed by the Appellant. In my view, the mere fact that the victim

did not notice PW3 does not diminish the credibility of PW3's evidence. Given the

unexpected and traumatic nature of the incident, the victim may either have forgotten

that PW3 had witnessed the incident or may simply have failed to notice who was

present in the vicinity at the time.

14. It is also pertinent to note that the victim was only eight years old at the time of the

alleged incident, while PW3 was fourteen years old. Both witnesses testified before the

trial court approximately ten years after the incident. Accordingly, in view of their

tender ages at the time of the incident and the significant passage of time before they

gave evidence, it is only natural that certain minor inconsistencies would appear in their

testimonies. Such inconsistencies do not, in my view, undermine their overall

credibility.

15. The evidence of a child witness must be assessed as a whole, taking into account the

entirety of the evidence adduced at the trial. At the conclusion of the case, the court

must determine whether the child's evidence, having been tested through cross-

examination, is trustworthy. This assessment depends upon whether the child was able

to narrate the events coherently, communicate effectively, understand the questions

posed, and provide rational and intelligible answers.

16. If the child's evidence has not undergone any material or substantial change and the

essence of the allegations remains intact, the court may safely accept such evidence as

satisfactory in all material respects after evaluating it alongside the evidence of the

accused. The expression "satisfactory in all material respects" does not require that

every aspect of the testimony be free from discrepancies or inconsistencies. Rather, the

court must consider the evidence in its entirety, bearing in mind that minor

discrepancies are not uncommon, particularly in the testimony of a child witness, and

determine whether the evidence, viewed as a whole, is sufficiently reliable to establish

the guilt of the accused.

17. In R v. Norbury [1978] Crim LR 435, the Privy Council admitted the testimony of a

six-year-old rape victim, stating that if the child understands the questions and provides

rational answers, corroboration is not necessary.

18. In Ranjeet Kumar Ram v. State of Bihar [2015] SCC Online SC 500, the court held

that:

“Evidence of the child witness and its credibility would depend upon the

circumstances of each case. Only precaution which the court has to bear in mind

while assessing the evidence of a child witness is that the witness must be a reliable

one”.

19. In Satish Kumar Gupta v. State of Haryana AIR 2017 SUPREME COURT 1072, the

Supreme Court of India upheld a conviction based solely on the testimony of a 12-year-

old who witnessed his father’s murder. The Court found his testimony to be reliable,

admissible, and free from external influence.

20. Therefore, in my view, the aforementioned inconsistency between the testimonies of

the victim and PW3 is not of such a material nature as to go to the root of the

prosecution's case or justify the rejection of their evidence.

21. It was also contended on behalf of the Appellant that there existed a dispute between

the victim's family and the Appellant concerning the use of a nickname for the victim's

maternal grandfather and that, as a result, the present complaint was lodged to falsely

implicate the Appellant. In support of this contention, Defence Witness No. 2 testified

that, when the victim's maternal grandfather was referred to as "Ali Banda", the victim's

mother became angry and scolded them using abusive language.

22. I find it difficult to accept this contention as providing a plausible motive for falsely

implicating the Appellant for the following reasons. The Appellant himself admitted

that the victim's maternal grandfather was commonly known as "Ali Banda". According

to his evidence, on one occasion, some outsiders had asked the Appellant's children to

show them Ali Banda's house while they were playing. This admission demonstrates

that the victim's maternal grandfather was referred to as "Ali Banda" not only by the

Appellant or his family but also by other persons in the village.

23. In these circumstances, the mere use of that name could not reasonably have been the

cause of such hostility between the victim's mother and the Appellant as would motivate

her to fabricate a serious allegation against him. Accordingly, I am not persuaded that

the above incident constitutes a cogent or credible reason for the victim or his family to

make a false complaint against the Appellant.

24. The Judicial Medical Officer who examined the victim also gave evidence regarding

the injuries he observed on the child. According to his testimony, the child was

produced before him for examination on the day following the alleged incident. Upon

referring to the Medico-Legal Report marked P5, he stated that the injuries found on

the child were consistent with the history narrated by the child.

25. It was also contended on behalf of the Appellant that the learned Trial Judge had failed

to properly analyze and evaluate the defence evidence and that, consequently, the

impugned judgment does not comply with the requirements of Section 283 of the Code

of Criminal Procedure Act.

26. Upon a perusal of the impugned judgment, it is apparent that the learned Trial Judge's

analysis and evaluation of the evidence do not represent the most appropriate

compliance with the requirements of Section 283 of the Code of Criminal Procedure

Act. Nevertheless, in my view, that shortcoming does not warrant the setting aside of

the judgment, as no other conclusion could reasonably have been reached in light of the

cogent and compelling evidence adduced by the prosecution.

27. Her analysis of the defence evidence could also have been more comprehensive than it

was. However, the impugned judgment cannot be set aside solely on account of that

deficiency, as a more detailed analysis would not reasonably have led to a conclusion

different from that reached by the learned trial Judge, having regard to the strength and

cumulative effect of the prosecution evidence.

28. Furthermore, it is well established that an appellate court will be slow to interfere with

the findings of fact made by a trial Judge unless such findings are shown to be perverse,

unsupported by the evidence, or otherwise contrary to established legal principles,

resulting in prejudice to the Appellant. This approach is founded on the recognition that

the trial Judge enjoys the unique advantage of seeing and hearing the witnesses and

observing their demeanor while they testify.

29. The well-established principles governing the limited circumstances in which an

appellate court may interfere with the findings of a trial court are set out in the following

authorities.

30. In Alwis vs Piyasena Fernando (1993) 1 Sri. L R 119, it was held that:

"It is well established that findings of primary facts by a trial Judge who hears and

sees witnesses are not to be lightly disturbed on appeal."

31. Similar views were articulated in Dharmasiri Vs. Republic of Sri Lanka [2010] 2 Sri.

L.R.241, where it was held that:

“Credibility of a witness is mainly a matter for the trial Judge; the Court of Appeal

will not lightly disturb the findings of a trial Judge with regard to the credibility of

a witness unless such findings of the trial Judge are manifestly wrong.”

32. In the Court of Appeal decision of Kumar de. Silva and 2 others V. Attorney General

[2010] 2 Sri LR 169, Sarath de. Abrew J had held that;

“Credibility is a question of fact, not of law. The acceptance or rejection of evidence

of witnesses is therefore a question of fact for the Trial Judge”

33. In Ariyadasa V. Attorney General [2012] 1 Sri LR 84, the Court of Appeal held that;

“Court of Appeal will not lightly disturb a finding of a Judge with regard to the

acceptance or rejection of a testimony of a witness, unless it is manifestly wrong,

when the Trial Judge has taken such a decision after observing the demeanour and

deportment of a witness…….”

34. In S v Francis 1991 (1) SACR 198 (A) at 198J - 199A, the Supreme Court of Appeal

of South Africa held:

“The powers of a court of appeal to interfere with the findings of a trial court are

limited. In the absence of any misdirection, the trial

courts conclusion, including its acceptance of a witness's evidence, is presumed to

be correct. In order to succeed on appeal, the Appellant must therefore convince the

Court of appeal on adequate grounds that the trial court was wrong in accepting

the witness's evidence - a reasonable doubt will not suffice to justify interference

with its findings. Bearing in mind the advantage which a trial Court has of seeing,

hearing and appraising a witness, it is only in exceptional circumstances that the

Court of appeal will be entitled to interfere with a trial court's evaluation of oral

testimony.”

35. For the foregoing reasons, I find no basis to interfere with the findings of the learned

Trial Judge. Accordingly, I affirm the conviction of the Appellant by the learned High

Court Judge of Kandy on 24.01.2025.

36. However, having regard to the fact that the Appellant did not use any violence against

the victim, did not threaten the victim to prevent disclosure of the incident, and has no

previous convictions or pending criminal cases, I am of the view that a modest reduction

of the sentence would not occasion any prejudice.

37. Accordingly, I vary the sentence of ten years' rigorous imprisonment imposed by the

learned Trial Judge and substitute therefor a sentence of seven years' rigorous

imprisonment. The substituted sentence shall take effect from the date of conviction,

namely, 24.01.2025. The orders imposing the fine and directing the payment of

compensation shall remain unchanged.

38. Subject to the above variation of the sentence, the appeal stands dismissed.
